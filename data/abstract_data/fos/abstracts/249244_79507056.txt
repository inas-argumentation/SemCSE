Book Review: Medical Miracles: Doctors, Saints, and Healing in the Modern World
Modern medical training is evidence based and does not account for miracles; and our modern culture tends to compartmentalize the two. In the saint-making process, a healthy respect exists between the physician and his knowledge and the theologian with his knowledge on spiritual matters. In Medical Miracles: Doctors, Saints, and Healing in the Modern World by physician-historian Jacalyn Duffin, this relationship is explored, and Vatican sources on fourteen hundred miracles spanning four centuries from six continents are examined. These miracles are considered evidence by the Roman Catholic Church effected by saints as a sign of their proximity to God. Dr. Duffin, a hematologist by training, developed an interest in these miracles after she was invited to read a set of bone-marrow samples without being told the reasons why. She would later learn that the slides were from a patient with severe acute leukemia. Assuming the patient to be dead, she was surprised that the patient was—and still is alive. Although the patient accepted aggressive chemotherapy, she attributed her cure to the intercession of Marie-Marguerite d’Youville who had been dead for two hundred years. Years later Dr. Duffin, a skeptic, and her Jewish husband would attend the canonization ceremony at St. Peter’s Basilica. She was presented the Positio, or bound testimony on the miracle, as a gift for her work on the case. Her historian instincts were intrigued, and in this book she describes the results of four trips to the Vatican from 2001–2007. On those trips, she set out to answer the following questions: 1. What were the miracles worked through the intercession of new saints in modern times? 2. How many were healings of physical illness?
