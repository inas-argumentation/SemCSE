The Dynamics of Economic Growth, Technological Progress, and Institutional Change
Recent research in the field of economic growth indicates an increasing appreciation of the role of technological progress in determining the expansionary potential of the economy.' The complementary role of institutional change has not, however, been given equal attention, despite the rich heritage of the institutional approach to economics. This anomaly may, in part, be explained by noting that growth theory has developed largely in the context of capital accumulation2 within a given, or assumed, institutional framework. The level of technology, or its rate of change, has always been an apparent and generally an explicit determinant of the productivity of a particular stock of capital. Bringing technology into the production function as a variable has long been an obvious "next step" toward a more sophisticated and realistic theory of economic growth. Because the institutional framework has been so widely regarded by the theorist as a remotely relevant, as well as an unmeasurable, determinant of the economy's expansionary potential, no theoretical progress on a par with that made in incorporating technological change has been achieved. Now that effective cyclical stabilization of the economy seems within our grasp, inquiry into structural stabilization should come to the fore. For this reason, considerations of technological progress and institutional change become of paramount importance and, as the present paper will attempt to show, theoretical development in terms of the institutional framework is as necessary as that in terms of technological progress. The principal economic role of the institutional framework is to minimize socio-economic dislocations that arise from economic growth and technological change.
