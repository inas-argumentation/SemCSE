Africanisation of music, dance and other spiritual, aesthetic and festive expressions followed. However, successful marketing of Brazilian samba, Cuban son or Dominican merengue, and of different carnivals by global cultural industries, not only resulted in cultural cannibalisation of tradition, but has also hidden persistent patterns of social exclusion of black people. This fuelled the social and political movements that during the late 1980s and early 1990s struggled against assimilation and in favour of the forms of multiculturalism that shaped the constitutional reforms implemented during those years in Brazil and Colombia, and later in Ecuador and Venezuela. Yet Reid Andrews argues that actors addressing racial and ethnic issues have not been entirely successful in building strong constituencies. Not only they have faced competition from the patron-client relationships that have long characterised politics in many countries, but neoliberal economic policies have also undermined Afro-Latin American ancestral territories and hindered affirmative action measures. In reference to the future, Reid Andrews notes that ‘accompanying the rise of neoliberal policy [_] has been an expansion and deepening of electoral democracy ’ (p. 197), which opens opportunities for new multiracial coalitions between palenques, quilombos and other autochthonous and autonomous forms which originated in resistance against slavery, and labour and other social movements. The author’s hope is that these new alliances will contribute to consolidating democracy in such a way that attainment of full inclusion will make racial politics inapplicable and obsolete.
