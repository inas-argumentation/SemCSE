To this he adds a perceptive analysis of a famous sequence in Don Quixote climaxed by the puppet theater of Maese Pedro (alias pícaro Gines de Pasamonte) revealing how Cervantes creates therein a microcosm and history of the early stages of Spanish theater, a poetics of jongleuresque performance, and an image of the survival of those traditions alongside the vigorous growth of classical Spanish theater in the seventeenth century shaped by Lope de Vega, Tirso de Molina, and Calderón de la Barca. Demonstrating how in the early days of cinema in the twentieth century, silent film incorporated the melodramatic techniques of vaudeville and burlesque theaters that supplied the first screen actors, Burningham shows how the comedia similarly corralled — in theaters constructed in courtyards where juglares had earlier performed — that multiform jongleuresque performance tradition in its multiple bill of fare. He maintains that the Lopean comedia is a development of the juglares’ art and often of the very historical-legendary ballads they performed, while the religious tropes usually identified as its origins developed instead into autos sacramentales. Despite pressure from the cultural elite to cultivate a theater akin to classical models, this performance tradition survived to supply the performative energy that would captivate the popular audiences of early modern Spain, France, and England. Accepting the importance of this radical theatricality helps explain the apparently sudden emergence and originality of the comedia despite the meager medieval Iberian textual tradition of liturgical drama. Although the history of Spanish drama is central to Burningham’s inquiry, students of other European theatrical traditions will find much food for thought as well in this wide-ranging long view of performance art.
