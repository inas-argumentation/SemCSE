If there is one phase of the American journey that has received less attention, it is probably that between March , the terminal point of the three-volume travelogue published in English as Personal Narrative of a Journey to the Equinoctial Regions of the New Continent, and March , when Humboldt and Bonpland (and their new companion Carlos Montúfar of Quito) landed in Mexico, which would be the subject of the Political Essay of New Spain and the object of much questioning from US political figures eager to learn of developments on their southern border. During this period the travellers moved through present-day Colombia, Ecuador and Peru, an experience which is quite modestly represented in Views, where the plates are not arranged in the chronological order in which they were taken, sketched by Humboldt, drawn (mostly by others) and engraved. Mexico and the Aztec empire dominate the volume, which has never before been published in its entirety in English – but in their introduction the editors line up the innocent reader for the greater shock of form that is to follow. They refer to an ‘Alternative Library’, since Humboldt is incessant in citing other authorities, and they describe ‘Humboldt’s multilayered image-text’ as being as much a ‘collection’ composed of a variety of pieces, often at a remove from their cultural contexts – several holdings of museums are included (and appear here in photographic colour). The closest analogy is evidently the internet. Just when one thinks one is on top of things the plates come to an end with the Dragon Tree of La Orotava, Tenerife, quietly suggested to be older than all the preceding monuments, and then a reproduction of a (heavily referenced) letter from Mr Visconti commenting on all that has gone beforehand. Radicalism in form is more than matched by the intellectual substance of a work that can be set not just in the eighteenth-century ‘dispute of the New World’, taking in De Pauw, Buffon et al., but also in the major nineteenth-century controversies over civilisation and barbarism. Here Kutzinski and Ette properly highlight Humboldt’s bold refusal of the fiercely confident Eurocentric beliefs of the era as he challenged the notion that the ‘old’ and ‘new’ worlds were of distinct geological ages. The social correspondence follows in what he called ‘a happy revolution’, the ‘beautiful and fruitful idea to consider all peoples of the earth as belonging to the same family’ (p. ). That remains a highly contested concept in ; to have made the case  years earlier through sensibility as well as science, intersecting text and image is a breathtaking achievement. It may well be that individual readers cannot afford to have this foundational text of the modern era in their private collections, but all university libraries should certainly obtain it as a fundamental part of their holdings.
