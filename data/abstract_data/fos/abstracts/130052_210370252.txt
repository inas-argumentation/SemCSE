Schwartz highlights the distance between academic and institutional definitions of Indian populations and the empirical evidence. That evidence shows that these populations were heterogeneous and often characterised by traits defined as modern, national and mestizo. In Chapter 10, José Luis Escalona Victoria examines the way in which archaeologists and anthropologists, above all Evon Vogt and the Harvard Chiapas project, produced indigenous alterity and stabilised the idea of the Maya as an ethnic group with uninterrupted links between contemporary and ancient civilisations. Chapter 11 by Vivette García Deister considers the Mexico genome project which, despite being unable to identify an ‘indigenous type’ distinguishable from a mestizo one, continued to associate pathologies such as susceptibility to obesity and diabetes with indigenous genes. Together these chapters show how the definition of indigenous has been ‘fixed’ through social and historical processes that construct indigenous alterity out of many socially determined possibilities, rather than such alterity being pre-existent, essential or self-evident from the start. Beyond Alterity lays down a challenge to students and scholars of indigenous peoples and rural society in Latin America and, like many innovative books, its implications can be unsettling. By problematising the meaning and usefulness of the concept of ‘indigenous’, and exposing the Occidentalism upon which such ‘othering’ is premised, the book questions the use of ahistorical identities such as ‘indigenous’ as a mode of analysis and challenges much of the historiography and social analysis produced about indigenous peoples to date. It also questions, implicitly, the use of identity as a means of political mobilisation to challenge the inequities of modernisation. In the afterword, Eiss argues that doubting the validity of a particular identity need not lead to a rejection of the legitimacy of indigenous people’s claims and political goals because the category of indigenous, like all political identities, is, in the editors’ words, ‘a permanent field of negotiation and dispute whose meanings are always volatile and elusive’ (p. 6). This may be so, but such nuances are not necessarily self-evident inside or outside the academy.
