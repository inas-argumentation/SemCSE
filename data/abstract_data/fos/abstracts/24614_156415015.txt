Causality and Consciousness in Economics: Concepts of Change in Orthodox and Heterodox Economics
Institutionalists have always considered causality a central concept of economics and have added weight to the theoretical orientation by contrasting the neo-classical concept of linear causality with the sophisticated and realistic concept of circular and cumulative causation pioneered by Thorstein Veblen [18981. Neoclassical economists have not only failed to give further ontic and theoretical profile to their causality concept but have also tended to reject it altogether as a basis upon which an analysis of economic processes could be built. Bertrand Russel's insistence that the causality concept was "humbug" has left a deep impression on those economists who were prepared to derive from an axiomatic treatment the ultimate scientific status of economic theory. Thus, institutional economics, with its concept of circular and cumulative causation, is opposed to orthodox economics, where causality is considered redundant or methodologically impeding, and, where if it enters the scene at all, is given a deterministic interpretation which robs it of its original substance. The differences in the causality concepts of orthodox and heterodox economics are profound-and where there is smoke there must also be fire.
