Thalidomide and derivatives: immunological investigations of tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) inhibition suggest drugs capable of selective gene regulation
Recent interest in thalidomide stems not from its properties as a sedative or anti-emetic [l], but from the observations of its efficacy in the treatment of erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL) (the reactive state of leprosy) [2,3], oral and genital ulcers [4] and of various autoimmune diseases. Collectively, clinical evidence suggests that thalidomide has potential as an immunomodulatory or immunosuppressive drug [5,6]. Many recent experiments have confirmed that thalidomide is indeed a powerful immunomodulant and has been beneficially used in diseases such as vasculitis [7,8], a model of HIV [9], rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [lo], sarcoidosis [ 111, chronic graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) [12], and also in preventing rejection in transplantation [ 131 and inhibiting angiogenesis [141. It has been demonstrated to exhibit steroid sparing properties [7,8,11,15], where high-dose steroids have been used for immunosuppression, which suggests a related additive or synergistic effect between these drugs. So, what is thalidomide doing, how does it work (mechanism of action), and can the beneficial effects be enhanced and separated from the harmful ones, such as teratogenicity and induction of peripheral neuropathies [16,17]? These questions are not trivial nor as specifically restricted to thalidomide as it might at first appear. It should become apparent below that this compound (and its derivatives) can provide an insight into the molecular interactions involved with specific gene regulation and the biochemical mechanisms of drug-targeted immunomodulation. Clinical evidence suggests that thalidomide is effective, in some circumstances, in reducing inflammation. The mode of action would appear to be different from that of corticosteroids or cyclosporin A and other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which can radically down-regulate a wide range of cytokines and other immunologically active molecules, to induce broad range immunosuppression. Acute-phase inflammation is known to be mediated by proinflammatory cytokines which include TNF-a, IL1 and IL-6. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments have shown that a major effect of thalidomide is the selective down-regulation of TNF-a production by activated monocytes [18], but similar inhibitory effects have also been noted on IL-6 and, to a lesser extent, on interferon-gamma (IFN-y) production [ 191. T cell proliferative responses seem relatively unaffected by thalidomide, and this is reflected by the absence of an effect on IL-2 production or gene regulation following T cell activation by mitogens or antigens [20].
