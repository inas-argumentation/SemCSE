Differences between Guidance Counselors Who Accept and Reject Psychological Consultation
The findings and recommendations of the Joint Commission's Report on Mental Illness and Health as summarized in Aciion for Mental Healrh (1961) suggest that an increasing number of trained persons who are not themselves members of the traditional mental health team will have to be relied upon in the treaunent of the mentally disturbed. In line with the Joint Commission's report, many clinical psychologists believe that they can better meet sociery's needs by functioning as mental health consulrants to "caretaker" personnel rather than by working on a one-to-one basis with the emotionally disturbed. The question has never been asked, however, as to whether or not these consulting services will be utilized, and, if they are, what variables will be involved in the decision to use them. The present study presents some data bearing on these questions. Fifty junior and senior high school guidance counselors were offered the opportunity to meet once a week in group sessions with a clinical psychologist at the university to discuss any problems in relation to their functioning as guidance counselors. The meetings were voluntary, attendance was not taken and no reports were given to the school system. All of the counselors received an envelope containing an Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (EPPS), a 25-item senrence completion test, and a personal data questionnaire. Thirteen guidance counselors attended at least one of the eight meetings. The test materials were completed by nine of the 1 3 "attenders" and by nine of the approximately 37 "non-attenders." O n the EPPS, it was found that "attenders" had more "need exhibition," less "need order," and less "need abasement" ( P < .05) . O n the sentence completion items there was some evidence that the scoring categories which Hiler (1959) used to differentiate terminators from remainers in psychotherapy could be used to differentiate between "atrenders" and "nonattenders." It appears that "attenders," just as Hiler's remainers, arc more willing to reveal personal feelings and confidential material. The only item on the questionnaire which significantly differentiated bemeen the two groups was an item dealing with the number of college-level psychology courses taken. The "attenders" had taken significantly more such courses. The obtained results, although not impressive, are consistent with the expectations that there are personality differences between guidance counselors who will and who will not voluntarily utilize the consulting services of a clinical psychologist.
