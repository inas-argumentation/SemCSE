Lung Aspergillosis in renal cell carcinoma patient treated with sunitinib.
Respiratory disease due to opportunistic fungal pathogens is well described during immunosuppressive therapy, bone marrow and organ transplantation and HIV infection.1 A variety of fungi including Aspergillus can cause respiratory disease in immunocompromised cancer patients, related both to their compromised immune status and to their immunosuppressive therapy.2,3 In the immunocompetent host, Aspergillus is cleared from the lung by alveolar macrophages and activated immune defence mechanisms.4 Fungi have conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) with which they bind to pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which include Toll-like receptors (TLR2, TLR4), C-type lectin receptors (CLR) as well as intracellular sensors of the NOD-like receptor (NLR-family) and RIG-I-like helicases.5–9 Such fungal PAMP recognition triggers phagocytosis, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), arachidonic acid-related eicosanoid production and the production of cytokines including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), Interferon--γ (IFN-γ), interleukin 12 and Tumour Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α).10–13 The production of pro-inflammatory cytokines is upregulated by the activation of transcription factors such as NF-κB which in turn help to elicit the innate host response by recruiting neutrophils, macrophages and lymphocytes to the pulmonary infection site to clear fungus.14 The most important cytokine mediator is IL-1β secreted by PAMPs-stimulated macrophages. IL-1β production is transcriptionally controlled via NF-κB and post-transcriptionally processed into the mature bioactive IL-1β by caspase-1-containing intracellular signalling complexes, called inflammasomes.6,11,15 As shown by recent studies, these caspase-1-containing complexes are coupled to both CLR- and NLR-receptors/sensors which operate as fungal PRRs.6,16,17
