Visions of Power in Cuba: Revolution, Redemption, and Resistance, 1959–1971 by Lillian Guerra (review)
This is provocative, given the emphasis in historical and anthropological literature on the schemes of both local intellectuals and local politicians to connect their places to national rhetorics and histories, and as such it is very welcome. This approach squares with the divorce from the national that Luis Gonzalez described in San Jose de Gracia, but it does so several decades later, and without finding antagonism between local and national. Stack elsewhere emphasizes the malleability of history, tracing the evolution of Tapalpa's origin story from an Indian to a Spanish to a hacienda community, and this continuity becomes interesting. Here we have the patria chica beating back mass media and education.
