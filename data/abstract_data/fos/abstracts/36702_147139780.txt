The New Job for Schools of Journalism: The Past and Present Status of Training for Mass Communication
A CARTOON hanging in the dean's office in a certain large school of journalism shows a city editor of the murderous Charles Chapin type.' Before him is a timid boy, obviously applying for a job. "And what, may I ask, is a school of journalism?" says the glowering c.e. But that was in 1936. Today some newspapermen still snipe at the schools, but it is not at all unlikely that several of their own colleagues are part-time teachers at one of the near-by institutions of higher learning. While the managing editor is likely nowadays to call upon that school when he needs personnel, there are some die-hards who regularly report in the surveys that attending a school of journalism is a waste of time. These are the men who are the victims of an ignorance about the schools that Herbert Brucker, editor of the Hartford Courant, once described as "so big, strong, beautiful, and shining it is impossible to dent." One such man, some years ago, declared openly at a meeting of the American Society of Newspaper Editors in New York that he wouldn't have a journalism-school graduate on his staff. On his return to the Midwestern city where the paper he edited was published, some thirty of his staffers waited upon him to tell him that they were all holders of journalism degrees. They were not fired. The number of college-trained reporters on any large paper is far greater now, for the graduate of the school of journalism is no longer a novelty.
