The Merits of Multiple-Answer Items as Evaluated by Using Six Scoring Formulas.
The indices of item difficulty and discrimination, the coefficients of effective length, and the average item in formation for both single and multiple-answer items using six different scoring formulas were computed and compared. These formulas vary in terms of the assign ment of partial credit and the correction for guessing. Results show that items with multiple answers are sub stantially more discriminating and reliable when partial credit is given. The formulas without correction for guessing seem to perform at least as well as the formulas with correction. MULTIPLE-CHOICE ITEMS typically consist of a single correct option or answer and a number of incorrect options or distractors. An alternative multiple-choice item format consists of more than one correct option. This has been called the "multiple-response" (3, 7) or the "mul tiple-answer" (2) format. Multiple-answer (MA) items are identical to true-false items if the options are scored sepa rately. MA items have been used regularly in some foreign countries (e.g., The College Entrance Examination of Taiwan), but they have never been studied thoroughly. Al though there are many studies concerning the assessment of partial knowledge during the past 30 years, almost all of them are limited to single-answer (SA) items. One ex ception is the study by Dressel and Schmid (2), which com pared the MA format with three other SA formats: conven tional, free choice, and degree of certainty, using four intact classes. These formats all involved typical multiple-choice items with a single correct answer and three incorrect op tions. The conventional format instructed the examinees to mark only one option. Each item was scored as correct or incorrect. The free choice format instructed the examinees to mark as many options as necessary for them to feel confident that the correct option had been marked. Each item was scored by giving four points if the answer was marked minus the number of marked incorrect options. The degree of certainty format instructed the examinees to mark one option and to indicate the degree of confidence on a four-point scale (4?highest, 1?lowest). The point cor responding to the indicated degree of certainty was given or deducted depending on whether the item was marked correctly or incorrectly. The MA format instructed the ex aminees that any number of the options might be correct. Each item in this format was scored by giving the number of answers correctly marked minus the marked incorrect options (distractors). The results showed that MA items had the highest reliability (KR-20), but the examinees were able to complete a smaller percentage of these items relative to other formats. Differences in item discrimination (highest 25% vs. lowest 25% in terms of the total score for each format) were not significant. Similar findings were obtained for different levels of student ability. However, since mean scores for the four groups were significantly different, thus confounding group differences with format differences, the observed format differences were "less conclusive"
