Mathematics Majors' Views on Academic Curriculum and Career Decisions
With the increasing emphasis on college preparation in the sciences, there is a growing body of literature on the higher educational experiences of mathematics majors. Since the majority of this literature has been focused on the positive aspect of undergraduate training, the current study gauged both positive and negative academic-related concerns of mathematics majors. The sample consisted of both undergraduate and graduate mathematics majors (N=30) from The University of West Florida. In rank order, the following areas were reported as causing the most difficulty for mathematics students in our sample: Financial concerns, insufficient time for study, and challenging course-work. Interestingly, about a third of our respondents plan to seek employment after graduation, whereas 57% indicated aspirations to pursue graduate studies. Introduction Astin (1993) reported in his text, What Matters in College, on the changing aspiration of American undergraduates over the past two decades regarding a profound decrease in interest in the field of engineering and sciences. The book addresses, in general context, the issue of student satisfaction with the undergraduate experience in areas like curriculum, student life, support services, and instructional facilities. However, Astin neglects to report on the specific concerns of mathematics majors' vocational interests, attitudes toward academics and career goals. We viewed this with interest, as there is a growing literature-base on the concerns of mathematics majors toward their college-level preparation. For example, Morrow (1971) tested the effectiveness of Holland's theory of vocational choice for predicting students' (mathematics and sociology majors) satisfaction with their choice of college major; satisfaction with major was significantly related to personality-type among students majoring in mathematics but not among those majoring in sociology. These findings were later supported in a sample of female mathematics majors with the Strong-Campbell Interest Inventory (Miller, Heck, & Prior, 1988). In a doctoral dissertation, Lyons-Lepke (1986) studied the factors that influenced female mathematics majors to persist and graduate with a degree in mathematics: the areas identified were parental influence, strong career commitment, dedication to hard work, and positive classroom experience. Interestingly, this study found that mathematics majors were dissatisfied with the teaching ability of the instructors. Maple and Stage (1991) stressed that the mathematics/science major is of concern in light of contemporary demands created by advancing technology as well as potential gaps in the occupational attainment of women and minorities. These authors argued that attitude toward mathematics and high school emphasis on mathematics and science course work are critical prerequisites to the declaration of a mathematics/science major during the first two-years of college. Recently, Lapan, Shaughnessy, and Boggs (1996) noted that mathematics self-efficacy beliefs are a mediator to selection of mathematics as a college major and in maintaining vocational interest in mathematics. Thus the purpose of the present study is to gauge the views of mathematics majors at one university on the coursework within its mathematics department. Secondly, we also address the issue of future educational and career plans of our select sample. Method The target sample in this study included all undergraduate and graduate mathematics (declared) majors enrolled at the University of West Florida. These students (N=85) were distributed a survey form during official class time and asked to complete and return the survey to the department chair. Of these, 30 students responded to the entire questionnaire and, thus, our data analysis was based on 30 respondents.
