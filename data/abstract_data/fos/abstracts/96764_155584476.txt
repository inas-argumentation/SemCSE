The Decline of Unions, the Rise of Inequality
How did we get from the rise of union membership and the decline of inequality in the 1930s, to the decline of union power and the rise of inequality in some 40 years? We well know that the public reacted with desperate anger at the onset of the Great Depression and had the good fortune to elect a president who was “a traitor to his class.” So his Harvard classmates called him, a man who was willing to take unprecedented measures on behalf of the desperate public. Franklin Roosevelt oversaw the passage of the Glass-Steagall Act regulating banks, and the Wagner Act authorizing industrial workers to organize unions into a countervailing force against big business. FDR had a certain acerbic gift: “government by organized money is just as dangerous as government by organized mob ...” Unions not only organized labor but also identified the public interest and how to realize it, the what and the how. Americans rose out of poverty into the middle class by the millions. But not African-Americans. Unions discriminated, and against women too. Trade unions grew from the 1930s, through World War II, and through the 1950s. But not without difficulties. The United Mine Workers’ strike during the war angered the public. Wildcat strikes and work stoppages angered the public. Instances of union corruption and bossism again angered the public. A few errant unions gave ammunition to conservatives, who viscerally opposed the power of unions to begin with. All this agitation led to the passage of the Taft-Hartley Act in 1947, which for all practical purposes annulled the Wagner Act. The figures show the dramatic trajectory of private sector union membership from 17 percent in 1930, to a peak of 36 percent in 1953, and down to 11.3 percent in 2013. Public sector unions ran a different course. They have held steady at 37 percent since 1980. Governments evidently prefer to deal with one agent instead of millions of cantankerous individuals.
