Externalist Self-Knowledge and the Scope of the A Priori
Recently, Michael McKinsey (1991) and Jessica Brown (1995) have developed a challenge to externalist views of content, an argument that externalism entails a priori access to external facts of a kind that is obviously unavailable. Because their objection does not rely on disputed claims about a subject's capacity to compare the content of her present mental state with contents of remote ones (claims which are disputed in Falvey and Owens (1994) and Ludlow (1995), for example), this is, I think, the most powerful challenge that externalism has as yet sustained. But externalists can rest easy. Contrary to Brown's and McKinsey's assumptions, a subject's access to the external facts that externalism connects with content is no less a priori than her access to the content of her own thoughts. McKinsey first laid out the general pattern of the challenge, arguing that externalism would combine with a priori knowledge of the content of one's mental states to generate a priori knowledge of external facts which 'you obviously can't know a priori' (1991: 16). In calling knowledge 'a priori', McKinsey stipulates, he means 'obtained independently of empirical investigation' (1991: 9). The pattern needs to be filled in with a description of an external fact which could fairly be characterized as a condition which externalism imposes on possession of a content and which could plausibly be said obviously to be discernible only on the basis of empirical investigation.1 This specification is Brown's achievement.
