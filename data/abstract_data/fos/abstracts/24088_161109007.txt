William Carlos Williams proposed Paterson as the City of God, though now in its wasteland phase, but insisted that nothing of any significance—even the invention of paradisal suburban communities—can evolve until an imagination activates the energies of the local. But, as Gertrude Stein also insisted, there has to be a there, there. What was there before validates its creation and makes it humanly significant. Suburban "centers" are centerless and "empty of association" (233). Beuka finds some hope in D. J. Waldie's Holy Land: A Suburban Memoir, a work that "suggests the values that inhere to a generationally rooted place, even if that place is a suburban development" (241). It is that very rootedness that Waldie posits as the factor that transforms a profane landscape into a sacred one. Certainly the movie Pleasantville traces the protagonist David's struggle to resacralize his black-and-white life into the colors of his past innocence as it was embodied in his favorite TV shows of the 1950s. A regenerated innocence potentially redeems the ennui and anomie of a sterile world. SuburbiaNation convincingly delineates the life-denying, emasculating effects of a suburban landscape waiting for the revivifying energies of the local. And he accomplishes this challenging task with impressive scholarship, imaginative and original insights, and convincing eloquence.
