Selective HIV-related attention in general practice: is it possible?: A French-Norwegian study
General practitioners will be Increasingly engaged In HIV-related work. Information, advice and testing should be directed towards those patients who are most at risk in the HIV epidemic. To define who these patients are Is difficult. A questionnaire concerning sexual practice and other HIV-related risk factors was distributed to up to 50 consecutive patients per doctor consulting 83 general practitioners in France and Norway. Eighty-eight per cent of these patients aged 18–49 years completed the questionnaire. The Information given In the questionnaires is presented. In France the proportion of never-users of condoms was higher and heterosexual anal sex probably Is more widely practised. Adding the higher seroposltlvlty, people In France probably have a somewhat greater risk of HIV-infection than Norwegians. Approximately 40% of the male and 30% of the female patients In both countries reported more than one opposite-sex partner the last two years. Patients less than 30 years of age more often have multiple partners and more often use condoms than patients aged 30 years or more, but there is some variation between the countries and between males and females. About one third of each of the two general practice populations in this study has a sexual behaviour which may expose them to the HIV-virus. Only for some subgroups there is any association between condom use and perceived or probable risk for HIV exposure. HIV testing and other HIV-related attention in general practice should become more selective. The broad patient contact enables general practitioners to play an important part in the fight against HIV, but to do this general practitioners must be willing to increase their HIV related knowledge, question their own attitudes about sexuality and develop their ability in sexual history-taking and advising. Gaining the patient's confidence is crucial.
