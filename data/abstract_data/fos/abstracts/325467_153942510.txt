The medieval origins of the great landed estates of the Guadalquivir valley
During the first half of the thirteenth century the kingdoms of Castile and Le6n, definitively united from I230 onwards after the accession of Ferdinand iII (I230-52), experienced the most spectacular advance in the history of the Reconquest. Skilfully taking advantage of the disintegration of the Almohad state, the Castilian king advanced his southern frontier from the banks of the Tagus to the shores of the Atlantic and Mediterranean, thus more than doubling the size of his kingdom. Baeza, Ubeda, C6rdoba, Jaen, Seville, and the other towns of Andalusia were either reconquered or voluntarily surrendered to the Christians. The kingdom of Granada alone survived as the last Muslim redoubt in Spain.' It is obvious that, given the weak resources of the period, the Castilians had to deploy an enormous effort in order to cater for the administration, defence, and economic development of these southern lands which had to cope with the very large numbers of settlers and colonists coming from the Leonese-Castilian kingdoms as well as from other areas of the peninsula.2 Inevitably the disparity between the magnitude of the task and the precarious resources available produced problems. One of these was the birth of the great landed estates or latifundios which were to be so typical of the south. This polemical issue will be the subject of the present paper.3
