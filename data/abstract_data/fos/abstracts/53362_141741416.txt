Sexualization of Awareness: Catchy, but Does It Actually Increase Knowledge of Breast Cancer?.
Currently, in the United States, there exist numerous public awareness campaigns about breast cancer. Many of these campaigns are highly sexualized, focusing on the breasts as an object of fun, as opposed to focusing on information about prevention/diagnosis/treatment. In spite of their popularity, it is unknown what effect they actually have on knowledge about breast cancer, particularly in their target audience of young adults. We surveyed 255 college students to investigate the relationship of awareness of breast cancer campaigns and their knowledge of the risk factors, symptoms, and detection of breast cancer and recommendations regarding breast health. We found that while awareness of campaigns was positively correlated with knowledge of symptoms and diagnostic methods, it was not related to accurate knowledge of risk factors. Further, there was a difference in knowledge between those familiar with the sexualized prototype of I love Boobies and the knowledge prototype of the Susan G. Komen campaigns. While awareness is an admirable goal, it does not appear that the sexualized campaigns are doing more than titillating young adults, while the more staid but informative campaigns do appear to improve knowledge. Future research should examine whether the sexualized campaigns increase interest in attaining knowledge. Keywords: breast cancer, health awareness, sexualization, young adults Introduction Since Betty Ford's announcement that she was undergoing treatment for breast cancer in 1974, breaking what had been a nearly universal public silence about the disease, there has been a tremendous increase in public discussion about breast cancer (Sulik, 2012). Perhaps the primary benefit of this increased awareness is proactive behavior by women: women today are likely to discuss breast health with their doctors, receive screening measures regularly such as an annual mammogram, and breast cancer is most often diagnosed in the earliest, most treatable stages (American Cancer Society, n.d.). Today, approximately 288,000 American women will be diagnosed with breast cancer ("U.S. breast cancer," 2012). Certainly, this is a distressingly high number, and the number of women diagnosed annually has been steadily increasing in the last 30 years (Forouzanfar, et al., 2011). Part of this increase is almost certainly due to the increase in women receiving annual mammograms. Mammography, the screening of choice for most American women and their health insurance providers, has been hailed as the one tool guaranteed to save women's lives by diagnosing cancer in its earliest, treatable stages (Komen.org, n.d.). As screening has become more common and there has been an associated increase in remission rates (American Cancer Society, n.d.). However, the statistics for survival are not nearly as clear-cut. Some sources point to a steady decline in the death rate since 1990 ("U.S. breast cancer," 2012), while others acknowledge that the death rate, approximately 39,000 women in the USA annually, has not significantly decreased since the 1930s (Garfinkel, Boring, & Heath, 2009; Mayer, 2004; Kolata, 2002; Ries, et al., 2003). It is precisely this disparity between one tumor diagnosed and whether one life will be saved that is so concerning. While these are evening news-worthy questions the oncology community grapples with, our primary question was whether the increased public discussion of breast cancer and proliferation of awareness campaigns, particularly those utilizing sexualization, has actually increased the young adults' knowledge and understanding of breast cancer. According to APA's (2007) taskforce on the sexualization of girls and women in the media, sexualization and objectification occurs when "a person's value comes only from their sexual appeal or behavior", and when they are "made into a thing for other's sexual use".
