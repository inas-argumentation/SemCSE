Relative State Autonomy and National Economic Planning
As the role of the state within the U.S. economy steadily increased in the prosperous postwar years, Keynesian economists considered that this prosperity demonstrated the necessity of an increased role for the state. By the late 1960s, when Milton Friedman conceded that "we are all Keynesians now," it appeared that the new activist-interventionist state that Keynesianism advocated had indeed largely solved the problem of macroeconomic stability. Yet, a mere ten years later, confidence in Keynesian prescriptions had virtually disappeared in the face of stagnation, inflation, declining productivity and income levels, monetary crises, and recurrent business cycles. The growth of the state's economic role in the 1970s, combined with the economic chaos of that decade, ushered in a new breed of economists who ascribe to a sketchily defined "supply-side" economics. Unlike the Keynesians of the 1950s and 1960s, the supply-side economists (perhaps a better label would be the austerity school) see the state as the source of much, perhaps most, of what seems to be wrong with the U.S. economy.
