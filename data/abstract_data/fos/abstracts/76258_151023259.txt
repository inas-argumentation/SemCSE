Courts Rule in Different Directions on Class Actions against Accountants
With the enactment of the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act of 1995, the plaintiffs' bar wall now consider filing securities cases in state courts to circumvent the federal restrictions imposed on plaintiffs by the reform act. The following cases illustrate two different state law responses to class actions suits against accounting firms. CPAs owe a duty In December 1995, a federal district court in North Carolina ruled on this issue in the case of Simpson v. Specialty Retail Concepts, Inc., et al. This class action suit also named various officers and directors of SRC and the company's auditor, Deloitte, Haskins & Sells (now Deloitte & Touche). The suit sought recovery of damages allegedly suffered by investors because the company's 1986 financial statements overstated the company's net income. The claims against the firm were based on its opinion covering the company's 1986 financial statements, which the firm had agreed the company could include in its 10-K and annual report. The accounting firm sought dismissal of the plaintiff's claim of negligent misrepresentation on the grounds that it did not owe the investors a duty of care. The court analyzed this claim under Restatement (second) of Torts, section 552. The court found that a reasonable fact-finder could determine that the accounting firm had conducted its audit and prepared its report with the knowledge that: 1. Its opinion would be included in SRC's 10-K filings and annual report. 2. SRC intended potential public investors to rely on that information. The court concluded that public investors could constitute a "class of persons, distinct from the much larger class, who might reasonably be expected sooner or later to have access to the information and foreseeably to take some action and reliance upon it," citing a comment to section 552. The district court's reasoning for this ruling was as follows: Although public investors who read and relied on 10-K filings and a company's annual reports likely constituted a large group whose members were not individually designated in advance, nevertheless they represented an identifiable group for whose benefit and guidance the firm either intended to supply the information or knew that SRC intended to supply it.
