A succinct index of names follows the bibliography. Throughout his work, Schnapp offers ample citations from the texts he discusses, providing both French translations and, in footnotes, original quotations in Latin. His careful analysis allows him to unveil not only authorial sources, but also the lack of theological concordance of each text. More importantly, Schnapp’s study demonstrates that, despite the lack of originality of the texts he examines, the myth of the Antichrist is adapted to the Turks by authors who, in the years following 1453, actualized prophecies and, in effect, rewrote history. In his study, Schnapp rightfully stresses the uniqueness of George of Hungary’s work, underlining, for example, its pessimistic outlook and the presence of factual details about the Ottomans. However, the structure chosen to present his analysis and his findings is conducive to repetition and prevents him from providing a fuller account of, for instance, the exceptionality of George of Hungary’s work. Moreover, to qualify his treatise as apolitical seems, to this reviewer at least, hasty (344). On the upside, this study will prompt readers to return toGeorge ofHungary’sTractatus itself, which Schnapp has recently deftly translated into modern French. Schnapp’s work is an important complement to Margaret Meserve’s study on the attempts by Italian humanists to circumscribe the Turks. One can only regret that several sections of this volume contain a significant amount of consequential typographic errors. Nevertheless, by highlighting the congruence of the Antichrist with the Turk in the late fifteenth century, Schnapp has produced a noteworthy study for those who wish to understand early modern and modern views of Islam.
