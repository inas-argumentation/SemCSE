At the heart of the tense-based argument lie two concepts: the shifting of the deictic center and the historiographical regime of disentanglement. They both make clear how the author sees texts working dynamically. The deictic center is the “here and now” of an utterance. In devotional literature the “now” of the writer may approach the “then” of the object of meditation (say, Christ’s Passion or even the entire history of salvation). In the case of epitaphs the words might be presented as an utterance by the dead, but shifting toward the act of reading by a passerby. The use of tense switching to make the moment of writing and the moment of reading appear to coincide may make texts temporally uneasy, but it also fills them with energy. Along that line, Montaigne’s use of tenses is based on a paradox: he creates a shifting deictic center within the fleeting moment, and that is where he pulls the reader into communion with the dead author. The often-repeated idea of disentanglement means the distinction between the elements of history, or of a story, that live on in some sense and the elements that do not. At the same time, the author recognizes that some of what he has found is period specific and some is not. Which suggests that we who read the book are in a position similar to what the book illustrates repeatedly: what is alive and what is dead for us in the words of that past time? That metaconcept enlarges the fresh perspective of a rich exploration.
