The book is packed with numbers-specific unemployment rates, inflation rates, growth rates, and so on, and they make for slow going at times because the reasoning always depends on the precise relationship among them. There are some errors. Brazelton suggests that the Keyserling Council avoided a recession after World War II in 1949 (p. 54). But in fact there was a recession in 1948-49. He has a confused discussion of forecasting: "Forecasting ... has two weaknesses. First, it forecasts .... second, as it forecasts on past averages, it underestimates the full employment goal" (p. 71). The first presumed weakness is a tautology, and the second assumes that extrapolation of the past is the only method of forecasting. There are in fact very many methods of forecasting, some highly sophisticated and many based on factors other than past rates of change. Moreover, Brazelton speaks of the "full employment rate of growth" in connection with the full employment goal as though it were a precise factual state of affairs instead of at best an estimate based on a number of assumptions. There are a few other errors. Throughout the book Brazelton makes it clear that as between overinvestment and underconsumption, Keyserling was an underconsumptionist. Thus, this is a refreshing alternative to the mainstream emphasis, customarily encountered, on investment and the need to bolster entrepreneurial incentives. However, a more balanced approach is probably to emphasize the need for coordinating rates of investment with expected changes in consumption. Stability requires both correct expectations and the ability to alter production correctly in light of those expectations. While there are a number of problems with this book, in the end it is an interesting study of a man who played a significant role in an earlier era in formulating macroeconomic policy. Keyserling deserves the attention.
