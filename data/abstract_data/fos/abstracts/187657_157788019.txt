Economies in Transition: Hypotheses toward a Reasonable Economics
Once again, Thorstein Veblen had it right when he lamented the ". .. more frequent and more spectacular triumph of imbecile institutions over life and culture . . . [in] a desperately precarious institutional situation" [Veblen 1914, 25]. This thought came to mind as I reviewed Anders Aslund's How Russia Became a Market Economy and considered his "central thesis" in light of far more cautionary interpretations of what the Russian reforms have accomplished to date [Goldman 1994; Leitzel 1995; Nelson and Kuzes 1995; Smith 1995]. Aslund and Jeffrey Sachs (whom he calls "our natural intellectual leader" [1995a, 19]) were the principal Western advisers to Russia's first post-Communist government, and as advocates of shock therapy they helped to guide economic policies during 1992-93 [Lipton and Sachs 1992]. While Aslund's book may provide some perspective on the events of that time, the author's message of market triumph is better viewed as prophecy than as critical analysis. For despite his claim to provide an "empirical" account, only the most fervent of market ideologues might accept his conclusion.
