Undergraduate International Students: A Resource for the Intercultural Education of American Peers?.
Intercultural education is widely perceived as desirable and international students are increasingly perceived as a resource for fostering intercultural competencies among American college students. This study surveys undergraduate international students at a small, primarily undergraduate, public liberal arts university in the Midwest to investigate their willingness to help educate their American peers. The survey found that undergraduate international students are quite willing to do so, but prefer events they themselves organize as the vehicle for intercultural teaching. Some differences between students from different continents are noted. The paper discusses the implications of its findings and makes recommendations INTRODUCTION Intercultural and international education are widely perceived as desirable (ACE, 1995; Cushner, 1998; Gurin et al., 2002; Hansen, 2002; Howard-Hamilton, 2000; Johnston and Edelstein, 1993; Kohls and Knight, 1994; Lambert, 1994; Latham and Dalton, 1999; Schoem et al, 1995; Stewart, 2004; Task Force for Transnational Competence, 1997). It is increasingly acknowledged that, in an interconnected and globalized world, the importance of international education lies not only in personal enrichment but also--and perhaps more crucially--in the attainment of intercultural competencies (Carter, 1994; Kitsantas, 2004; Lewis, 1995). Intercultural competencies are defined by the ability to see both unique aspects of cultures as well as commonalities between them (Cushner, 1998; Khoi, 1994). Further, intercultural competencies are closely linked to the development of higher order thinking skills, i.e. the ability to perceive and understand different perspectives. Personal enrichment and the development of intercultural competencies are not strictly separable and certainly not antithetical. However, the difference is not irrelevant: personal enrichment can easily be dismissed as a luxury, whereas intercultural competencies become essentials skills in diverse societies and a globalized international environment. Indeed, a number of authors (Carter, 1994; Coulby et al., 1997; Khoi, 1994; Lewis, 1995; Merrifield, 1996) suggest that intercultural education has both domestic and international relevance. To provide access to intercultural education on campus, several authors advocate a role for international students (Carter, 1994; Cushner, 1998; Peterson et al., 1999). Such studies assume (sometimes implicitly) that international students are prepared and willing to perform such an educative function. This study investigates to what degree undergraduate international students at a primarily undergraduate public liberal arts university in the midwest are indeed interested in educating their peers about their respective cultures. It seeks to establish if, and in what way, undergraduate international students are willing to contribute to the intercultural education of their American peers. The study concludes with recommendations based on a survey of international students. RELEVANCE OF INTERNATIONAL/INTERCULTURAL EDUCATION Frequently, discussions on internationalizing higher education treat international students in the US and American students studying abroad separately. The literature on international students devotes much attention to adjustment issues (e.g., Al-Sharideh & Goe, 1998; Lacina, 2002; Ladd & Ruby, 1999; Wan, 2001). The literature on study abroad devotes much attention to the initiation, maintenance, and justification of such programs (e.g., Carmical, 2002; Hansen, 2002; Kitsantas, 2004; Lambert, 1989, 1994; Lewis, 1995).
