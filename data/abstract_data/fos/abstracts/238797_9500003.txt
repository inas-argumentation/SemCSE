Management of atrial fibrillation. Rhythm or rate? That is the question.
Relevance to family physicians Atrial fi brillation is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia. Its prevalence is estimated at about 1% of the population, and studies have projected the number of patients with AF will increase 2.5-fold in the next 50 years, directly refl ecting the aging population. This is important when we consider that AF and age are both strong risk factors for stroke and that AF can lead to a five-fold increase in incidence of stroke. Cerebrovascular disease is the second leading cause of death worldwide. When patients have AF, strokes are typically larger, are associated with higher early mortality, and occur more often in older patients. As well as suffering symptoms, patients with AF are at risk of serious complications, including cardiomyopathy and congestive heart failure (CHF). The mainstay of initial therapy for AF has long been directed toward maintenance of sinus rhythm with cardioversion and antiarrhythmic agents, an approach commonly believed superior to rate control only. Many antiarrhythmic agents, however, have potentially serious side effects and have not been shown to be consistently effi cacious. Second-line therapy is often ventricular rate control with atrioventricular node blockers. This approach appears attractive because it involves use of medications with better safety profi les, but how does it compare with regard to outcomes?
