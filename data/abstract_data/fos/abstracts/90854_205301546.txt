Russell's viper envenomation: acute hypopituitarism or acute primary adrenal insufficiency.
Sir, We read with interest the article by Rajagopala et al. where they have described a case series of acute hypopituitarism (AHP) complicating Russell’s viper envenomation. We are concerned that in the patients described, there is inadequate evidence of AHP. Firstly, AHP cannot be defined on the basis of random cortisol values of <4.3 lg/dl without a corresponding Adrenocorticotropic hormone, especially in the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation where adrenal hemorrhage is a known complication. Acute adrenal insufficiency has not been sufficiently investigated and ruled out by imaging or biochemistry. Further the appearance of adrenals in autopsy has not been described. Secondly, diagnosing secondary hypothyroidism in a critically ill patient on the basis of suppressed Thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T3 and free T4 is fallacious, as sick euthyroid syndrome cannot be convincingly ruled out. There is also no current evidence supporting benefit of thyroid replacement in this clinical setting. Thirdly, there are several plausible explanations for hypotension and hypoglycemia in these patients such as capillary leak, bleeding, renal and hepatic dysfunction. Abnormalities in pituitary imaging were conspicuous by their absence. In the survivors who were followed up the details of which pituitary hormones remained suppressed and needed replacement have not been provided. This is important as critical illness is a known cause of transient hypothalamopituitary dysfunction.
