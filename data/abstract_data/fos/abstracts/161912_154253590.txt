Jewish Kinship at a Crossroads: Lessons for Homelands and Diasporas
The transmission of culture and identity across frontiers has become an important feature in the interaction between diasporas and their kin states.1 This exchange is especially powerful in the United States where immigrants, old and new, continue to assert their identity with their kin states by virtue of ethnic, economic, or familial ties, and are attuned to the general concerns of their ancestral homelands.2 The ties and psychological identification of diaspora communities with their kin states, along with the concrete effects of homeland affairs on their own lives, lead diasporas to try to play an active role in the homeland. Moreover, ethnic communities in America have managed to maintain their identities by using homeland-related affairs as a vehicle to mobilize and sustain communal identities in America. These newly mobilized communities are in principle better able to turn their organized attention to critical issues in American politics and society. The real or symbolic connection with the homeland has been nourished by the growing legitimacy of organizing American social and political activity along specific ethnic lines as the cornerstone of multiculturalism.
