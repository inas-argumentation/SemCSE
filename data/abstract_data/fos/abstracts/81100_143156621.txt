Applied Psychology Revisited: Ward Psychology in the Neuropsychiatric Hospital
It is generally accepted that the role and functions of the applied psychologist in a hospital setting (clinical-counseling) shows modification. In many instances such change has been primarily motivated by pressures from outside the profession. In the past two decades, one observes conditions which have resulted in significant modification in the role of the applied psychologist. The needs of the veterans of World War I1 brought the psychologist out of the university and guidance center into the psychiatric hospital and clinic, primarily as it became clear that the demand on related disciplines was too great for the existing resources. Recently, attention has been directed toward the large number of these veterans who have required continuous psychiatric hospitalization over the past two decades. With increasing chronicity of illness, there has been a diminution in the need for classical diagnostic and psychotherapeutic activities. In effect, the traditional functions of the clinical psychologist appear less crucial than his clinical acumen in dealing with psychological illness. This has given rise to a new "breed" of applied hospital psychologist-the ward psychologist. Ward psychology implies a wider application of the clinical psychologist's skills rather than the acquisition of broader skills per se. Assessment is now oriented more toward assets, aptitudes, and capabilities. Therapeutic activities are utilized to help the patient return, as much as possible, to reality and society. Ward psychology means that a psychologist assumes longitudinal responsibility for a segment of patients. Assignment is generally defined on a ward basis with the psychologist primarily working on one or two wards. To be sure, medical responsibility and responsibility for the overall psychiatric treatment program remains with the psychiatrist. However, the ward psychologist is now perceived as a clinician who can help implement this program, as well as make recommendations as he has done in his more traditional role. Thus ward psychology involves a new orientation. It provides the psychologist with experience in what might be called social engineering, that is, in assisting chronically ill patients to fit into the niche which society provides. The neuropsychiatric hospital program of the Veterans Administration has paved the way for the inception of this new role for psychologists. However, the role is gaining acceptance in state hospitals which also have a large group of chronically ill patients. This increasing use of the psychologist could be accompanied profitably by assessment of the effects of such functioning on a hospital staff as well as on patients. If nothing else, the existence of a ward psychology program implies two advances, vzz., a renewed and possibly somewhat new emphasis on the "clinical" in "clinical psychologist," and the possibility of making the skills of more clinicians available to more patients.
