Theoretic Interests and the Schools
Schools provide children and youth with the in formation and skills needed for conscious deliberate choice. In order for the future adult to be able to | test and analyze in his mind the consequences of a certain choice of behavior, he is provided with con cepts and taught to manipulate the symbols that rep resent the concepts. If the school exercises are to interest the child, he must be able to relate the ideas to phenomena about which he is already curi ous, or about which his curiousity can be aroused. Some phenomena are concerned chiefly with things, others with people. We will call an interest in ideas about things THEORETIC; an interest in ideas about people PRUDENT. Ideas about people, at least in elementary and secondary schools, are usually ex pressed in words. Because the ideas are often dif ficult, the words must be used withfinediscrimina tion. Some children come to school already in com mand of many of the needed discriminations. Others do not. In the competition for learning fostered by the school they suffer a severe handicap. Ideas about things may also be expressed in words, but the use of other symbols is appropriate and some of these can be manipulated mathematically. Chil dren are less likely to come to school with ability in mathematics than they are to come with verbal ability. Skill in mathematics is not acquired in the normal course of family activities. Hence, when they begin school, few children can be far ahead in mathematics. Tests have been prepared to distinguish children and youth with Theoretic interests from those with Prudent interests and these have been tried out with thousands of children in the Bay Area, in London, England, in Puerto Rico, and in Ontario, Canada, in elementary schools, and in secondary. Teacher grades, scores on tests of intelligence and of achieve ment, grade placements, and father's occupation have all been collected for the same children. These data will be used to show that schools fav or children with Prudent interests. Those with The oretic interests are given little consideration. This suggests that the enjoyment some children experi ence from the manipulation of things could profit ably be exploited in the development of skill in math ematics or in augmenting their power over language. Children from cultural minorities would be at less of a disadvantage if Theoretic interests were con sidered. The data will show that teachers in lower class schools, both in California and in England, pay more attention to Theoretic interests than teach ers in upper and middle class schools. The princi ples at work call for elucidation.
