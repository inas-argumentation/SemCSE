Cattle on the Estates of Medieual Cistercian Monasteries in England and Wales
In I237 the monks and converse of English Cistercian houses were forbidden to trade in wool and hides.1 There is, however, little if any direct evidence of their actually marketing hides at this time. The only possible shred to come to light is of much later date: a note among the records of the borough of Nottingham under the year I397 showing that, in an action for debt, the abbot of Rufford had been 'attached by a cart with tanned leather to answer'.2 On the other hand it is quite clear from surveys, references to tanneries and vaccaries, and grants of pasture and rights of way that cattle commonly occupied an important place in Cistercian economy, notwithstanding the well-known interest of the Order as a whole and certain houses in particular in the great wool trade. Sheep were not everywhere and at all times second to none among the monks' farm animals and perhaps only stood well to the fore during the middle period of Cistercian activity from ca. I I75 to I350.
