Chapter 6, the last and longest, deals with a large time span: the last thirty years of Newton’s life. It is somewhat of a mixed bag and covers his political career, his works on biblical chronology, his controversies with Leibniz over the invention of the calculus and over metaphysical issues, and nods to relevant passages from the Opticks (1704) and the Principia. These topics are all treated concisely but informatively. Of interest is Guicciardini’s view that Newton did not enter philosophical debates unless he was put on the defensive: this underlines the private nature of most of Newton’s investigations. This graceful biography is not intended to supersede such monumental works as Richard Westfall’s Never at Rest (1980). Instead, this beautifully designed book is meant to be an entry point for anyone interested in Newton. However, it surely is also a gem for professional historians of the early modern period, and even seasoned Newton scholars might find some refreshing insights in it. Guicciardini’s profound knowledge of the subject shines through, especially on matters pertaining to Newton’s mathematical interests, on which the author is a leading scholar. On subjects where his expertise is less direct, Guicciardini aptly relies on and discusses the most recent secondary literature, as well as authoritative works by previous generations of Newton scholars.
