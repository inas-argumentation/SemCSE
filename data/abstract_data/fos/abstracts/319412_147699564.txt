Universities and the Foreign-Assistance Program: Guarding the Integrity of the Educational Enterprise
O NE of the popular subjects these days-in the daily press, in current periodicals, and in the halls of Congress is the way in which colleges and universities are being affected by the large amounts of money solicited from (or pressed on them by) a host of federal agencies. Is teaching suffering at the expense of research? Are universities prospering at the expense of undergraduate colleges? And, the most painful question of all, should all universities prosper equally, or should some be more equal than others? Everyone with even a passing interest in the status of higher education has encountered questions of this kind somewhere and in some form. And they are important questions, for their answers will shape, in a significant measure, the development of science and technology and the course of higher education in this country. The major attention in these discussions has, of course, been focused on federal support for scientific research. And why not, since, as Willie Sutton said about robbing banks, "that's where the money is"? There is, however, an aspect of the general problem of government-university relations that is largely non-scientific in character, that is growing in importance, that raises fundamental questions about the nature of higher education, and that has had insufficient airing both inside and outside higher educational circles.
