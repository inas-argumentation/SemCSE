Free and Unfree Rural Labour in Puerto Rico during the Nineteenth Century
Both these books form part of the current historiographic reassessment of the Puerto Rican colonial economy. The two case-studies considered here complement one another neatly, as Scarano deals with the operation of sugar plantations in the municipality of Ponce during the first half of the nineteenth century while Bergad focuses on coffee haciendas in the highland region of Lares during the second half. In each case, the argument concerns the nature of the agrarian workforce at a period when these respective cash crops dominated the export economy of Puerto Rico. Using the same kind of source materials (notarial records in municipal archives), Scarano challenges the view that chattel slavery was not an important component of sugar production, and Bergad contends that coffee production marked the emergence of a rural proletariat. Nineteenth-century Puerto Rican sugar plantation slavery has long been a bastion of the Freyre-Tannenbaum thesis, which asserts that ideological differences resulted in a more humanitarian master-slave relation in Catholic Latin America when compared with the harsher conditions prevalent in the Protestant Anglo-American colonies.'
