The Use and Misuse of College and University Counsel
That the Academy has become litigious is by now a circumstance too pronounced to be disputed. Those administrators who are not aware of the increase litigation by direct involvement in it are aware of its incidence through any of the several publications which daily come over their desks. Not surprisingly the reasons are complex and overlapping. They include the availability of counsel to individuals and groups who, as a practical matter, were not able to secure effective legal representation in the past; the advent of class-action lawsuits which, even with recent limitations, provides a means to get at issues which were impractical to raise before; and a coincident multiplication in the range of cognizable actions and remedies for those actions as well as agencies established to pursue them. The administrator who chooses to act without advice of competent counsel in areas where he or she knows (or reasonably ought to know) that advice should be sought, is just as foolish as the administrator who plunges into a new and costly program with reckless abandon as to where the funds or the students are coming from. The tenure of each will probably be about the same. Exactly what then should the lawyer be expected to do? It has been my observation that most "institutional" lawyers, particularly those in government, seem to play a limited role. They usually review transactions (such as applications for one thing or another or contracts) or they litigate.
