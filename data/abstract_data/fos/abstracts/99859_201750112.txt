The Origin of Bourbon Reform in Spanish South America, 1700–1763 by Adrian J. Pearce (review)
One of Murphy’s key claims is that the struggle for housing has been closely connected to notions of propriety. Such notions have been central to these struggles—among both politicians who decide housing policy and dispense (or take away) property and the pobladores themselves, who demand not just any home but a decent one that affirms their dignity. Many scholars have written about the land seizures that took place in the decade leading up to Chile’s 1973 coup, but Murphy is the first to discuss the ways in which participants in those actions sought to conform to notions of proper behavior in order to demonstrate they were worthy of urban services. “By demonstrating that they were responsible and disciplined, pobladores showed that they deserved, as citizens, to be homeowners who would have access to basic city services” (p. 97). In concrete terms, this meant people had to keep their houses clean, send their children to school, go to work if they had jobs, avoid alcohol, and avoid fights. The propriety imperative constituted a source of continuity with the past that challenged revolutionary claims to new ways of doing politics and establishing legitimate political claims.
