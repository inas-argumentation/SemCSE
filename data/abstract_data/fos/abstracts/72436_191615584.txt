Animal Imagery in Renaissance Art
One would generally begin a review of scholarship in a defined field by presenting those classical, groundbreaking publications that promoted and inspired subsequent writings and established precedents for discussions and debates of controversial issues. Yet in the case of animal imagery in Renaissance art, we are faced with a paucity of relevant studies. The lacuna is even more salient when we examine the comprehensive literature on animals in medieval art, relating to bestiaries, religious iconography (in painting, illumination, and architectural sculpture), moralistic allegories (such as virtues and vices), fabulous beasts (for example, hybrids and marine creatures), and classical influences in animal imagery, as in the studies by Joyce B. Salisbury, Luuk A. J. R. Houwen, Debra Hassig, Francesco Maspero and Aldo Granata, and Maria Pia Ciccarese. Most of these aspects and many more are, in my estimation, no less relevant to Renaissance than to medieval art. Not all Renaissance scholars, however, would agree with this assertion. It is generally stated in the literature that bestiaries exhausted their functions and gradually became extinct by the late medieval period. Animals in religious iconography of the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries are often interpreted as expressions of Renaissance naturalism and secular genre, assuming that this entails the rejection of medieval symbolism. It is doubtful that any art historian would deny the importance of moralistic allegory in Renaissance art, but somehow the determining function of animal images in these allegories is often overlooked. As for animal or human-animal hybrids: they flourished in every sphere and artistic medium, including paintings and monumental frescoes, architectural and freestanding sculptures, and sepulchral monuments and grotesque ornamentation, yet there seems to be a tacit assumption in most relevant studies that they were devoid of symbolic content. Some of the above contexts have been treated in focused scholarly articles, but questions regarding animal symbolism in Renaissance art have too often been treated as peripheral issues.
