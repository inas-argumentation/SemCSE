Economic Considerations in Televised Instruction: Their Implications for Colleges and Universities
HE problem of growth and development in a university community T differs in degree but not in kind from the problem of growth in underdeveloped countries-a topic to which economists, political scientists, and politicians are devoting increasing attention. Among the chief barriers to progress in the underdeveloped areas of the world are entrenched interest, lack of physical capital, and a paucity of education and industrial know-how. Ignorance, fear, superstition, and poor health compound the problem. In addition, the traditions, habits, institutions, and customs peculiar to underdeveloped countries may be inconsistent with, or inimical to, the requirements for the increases in productivity which in general are synonymous with economic growth. Instructional television presents the university community and society at large with opportunities for greatly increasing the productivity of the professional educator. It is now technically possible for the instructor to talk to, to reason with, and to demonstrate before a vastly enlarged audience. The problems which arise in the transition from the old to the new are analogous to those confronting underdeveloped countries: facilities are inadequate, professors are untrained in the use of the new tool and unaccustomed to new ways of accomplishing old tasks, lack of information and misinformation concerning the effectiveness of television instruction are almost universal, professors and administrators are frequently operating from entrenched positions, and so on. The first and the major cost to a large university planning to use instructional television is the purchase of capital equipment and facilities. This expenditure may vary enormously according to the quality and amount of equipment purchased. It is unlikely, however, that a major institution can develop a significant program involving instructional television for an investment of much under $ioo,ooo, although the initial cost may be less. If videotape recording is to be utilized-and both economic and educational considerations clearly indicate its desirabilityan additional $5,ooo will be needed for a machine suitable for broadcasting, or about $2o,o00 for a machine suitable for closed-circuit systems.
