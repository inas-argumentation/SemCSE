Labour Specialization and the Irish Economy in 1841: An Aggregate Occupational Analysis
Ireland in the I 840s is usually remembered as the location of one of the western world's last great demographic catastrophes: the potato famine of I846-I850.1 Quite rightly, much of nineteenth-century Irish historical and economic scholarship has concentrated on the famine, its demographic antecedents, and its social consequences for Ireland, Great Britain, and the New World.2 By i850, Ireland emerged as an apparent victim of Malthusian processes and as a major exporter of human resources.3 From a less idiosyncratic perspective, however, Ireland in the early nineteenth century can be viewed as one of many pre-industrial regions of western Europe which shared common characteristics of increasing population, economic growth, mercantile expansion, urbanization, and a growing socioeconomic complexity.4 The present research examines an aspect of the Irish economy in i84I that was shared to some degree by all European countries at the time. A marked regional variation existed in the division of labour and in the specialization of the proto-industrial and agricultural labour force. This regional variation may be explored, with a good deal of rigour, through occupational data. When examined in empirical detail, regional variation and covariation of occupations (as enumerated in censuses) can illuminate some otherwise dim corners of economy and society, yielding information on specific crafts and occupations and on the sexual division of labour; such an approach can also assist in identifying economic sectors and regional specializations. Each of these topics is touched upon here, leading on to a consideration of Ireland's industrialization, or lack thereof, in the nineteenth century.
