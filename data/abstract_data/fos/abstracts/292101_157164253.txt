Vienna & Chicago: Friends or Foes? A Tale of Two Schools of Free-Market Economics
This is an instructive and frustrating book. I read it seeking to learn about the distinctions between the Chicago school, which is well integrated within the neoclassical mainstream, and the Austrian school, which is not so. The book is a readable description of the development of the two over the course of (mostly) the twentieth century. The discussion of Menger, Bohm-Bawerk, von Mises, Hayek, and Rothbard is instructive, as is the comparison between theories of business cycles and macroeconomic stabilization policies. There are a number of problems, however the principal one being the premise that the only correct choice on any issue is between the Chicago school and the Austrian school. Thus, Keynesian views are brought up only to be corrected preferably by Keynesians themselves, as when Hicks, 84 years old but still "alert," tells the author "how he had gradually become disenchanted with many aspects of the modern economic theory he helped to develop." He "seemed greatly displeased by the failure of orthodox economists to teach the importance of time and the stages-ofproduction concept in macroeconomics, a subject he learned from the Austrians ... " (p. 289). Similarly, the views of the American institutionalists are generally brought up only when they can be made to appear silly. Galbraith is a favored foil: a chapter on competing economic methodologies contains a section on the institutionalists that consists entirely of attacks on The Affluent Society, The New Industrial State, and a television series Galbraith did for the British Broadcasting Company. John R. Commons and J.M. Clark are not listed in the index, though there is a short discussion of Wesley Mitchell. Thorstein Veblen is mentioned only to demonstrate that the University of Chicago, at times, has employed heterodox economists. There are many examples of where this triumphalist view leads the author in unfortunate directions. Readers may be surprised to learn that Public Choice has replaced Public Finance, as evidenced by the fact that Musgrave's classic public finance text has gone out of print. There will be similar surprise at the announcement that within the profession "there appears to be universal support for Say's law of markets" but then Say, considered a precursor of the Austrians, is given exalted treatment throughout the book. Skousen cannot bring himself to admit that Say's Law as interpreted by Ricardo, Mill, and pretty much everyone since is a specific statement of the self-regulating nature of the macroeconomy and the impossibility of a "general glut."
