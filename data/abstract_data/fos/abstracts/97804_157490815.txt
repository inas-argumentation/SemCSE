Modern Capitalism and the Trend toward Deindustrialization
For over half a century, economists interested in the industrialization process of capitalist economies have been searching for regularities in the historical patterns followed by different countries. In some cases, attention has been focused primarily on the changing distribution of employment by industrial sector or occupation. In other studies, concern has centered on the changing patterns of output [Clark 1940; Kuznets 1958, 1959; Chenery 1960; Maizels 1970; Chenery and Taylor 1968; Fuchs 1968]. Their efforts have not gone unrewarded. In almost every study, emphasis has been given to the importance of levels of per capita income in determining the composition of employment or output. Income levels dictate consumption and investment patterns, and these, in turn, have a pronounced influence on employment patterns. Some investigators have also included supply factors as important determinants of output patterns, arguing that similarity of tastes across countries with comparable income levels is not alone sufficient to explain similarity in industrialization patterns [Chenery 1960; Maizels 1970].
