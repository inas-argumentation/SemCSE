Models in history: a micro-study of late nineteenth-century British entrepreneurship
One of the most notable achievements of the econometric historians has been the establishment of a new orthodoxy regarding the performance of late Victorian and Edwardian entrepreneurs. What was once dismissed by an earlier generation of scholars as an abysmal record, attributed by H.G. Wells to the doubly malign influences of Oxford University and the Church of England, has now been firmly enumerated away.' At the level of the individual firm matters remain, however, somewhat less clear. There were, of course, the well-known success stories like Boot, Lever, and Lipton, all of them-initially at any rate-in the retail sector. On the other hand, there is an enduring belief, not least among politicians and journalists, in the thesis of entrepreneurial failure, and studies of firms in manufacturing industries as diverse as pianos, hardware, watches, and motor cars have suggested that the performance of British entrepreneurs was not always as convincing as the new orthodoxy suggests.2 It remains true, as Payne hinted some years ago, that the calculus of the new orthodoxy will acquire real conviction only when it is seen to rest on the more empirical foundation of individual firms' histories.3 This essay represents a contribution to such a foundation. It is a study of a single, small firm, but one which responded positively to market opportunity and which in slightly under 20 years eliminated continental competition from the domestic market, growing to become the world's largest producer in its own particular sphere of manufacturing. In general the toy industry has not attracted much attention from professional economic and social historians.
