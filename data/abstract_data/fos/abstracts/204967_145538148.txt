The obvious answer is that they didn't know they were doing something seriously stupid in fixing the exchange rate at about 3.5 and holding it there. While there is a great deal of retrospective fingerpointing in this volume, very few people, with the honorable exception of Rudi Dornbusch, were really convinced that the exchange rate was far out of line. Many observers, not all of them fools, believed that a small adjustment was needed. But if you had suggested a devaluation on the order of what transpired, they would have thought you were crazy. What occurred in December 1994 was not simply a devaluation of the peso, but a massive speculative attack on it as well. Such attacks were not unprecedented. One had occurred most recently in September 1992 against sterling, costing Great Britain millions of pounds and Norman Lamont, then Chancellor of the Exchequer, his career. And the academic literature also suggested that managed exchange rates were most vulnerable to destabilizing speculation: you either fixed or floated. So why did the Mexicans think they could succeed where others failed? Why did they, as Weintraub observes, bet a nation and lose?
