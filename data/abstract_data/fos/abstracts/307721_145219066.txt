We know, for example, that physicians, state governors, and diplomats are considered to have high prestige, and shoe sh ine r s , street sweepers, and garbage collectors, low prestige. Teaching tends to occupy the middle ground, although it is somewhat closer to the high prestige than to the low prestige occupations (Hatt and North, 1947). Perceptions of the prestige of the special class teaching areas have been studied only recently (Jones and Gottfried, 1966). The Jones-Gottfried study revealed that special education teaching was judged to carry higher prestige than regular class teaching. However, the Jones-Gottfried sample was limited to comparisons within the field of education. Thus, little was revealed about special education in relation to other occupations. And while information about the standing of special education teaching in relationship to regular class teaching is valuable, it seems important, also, to Itnow something about the standing of the special education fields in relationship to other occupations. The present, study was designed to obtain this information.
