The German Business Cycle in the 1920's: A Comment
Both Prof. Temin and Dr Falkus differ from the traditional historiography in that they regard the behaviour of inventory investment as a major component in the explanation of this recession. Temin, however, rules out monetary determinants of the rate of inventory investment, while Falkus rehabilitates them. I believe that in this period, inventory investment is something of a red herring, introduced by defects in the statistical series used. Temin relies on Hoffmann's estimates of the annual rate of inventory investment in mining, manufacturing, trade, and the artisan sector, deflated to constant (I 9 I 3) prices.3 Hoffmann deflates by applying the annual average of the official wholesale price index to cumulating estimates of inventories at the year-end. I think his deflator is faulty for two main reasons. Firstly, the construction of the underlying current value data4 (based chiefly on balance-sheet analysis) provides little justification for the use of annual average prices. In somewhat analogous cases, Abramovitz and Feinstein use only the spread of prices during the last four to five months of the year.5 Secondly, since in this discussion the concern is expressly with inventory investment outside agriculture, the commodity weighting of the official wholesale price index is inappropriate. It gives unduly heavy weighting to agricultural prices, since it is designed "to measure the purchasing power of the circulating capital of the entire economy".6 I have prepared alternative estimates of non-agricultural inventory investment at constant prices.
