The chapter also conjures a castoff servant girl with whom, Weil argues, Lear identifies. Examining slavery and freedom in Macbeth, Weil startlingly reads the final image of Macbeth’s head on a pike as an “emblem of a tool wearing a head” that “makes visible the horror of becoming a human thing in a world where service and clientage were still facts of life” (145). Weil’s study provides impressively detailed readings of the vocabulary, imagery, and characteristic problems of service in Shakespeare’s plays and engages a broad range of the contemporary intertexts. The author also draws from a valuable depth of literary scholarship as well as from social history and historical sociology. Service and Dependency offers important new perspectives on Shakespeare’s plays and the institutions of early modern service, especially in its emphasis on the varied forms and fears of agency and servility that such service allowed and provoked.
