Welfare Reform: What Are the Numbers, and Does Anyone Care?
When the 104th US Congress passed HR 3734, The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Act of 1996, it significantly redefined the federal government's role in welfare I delivery. The act was the product of a long period of widespread disillusionment with the welfare system and brought an end to Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC), the foundation of the US system of cash income transfer since the 1930s. The Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) program replaced AFDC. As the titles ofthe act and the program suggest, the intent ofthe new program is to provide temporary assistance and induce behavioral changes in recipients, moving welfare recipients off the welfare rolls and into jobs. Because of implementation and data collection lags, evidence regarding the impacts of the new program is only now beginning to emerge. It will be several years before more definitive analyses can be done. Still, even at this early stage, it is worth considering the impacts of welfare reform. To do so is the purpose of this paper: to look at recent trends in welfare-related variables, to review the early analyses of the trends, and to briefly survey attitudes toward welfare reform. The paper will begin with a brief discussion of the character and purposes of the reform legislation. Then trends in selected data series will be examined to see whether the variables targeted by the reform seem to be changing in the predicted ways. Next, two major studies will be reviewed to see whether or not the data trends can be attributed to welfare reform. The paper will conclude with evidence regarding attitudes toward the apparent success or failure of welfare reform and a few closing cornments.
