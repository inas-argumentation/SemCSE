Race, Rights, and Recognition: Jewish American Literature since 1969 by Dean J. Franco (review)
Because Gavin does not attempt to explain the mix-up of letters he sent to both women, they give up on him, with the result that for twenty years he suppresses an important dimension of himself and associates himself more with the gossip of Chick's mother and her five girlhood friends, who follow Melisandre's seeming nonlife from a great distance. In saving Gualdres's life from Max's ploy to replace a blind horse with a wild, uncontrollable one, Gavin emerges the victor and becomes king of the castle. Wainwright presents many considerations, some convincing and others less so, which open up not only the intricate world of chess in many of its ramifications, but also Faulkner's own gambit in accounting for his view of the world where pieces, however knowledgeable or limited, are sometimes moved about by an unseen player.
