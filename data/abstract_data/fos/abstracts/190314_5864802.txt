Is it time for plakoglobin immune-histochemical diagnostic test for arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy in the routine pathology practice?
Since the beginning, the major clinical challenge in arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (AC) has been the absence of a single, goldstandard criterion sufficiently specific to establish reliably a diagnosis [1]. Multiple parameters are needed, as included in the original 1994 Task Force diagnostic criteria, which combine several sources of information, such as familial, electrocardiographic, arrhythmic, morphofunctional, and histopathological findings [2]. Revised criteria were published in 2010 to improve the diagnostic sensitivity but with the important prerequisite of maintaining the diagnostic specificity [3]. These criteria provide quantitative imaging cutoff points to define a normal right ventricle and to categorize the various degrees of morphofunctional right ventricular (RV) abnormality. The need to assess the specificity of tissue characterization parameters and of quantitative data in terms of amount of fibrofatty replacement of the atrophic myocardium in samples obtained from the RV free wall was also recognized since long time ago [4–6]. Thus, residual myocytes b60% by morphometric analysis (or b50% if estimated), with fibrous replacement of the RV free wall myocardium, even in the absence of fatty replacement, is listed as major criterion; whereas residual myocytes 60% to 75% by morphometric analysis (or 50% to 65% if estimated) as a minor criterion [3,7]. Meanwhile, alongside with the increasing evidence that AC is a genetically determined disease of cell-to-cell junctions [8], Asimaki et al. [9] developed a new diagnostic test for AC that is based on immunohistochemical analysis of junctional proteins in human myocardial samples, obtained either from full heart specimens (autopsy and cardiac transplantation) or endomyocardial biopsy. This test revealed a reduced signal for junctional plakoglobin at intercalated discs in most AC patients. Compared with the 1994 AC clinical Task Force criteria, the immunohistochemical test seemed to have higher sensitivity (91%) and specificity (82%) for the diagnosis. Noteworthy, the low immunoreactive signal for plakoglobin was observed in myocardial biopsy specimens from all areas of the heart, regardless the presence of fibrofatty replacement on routine histology. It was thus suggested that, when applying this immunohistochemical test, it is not necessary to sample the heart in areas showing structural changes, at difference from the classical histomorphometric quantification of fibrofatty tissue that needs to target the diseased areas. The discovery was well received and raised great expectations among the investigators. However, additional validation studies were advocated before this immunohistochemical test could enter the clinical arena as routine biomarker.
