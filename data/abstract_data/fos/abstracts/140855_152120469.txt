The Relationship among Childhood Discipline, Adult Attachment, and Academic Dishonesty in College Students.
Academic dishonesty among college students is now recognized as a serious problem across colleges and universities, with as many as 8 out of every 10 students admitting to engaging some form of cheating in their collegiate courses (Gabriel, 2010; McCabe, Butterfield, & Trevino, 2006). Academic dishonesty encompasses a number of inappropriate scholastic behaviors such as copying others' answers during quizzes or examinations, receiving inappropriate help from others in completing assignments, and using information without properly citing sources. Previous researchers have found that a variety of environmental and personal factors are associated with academic dishonesty. Environmental variables associated with cheating in college courses include the quality of students' secondary education (Jackson, Levine, Fumham, & Burr, 2002), sanctions against cheating on college campuses (Robinson, Amburgey, Swank, & Faulkner, 2004), and the attitudes of one's peers toward cheating (McCabe et al., 2006). Personality characteristics found to be associated with academic dishonesty include increased egocentrism (Brunell, Staats, Barden, & Hupp, 2011), greater levels of extrinsic versus intrinsic motivation (Rettinger, Jordan, & Peschiera, 2004; Rettinger & Kramer, 2009), decreased self-efficacy (Finn & Frone, 2004), and heightened impulsivity (Anderman, Cupp, & Lane, 2009). More recently, researchers have investigated various family-of-origin factors that may play a role in predicting academic dishonesty. For example, Bong (2008) found that students who perceived greater obligations toward their parents were less likely to cheat; whereas, students who experienced higher levels of conflict and received less parental support were more likely to engage in academic dishonesty. Madill, Gaia, and Qualls (2007) found that academic dishonesty was associated with students' lower ratings of satisfaction with their familial relationships. In their study, college students from families who demonstrated higher levels of positive communication and supportive engagement participated in fewer incidents of cheating. Additionally, students who described receiving more severe forms of physical punishment as children (e.g., being slapped or being hit with fists) endorsed greater rates of academic dishonesty. This association between harsh physical discipline in childhood and increased cheating in college was replicated by Qualls (2014) in a larger college sample, with the frequency of severe physical punishment explaining 13% of the variance associated with academic dishonesty. In this study, spanking had an indirect effect on academic dishonesty through its increased association with severe physical punishment, with higher rates of spanking found to be predictive of harsher acts such as being punched or kicked. Another individual characteristic that stems from one's family of origin is the quality of attachments with others. Based on pioneering work by John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, Attachment Theory holds that the quality of early relationships between children and their primary caretakers influences the development of internal working models of attachment that children have not only with their primary caretakers, but also with other people as well (Ainsworth, Blehar, Waters, & Wall, 1978; Bowlby, 1969). Bowlby found that healthy attachments develop through different stages and are engendered by caretakers providing consistent nurturing and supervision of infants and toddlers (Bowlby, 1969). Ainsworth extended Bowlby's work to demonstrate that the failure to provide adequate nurturing and support in early childhood increased the likelihood of children developing unhealthy styles of attachment (Ainsworth et al., 1978; Ainsworth, 1979).
