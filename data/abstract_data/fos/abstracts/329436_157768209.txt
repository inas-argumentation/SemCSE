Centralized Private Sector Planning and the allocation of Automobile Credit
The development of a wide and deep market for consumer durable goods in the twentieth century was made possible in large part by the evolution of large-scale systems of marketing and distribution. A salient characteristic of the modem form of business organization is the forward integration of large business enterprises into previously nonintegrated activities such as wholesale and retail distribution. Alfred Chandler attributes this phenomena to the inadequacy of existing networks of marketing and distribution for the requirements of modem mass production technology.' Similarly, the integration of the major durable goods manufacturers into the finance industry can be explained by the inadequacy of existing facilities of credit allocation to the needs of modem large-scale distribution. The general availability of wholesale and retail credit is of strategic importance in the consumer durable goods sector. A predictable, stable flow of wholesale and consumer credit extensions is essential to production planning as well as to sustaining the demand for consumer durable goods.
