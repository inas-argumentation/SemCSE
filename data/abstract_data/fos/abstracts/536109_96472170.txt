Suenan las piedras: Alzamiento ocurrido en la ciudad de Mexico, 14, 15, y 16 de septiembre de 1847 (review)
Recent scholarship on early republican Mexico has stressed the role of the urban popular classes as key historical actors during the turbulent post-independence era. The work under review, a revised version of Luis Fernando Granados' B.A. thesis, builds on this trend as it analyzes a neglected aspect of the U.S.-Mexican War of 1846-1848: the three-day September 1847 riot by Mexico City's so-called "masses" against General Winfield Scott and the United States expeditionary army. Granados rejects the conventional notion that anti-U.S. feelings prompted the confrontation. He instead portrays the rebellion as a massive and spontaneous movement, largely driven by the growing involvement of the urban poor in public affairs and by governmental policies that, as they sought to mobilize the populace for the war effort during the months prior to the insurrection, instead worsened their living conditions. This state of affairs ruptured the loyalty of the poor to Mexican authorities and led them to resist Scott's forces.
