A second strength of The Proletarian Moment is its meticulous examination of what was actually said by the various American critics. While Philip Rahv, Wallace Phelps (William Phillips), and James T. Farrell subsequendy portrayed themselves as the lone defenders of modernism and aesthetic standards against the New Masses philistines, Murphy demonstrates that, in the pre-1937 period, Eliot, Joyce, and Proust were as often attacked by the anti-Stalinists as they were defended by New Masses critics like Granville Hicks, Joseph Freeman, Moissaye Olgin, and even the notorious Mike Gold. Going beyond the two major literary magazines to various "little mags" of the John Reed Clubs and, above all, to the Daily Worker, Murphy produces rich documentation for the case that the entire American literary left was skeptical of the tendentiousness and didacticism that they commonly grouped under the rubric of "leftism."
