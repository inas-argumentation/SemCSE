The Economic Challenge for Europe: Adapting to Innovation based Growth
While I am probably one of the few who enjoy research about pension institutions, I still had to be won over by Clark's arguments. I gradually shifted from floundering a bit on the importance of the multiple and complicated linkages he was making to a view that the book probably tries to do too much, but his thoroughness serves his purpose well. The book is certainly not for novices in the field. Despite the thoroughness of explanation, grappling with the linkages among pension fund design, control, portfolio structure, spatial, social and economic interface of financial intermediaries, AlP evaluation, and the myriad of philosophical arguments requires considerable intellectual intensity to stay with the arguments. F or those who are struggling with devising an intellectual infrastructure in which to place the gargantuan financial size and related issues of pensions in the Anglo-American world, the book offers much. I'd also recommend it as a graduate student text, which the instructor can use to prompt students to inquire about the linkage components. In the first line of the introductory chapter the author writes, "This book is an exercise in political economy and economic geography" (p. 1). Measured against this objective, the author has succeeded, while I think some minor reorganization would help the flow of the book and the thematic grasp by the reader. The book clearly contributes to the literature by exploring a new terrain. Calibrating the magnitude of this contribution in adding a span to the economic geography and pension institution connection will require time. A more subtle contribution may be that a few more scholars will be prompted to the area of qualitative research about pension institutions.
