The tectonics and depositional history of the Ordovician and Silurian rocks of Notre Dame Bay, Newfoundland: Discussion
Amott et al., on the basis of stratigraphic and paleontological evidence, interpret the present distribution of Ordovician and Silurian strata in north-central Newfoundland to be the result of the deposition of volcanic-arc-derived sediments in subsiding fault-bounded basins. They further conclude that there has been no large-scale strike-slip faulting in the Notre Dame Bay area and that any major lateral transport of the Dunnage Zone must have occurred along faults somewhere west of the Baie Verte Line. The following discussion challenges some of the arguments on which their conclusions are based and stresses the complexity of deformation in the Notre Dame Bay area. We wish to point out the difficulties in deriving Ordovician and Silurian paleogeography from present lithological distribution, particularly if no cognizance is taken of deformational structures. Similar criticisms were made of an eariier paper by Amott (1983) in a discussion (Karlstrom et al. 1983) to which there has not yet been a reply. First, we consider the eastern boundary of the Dunnage Zone as interpreted by Amott et al., since this boundary has significant bearing on the interpretation of central Newfoundland geology. The Reach Fault has been interpreted as the Iapetus suture by Kennedy (1975) and McKerrow and Cocks (1977), and Amott et al. adhere to this interpretation on the grounds that no Ordovician or Silurian formations cross the fault and that distinct pre-Late Ordovician benthic faunas are separated by the fault. However, although no unit crosses the fault undisturbed, stratigraphic correlations have been made across the fault by Dean and Strong (1977), Karlstrom et al. (1982), and Colman-Sadd and Swinden (1984). These correlations suggest that the Reach Fault does not represent a major lithological break. Furthermore, there is clear evidence that the fault is a late feature (post-lithification): it overprints the regional S, cleavage, which is Llandoverian or younger (Karlstrom et al. 1982), and deforms the Loon Bay Granodiorite, which contains xenoliths dated at 365 f 10 Ma (Devonian) by Kay (1976). Stouge (1980a,b) correlated midOrdovician conodont faunas from the Davidsville Group east of the Reach Fault with those of the Cobbs Arm limestone of New World Island. In reply to a discussion by McKerrow and Cocks (1980), he pointed out the ambiguities involved in the use of faunal provinces to distinguish separate lithological plates, rather than depositional environments, and further indicated that conodonts may be viewed as benthic faunas (Stouge 1980~). More recently, Wonderley and Neuman (1984) have recognized in the Indian Bay Formation of the Gander Terrane a late Early to early Middle Ordovician benthic fauna (brachiopods, trilobites, bryozoans, and pelmatozoan ossicles) similar to that found in the Summerford Group of New World Island.
