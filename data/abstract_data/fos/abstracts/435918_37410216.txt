Methodological Problems in Describing the Addictive Personality
Attempts to describe the "addictive personality" have been unsuccessful (Millon, 1969). This report suggests that inconsistencies between different investigators may result from the high proportion of invalid test protocols given by addicts and the influence of psychopathology on the psychological test scores. The protocols of 55 ex-addicts in a vocational training program (Lester, et al., 1975), to whom the MMPI and the Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (EPPS) had been administered, were examined. Twenty-four ex-addicts had invalid protocols, that is, consistency scores on the EPPS 4 9, lie scale scores ( L ) on the MMP1 A 10, or faking good scale scores (F) on the MMPI 2 16. The EPPS scale scores of the remaining ex-addicts (mean age 29.6 yr., SD = 7.6; 9 white, 19 black, one Puerto Rican) were compared with norms for the test. These 29 ex-addicts had significantly higher needs for Achievement, Autonomy, Intraception, and Heterosexuality and significantly lower needs for Affiliation, Nurturance, and Endurance. Of the 208 product-moment correlations between scores on the EPPS and the MMPI, 25 were significant at the 5% level or better on two-tailed tests. These significant correlations ranged from 0.37 to 0.65 (median = 0.44). Only scores for the needs for Autonomy, Nusturance, and Endurance were unrelated to scores on the scales of the MMPI. The other 12 needs measured by the EPPS were each related to one or more of the scores from the clinical scales of the MMPI. These two findings, of faking and inconsistent responding to psychological tests and of associations of personality with psychopathology, may account for inconsistencies in the reports from different investigators of the psychological characteristics of addicts.
