Self-Reports of Group Cohesion under High and Low Cooperation
The classical studies by Sherif and Sherif (1956) demonstrate that activities which evoke cooperation in a group may be staged in a special camp environment. A decade of observations in a therapeutic summer camp indicate, also, that a program designed primarily for treatment can be structured so that a high level of cooperative behavior emerges (Rickard & Dinoff, 1974). It has been observed, too, that campers engaged in cooperative activities appear happier and better adjusted. The present study tested the hypothesis that campers behaving cooperatively will report a greater liking for their fellow members and that they will judge the group as a more attractive, cohesive unit. Three groups of boys ( N = 22) attending a summer camp for emotionally disturbed children served as subjects. The counselors identified a day, based on observational criteria. when each group displayed a high degree of cooperative behavior, and a day when each group showed a low degree of cooperative activity. On each of those days the subjects were asked to list the fellow campers whom he liked, those whom he disliked, and to rate the group as to its over-all attractiveness. It was predicted that during periods of high cooperation subjects would view the group as attractive and report more liking and less disliking of fellow campers as compared to periods of low group cooperation. Data for the three groups were combined; under conditions of high cooperation an average of 6.0 campers were identified as liked and 1.14 as disliked. A mean of 4.14 were identified as liked and 2.36 as disliked under conditions of low cooperation. Ratings as to the attractiveness of the group were more favorable, also, under high cooperation. Sign tests (Siegel, 1956) indicated that differences in each of the self-report measures were statistically significant and in the predicted direction ( p < .01). While other self-report measures have correlated poorly with each other and with behavioral ratings when between-group comparisons are made (Eisman, 1959; Gross & Martin, 1952; Gruen, 1965), the present findings suggest that simple self-report measures may be sensitive to variation in conditions of cooperation ruithin a group. A logical next step would be to manipulate specifically conditions of cooperation and to record the impact upon self-report measures.
