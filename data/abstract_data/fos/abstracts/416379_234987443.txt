Subjected to verbal and physical abuse, sexual threats, and compromising situations far outside their social experience and standing, Duplessis-Mornay, Chaufepié, and Noyer demonstrate great ingenuity and fortitude. They craft disguises, transgress social and gender roles, and deploy patronage networks at their disposal to survive. Winn takes pains to emphasize that these narratives are more than stories of personal resolve and ingenuity in the face of extraordinary pressure. They represent a larger religious and literary tradition rooted in Calvinist tropes of divine election and protection, while also speaking to the central importance of women’s voices to the early modern experience of religious warfare, violence, and state building.
