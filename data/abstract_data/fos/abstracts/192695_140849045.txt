Trapped: Technology as a Barrier to Leaving an Abusive Relationship
A 42 item Internet questionnaire was completed by 259 undergraduates at a large southeastern university designed to assess the degree to which individuals involved in abusive relationships would report that technology in the form of text messages, email, and Facebook would impair their ability to terminate the relationship. Almost sixty percent respondents (regardless of their demographic background or whether they were in an abusive relationship) reported frequent use of technology. However, respondents needing to leave an abusive relationship more frequently identified with those areas that would be perceived as a barrier (e.g. receiving text messages that called the respondent names). And, those who were "abuse prone" were more likely to stay in abusive relationships even though parents and friends disapproved. Implications and limitations are identified. ********** Undergraduates begin, develop, and end relationships using text messages. This study examined the degree to which technology (texting, Facebooking, and email) resulted in romantic partners being technologically embedded in each others' lives and how, at breakup, extricating oneself from the relationship was made difficult by technology. While stopping or ignoring text messages is an obvious solution to removing a previous partner from one's life, the compulsion to check ones text messages and respond (characteristic of the abuse prone) may inhibit one's ability to withdraw. Background With the introduction and increased use of technology, previous research has included the impact of technology on relationships. Some of the studies have focused on the different stages of leaving a relationship, but have failed to address the emotional and verbal abuse through the use of technology after the relationship has been terminated (Edwards et al. 2012; Bergdall et al. 2012). Researchers have also recognized social networking (e.g. Facebook) as a problem in relationships but only to the extent of intensifying feelings of jealousy, possession, and insecurity (Gershon, 2011; Bowe, 2010). College students not only use Facebook for stalking their ex-partners, but also to make the previous partner jealous by posting photos of ones new romantic relationships (Lyndon et al. 2011). Finally, previous research has shown that battered women have difficultly moving on and that individuals in romantic relationships use cell phones to express affection, but research has failed to connect the use of technology and extricating oneself from a romantic relationship (Coyne et al. 2011; Anderson, 2003). Hence there is a gap in previous research in regard to how technology may be a barrier to breaking free from an abusive partner. Through technology the abuser continues to stay in contact with the victim through social networking and cell phones (e.g. text messages) so that the victim feels trapped. This research sought to reveal the degree to which such use of technology is a problem. Previous research on why women are slow to leave abusive relationships has focused on women not acknowledging that they were abused, minimization and normalization of abuse (Edwards et al. 2012), high levels of perceived risks (Amanor-Baodu, et al. 2012), social/cultural influences (Baly, 2012), minimal use of self talk to keep in motion (Patzel, 2012), and being married/having more adults in the household (Lacey et al. 2012). No previous research has focused on how technology in the form of receiving text messages/emails/Facebook postings from an abusive partner slows the process of leaving an abusive partner. Questionnaire and Sample A 42 item questionnaire on "Technology as a Barrier to Leaving an Abusive Relationship" was approved by the Institutional Review Board at a large southeastern university and posted on the Interact.
