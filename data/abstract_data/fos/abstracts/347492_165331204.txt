A lady from an aristocratic family of Siena, a sister of the author’s noble friends, appears here as an unapproachable dame of chivalric poetry, hardly accepting due tribute to her. This way of describing Angela’s beauty is rooted in Ovid and Propertius, but the love story, Marrasio’s attitude toward her, and the violence of feelings borrow from troubadours’ poems rather than from Roman elegy. The next decades of the genre’s development bring more classical versions of the elegiac cycles, but this mixed tradition is still present in later Latin poetry, e.g., Pietro Bembo’s elegiac verses to princess Lucrezia Borgia. Reading Marrasio, we look forward to the next volumes of the I Tatti series that will certainly help scholars in their research of the Neo-Latin Renaissance.
