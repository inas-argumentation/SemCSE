Mercedes Cisneros de Petón, married for  years to a man of high society, testified to being forced to eat dinner alone in the kitchen and not having enough money to present herself appropriately in public, forcing her to borrow money from her servants. Cisneros was granted her divorce, on the grounds that this humiliation damaged her social standing. Despite cases like these, over the course of the s divorce increasingly became a route that more men than women took. Smith concludes that while, especially for elite women, divorce proceedings could be empowering, women often fought not only their husbands but also their husbands’ networks. It was particularly damaging for women when these networks included local officials who could help sway the case. Thus, as much as individual women used the courts to redress abuse and inequality in the domestic sphere, in practice liberalised divorce ‘further eroded women’s position in society by enabling men to divorce their wives and leave them with little means to support themselves and their children’ (p. ). The government’s efforts to reform the foundations of family life brought about a clash with the Catholic Church, as Governor Salvador Alvarado initially sought to purge the Church of employees and spaces, banishing priests and converting churches into museums or the homes of municipal bands. Meanwhile, printing presses churned out tracts and articles that ‘ideologically linked a modern country with a secular household freed from traditional religious ties’ (p. ). But throughout, Yucatán women defended their churches and their right to attend them, at times successfully negotiating with the state for the return of seized churches or church property. In Ticul a group of  women asked the local military commander to unlock the city’s main church, which he agreed to do (perhaps having already received orders to do so); the women left the meeting with the church keys in their hands. But the fireworks of the conflict must not overwhelm the ways in which Church and state found common ground, as Smith makes clear, especially in their shared views of women as guardians of morality and future generations. While revolutionary Yucatán has a reputation as the ‘laboratory’ of revolution and a hotbed of feminist reforms, Smith’s carefully researched and well-written monograph shows that ‘despite the volume and variety of... liberal laws, most of them failed to challenge entrenched notions of patriarchal privilege and instead left women without equal rights or status’ (p. ). She also provides us with a unique and nuanced view of the values, concerns and daily struggles of these Yucatecan women. Focusing on how women used the opportunities of the revolution to improve their lives, Smith concludes that through taking cases of injustice to court and through direct political action, women began to make changes that would help create opportunities for future generations. In that way, perhaps, these women really were the guardians of the future.
