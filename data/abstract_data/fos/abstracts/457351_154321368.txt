The Joint Chiefs of Staff: From Service Parochialism to Jointness
The Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) is responsible for managing the U.S. armed services. It represents the interests of the military and participates in the national security policy-making process. The JCS can facilitate or harm an administration's pursuit of its national security policy objectives. Above all, the relationship between the Joint Chiefs and the president is the central link in civil-military relations in the United States. Since World War II, the JCS has been the institution to manage and coordinate the separate military services. Interservice rivalry has been endemic to the armed forces of the United States. C. Kenneth Allard persuasively argues that in coping with the challenges that military operations on land, sea, and air have posed for America's armed services over their entire histories, each branch of the military developed its own entrenched and distinctive "strategic paradigm."1 In a similar vein, Carl H. Builder asserts that "the most powerful institutions in the American national security arena are the military services-the Army, Navy, and Air Force-not the Department of Defense....." and that each of these institutions ". . . ha[s] distinct and enduring personalities." These service identities and personalities make them almost impervious to change from the outside, Builder argues. Hence their continuing autonomous and selfinterested behavior.2
