JON EISENSON, Ph.D. Sanford University School of Medicine The Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT) was designed “...to provide a well standardized estimate of a subject’s verbal intell igence through measuring his hearing vocabulary” (Dunn, 1959, p. 25.) The test calls for a motor, rather than a verbal, response: children are asked to point to the correct picture when the examiner names it. This procedure eliminates the need for the child to use words, yet permits a measurement of his understanding of them. This is a definite advantage when testing children with speech problems. Findings with the PPVT have been correlated with several full-scale intelligence tests. Preliminary validity studies reported by Dunn indicate high correlations between the PPVT and other tests given to several different groups of children, including some classified as educably retarded, trainable, and cerebral palsied (Dunn & Brooks, 1960; Dunn & Harley, 1959; Dunn & Hottel, 1961). Recent studies have sought to determine the relationship between the PPVT and intelligence tests such as the WlSC and the Stanford-Binet (Burnett, 1965; Hughes & Lessler, 1966; Koh & Madow, 1967; Shaw, Matthews, & Klove, 1966). Their evidence suggests that, although the PPVT may be particularly sensitive to language development, it may be used as a screening device for assessing intelligence. However, the results of studies with children enrolled in classesfor the educably mentally retarded (Brown & Rice, 1967; Carr. Brown, & Rice, 1967; Rice & Brown, 1967) caused the investigators to seriously question the validity of the PPVT as an estimator of intelligence.
