Giles of Viterbo: A Reformer's Thought on Renaissance Rome
ILES of Viterbo (Egidio da Viterbo, 1469-1532) has been receiving increasing attention as his place in early sixteenth-century intellectual and religious history becomes clearer: he combined a central and effective position in ecclesiastical administration with an active role in the leading scholarly and literary circles of the late Italian Renaissance.1 As prior general of the Augustinian friars from 5o8 until 1518 he undertook from Rome a vigorous reform of the order. He was a trusted adviser of Pope Julius II, under whose powerful patronage he had been elected head of the Augustinians, and he enjoyed a very cordial relationship with the two Medici popes, Leo x and Clement vii, the former of whom created him a cardinal in 15 I7. Giles opened the Fifth Lateran Council (II52-I7) with an insistent call for reform of the Church, and in this inaugural sermon he enunciated his well-known norm for authentic ecclesiastical renewal: 'Men must be changed by religion, not religion by men.'2 During the council he and Cajetan (Thomas de Vio), the superior general of the Dominicans, fought side by side to defend the privileges of the mendicants from the attacks of the bishops, and after the council Giles was sent by Leo x as papal legate to the court of the young King Charles I of Spain, the future Emperor Charles v.
