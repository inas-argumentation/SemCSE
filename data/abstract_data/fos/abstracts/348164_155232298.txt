Military Spending, Technical Change, and Economic Growth: A Disguised Form of Industrial Policy?
With the publication of Robert Reich's two highly influential books, The Next American Frontier and Minding America's Business (with Ira Magaziner) the idea-long current in heterodox economic thoughtthat military spending has had as much to do with the industrial needs of the U.S. economy as it has with "defense" (if not more so) received its first popular presentation.' Largely because of Reich's straightforward and vigorous presentation, an idea that verged upon heresy but a few years ago now has gained broader adherence. Military spending is now occasionally understood to have a logic that transcends the more limited and purely strategic realm of militarily defined pursuits. Reich maintains that the United States "has an irrational and uncoordinated industrial policy," dominated in its scope and impact by activities that are putatively labeled "defense."2 This article seeks to explore the question of the relationship between military spending, technical change, and economic growth beyond the level of popular discourse that has dominated much of the discussion of this matter to date.
