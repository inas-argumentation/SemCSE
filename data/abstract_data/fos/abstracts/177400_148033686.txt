The present volume does not advance the theoretical discussion of resistance beyond these historiographic hallmarks, notwithstanding different authors' citations of newer works drawn from Latin American, Asian, and African literatures. Susan Schroeder stresses the diversity of theoretical viewpoints; yet, taken as a whole, this volume verges on eclecticism, ranging from moral economy to revitalization and nativism without approaching a new synthesis or conceptual approach to questions of resistance, colonialism, and indigenous-Iberian confrontations in Spanish America. The phrase "Pax Hispanica" is not compared with Tristan Piatt's notion of a "colonial pact" or with the variegated meanings of hegemony current in both colonial and modern studies. Even within the book, the two general essays do not juxtapose the authors' separate points of departure. Does Deeds's notion of "first generation rebellions" have comparative value for Oaxaca or Yucatan? Are resistance and rebellion categorically similar? Is the emphasis on "culture" and religion, desaggregated from material grievances, warranted as the interpretive entrada to the complex thickets of native resistance?
