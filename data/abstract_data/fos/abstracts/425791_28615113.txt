Psychology, Medicine and Collaborative Innovation: An Appreciative Reply to Kirch and Ast
Comments by Kirch and Ast (2017) are a welcome invitation to psychologists from the President of the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) that asks psychologists to participate actively in the evolution of Academic Health Centers (AHCs), as these centers confront new challenges posed by transformations in the US health care system (Kirch & Ast, 2017). Moreover, the authors go a step further; they invite psychologists to be not only participants but also to assume leadership roles in AHCs. The authors’ invitation is not the first time in the history of psychology’s role in AHC affairs that the AAMC leadership has extended a friendly, supportive invitation to psychologists, especially those working in medical schools and AHCs, which are the home base of academic medicine. Thirty-five years ago, psychologists employed in these settings formed a professional association to better represent and define themselves as participants in the AHC community and the world of academic medicine. That organization was the Association of Medical School Psychologists (AMSP), today known as the Association of Psychologists in Academic Health Centers (APAHC). They sought to establish their presence, and define themselves in relation to medicine, and also in relation to their own discipline, psychology. From its beginning in 1982, the founders of the AMSP realized that the organization faced many challenges, among them the necessity of educating both the American Psychological Association (APA) and the AAMC as to the increasingly important role psychology was playing in medical education and practice. Toward that end, the AMSP founders established annual meetings in conjunction with both APA and the AAMC and began negotiations with both organizations for formal affiliation. In 2000, AMSP became Section VIII of Division 12, Clinical Psychology, establishing their formal connection to psychology. Beginning with the early efforts of President Robert Thompson, and succeeding presidents, especially Nate Perry, affiliation with AAMC proved more challenging. After almost two decades, however, in 2002 the AMSP was admitted as a member of AAMC’s Council of Academic Societies (CAS; Hong, Leventhal, & Seime, 2005). That significant event came about in response to the initiative of AMSP President Barry Hong and an invitation from AAMC President Jordan Cohen to Hong and AMSP Secretary Christine Carrington to attend a meeting of CAS. There, Hong made an informative presentation to a CAS membership that was largely unaware of the number of psychologists who were on the faculties of medical schools, principal investigators on NIH and NIMH funded research studies, active members of medical staffs, and who held appointments to senior administrative faculty posts in medical schools (e.g., Deans, Vice Chancellors, Institute Directors, Chairs, Division Heads, etc.). In response, CAS voted AMSP in as a CAS member organization (Hong & Robiner, 2016) Today, AAMC once again is sending an invitation from the President of AAMC to psychologists to assume leadership roles in the ongoing evolution of AHCs (Kirch & Ast, 2017). As these authors point out, AHCs must cope with major transformations in the US health care system.
