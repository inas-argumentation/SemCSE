The Consequences of the Euro
euro is finally a reality. Referenda, financial market crises, recession, deteriorating budgets, painstaking negotiations, political horse-trading, and the sheer immensity of the task threatened to wreck the project at various times over the past seven years. Yet the euro weathered these storms and has become the common currency of eleven of the fifteen member states of the European Union (EU).1 Already the euro embraces an economic market roughly the size of the United States. As more current EU members adopt the euro and as more countries join the EU, the size of this market and the euro's relative importance in international economic relations are likely to rise. The euro is certainly a political accomplishment for the governments of the EU's member states.
