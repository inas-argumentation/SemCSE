CPAs vs. Operating Managers: How to End the Conflict
The traditional antagonism between financial staff and line managers can be reduced if each side invests time in understanding how its counterpart works. Allan M. Rabinowitz, CPA, professor of accounting at Pace University in New York City, tells how to get started. It's no secret that in many companies the relationship between staff CPAs and operating managers is characterized by conflict and suspicion rather than cooperation and trust. It's also no secret that this divisiveness is destructive to both the employer and the accounting profession. It doesn't have to be so. In my 25 years in industry, I've worked both as a financial manager and as an operating manager, so I believe my view of the conflict is balanced. My goal in this article is not to blame one side or the other but, rather, to suggest ways to overcome the conflict by examining its causes. THE NUMBERS BARRIER Generally, operating managers have had little or no formal financial education. This largely is due to the current state of college curriculums, in which arts and science majors are inadequately prepared to understand financial systems. As a result, many otherwise successful operating managers have a fear of numbers and a resistance to dealing with them. Such an attitude creates significant barriers in dealing with financial people. In addition, operating managers, because of their leadership roles, often are strongly opinionated, particularly after experiencing some success in a business venture. They often think, "Why should I listen to financial people? I can make the financial decisions on my own; certainly they are easier to make than all the correct operating decisions I made before." Managers who think this way are not pleasant to deal with. Many insightful operating managers have found, after years of work, that true wisdom is recognizing how little they really know relative to all there is to know--even in their areas of expertise. TOOLS OF TOP MANAGEMENT Operating managers often mistrust financial people, seeing them as tools of top management. Moreover, they often are perceived as acting mainly to advance themselves by displaying their value to top management. While this certainly happens, it is unjust and destructive to broadly attribute such motives to staff CPAs as a group. Once this negative perception takes root, operating managers are reluctant to trust a financial person, no matter how well intended he or she may be. Top management invariably contributes to this distrust by using data gathered by financial people to serve as ammunition in confrontations with operating managers. As a result, operating managers often think it's a waste of time to hold planning meetings with the financial people. OVERCOMING RESISTANCE TO CHANGE Complicating things is our tendency to resist change. After all, change creates unknowns, lessens our ability to control and, at least initially, may cause us to work longer and harder until we get used to a new procedure. But financial people over the years have become messengers of change: They like to look at different approaches and alternatives, to set priorities, to study cost-benefit relationships and rates of return and to forecast and develop updated action plans to stay on budget. In response, operating people assert, "It's easy for financial people to suggest change. We have the hard job of implementing it." Not only does this response have some merit, but now, with computers, the financial people also can work up new scenarios with ease, adding even more strain to the relationship. Many financial people get lost in the details and the accounting procedures--ignoring what the operating people consider the big picture. Also, they often fail to identify sufficiently with their employers.
