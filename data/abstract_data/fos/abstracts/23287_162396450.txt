Renaissance commentators (including the unknown author of the Morbisanus text) marveled at the Great Turk’s supposed emulation of Western Civilization: he had Greek and Latin histories read to him; he wished to surpass Alexander; he wanted to avenge his “Trojan” ancestors; he dreamed of conquering Italy. How true was any of this? Ottoman historians believe Mehmed II was more concerned with taking on the older and more culturally prestigious Islamic dynasties to his east and south. Certainly most of the architectural projects the Ottomans sponsored in Istanbul, Bursa, and Edirne reflect the young dynasty’s insecurity with respect to the great powers of the Muslim East rather than emulation, cross-fertilization, or “dialogue” with the Renaissance courts of the Christian West. To the early Ottomans, Europe represented not much more than a strategic place to consolidate power and muster resources between attacks on the heartlands of Islam. But, with a peculiarly narcissistic horror, Westerners liked to imagine that Mehmed thought about them constantly. The image of the Ottoman sultan as a sort of sinister paragon of the ideal Renaissance prince — cultured, rich, energetic, a generous patron of arts and letters, the only prince in Europe who actually listened to his humanist advisors — was a source of morbid fascination. This book follows in much the same vein: of course the Muslims did everything just the way Christians did; only they did it better. The same self-negating, yet ultimately self-regarding Eurocentrism that shaped many Renaissance views of the Islamic world seems also to animate this judgmental essay. MARGARET MESERVE University of Notre Dame
