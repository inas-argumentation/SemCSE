Research in Labor Problems in the United States
Research in Labor Problems in the United States. By Milton Derber. New York: Random House, 1967. viii, 184 pp. $2.45. Both the novitiate and the experienced researcher can profit by reading Derber's deceptively modest and thoughtful ittle treatise on research in labor problems. The author has managed to cover, in this paperback which is one of a series of labor studies, many of the most significant issues facing researchers concerned with labor problems. After considering the subject area as a possible identifiable discipline, Derber points out that it cannot yet be regarded as a separate social science discipline. He correctly concludes that the subject of labor problems is a field of knowledge in which all of the social sciences as well as law and engineering meet. Derber may be a bit too harsh on his fellow researchers when he compares lack of achievement in developing theoretical principles with the progress of their counterparts in the physical and natural sciences. Both the inherent problems in studying highly volatile and dynamic social conditions and the relatively short period of time that has been given to this effort, as compared with the long experiences in the physical sciences, tend to hamper the development of a scientific body of knowledge in labor economics. Young scholars in this field will find Derber's book useful in learning how labor research developed-and why it took that course. They will be exposed to some of the early leaders in the field and learn how labor economics evolved after World War II. Unfortunately, the author does not carefully differentiate in the period of the early 1960's between the concern for manpower, expressed through legislation calling for a better matching between workers and jobs through retraining, and the problem of poverty. He thus underestimates a significant change in thinking which took place at legislative and executive levels. The contribution of the researchers who carefully unraveled the poverty skein and the policy makers who brought the issues to the surface of public knowledge deserves more consideration than the author was able to give in his abbreviated summary. Academicians who are not participating in policy decisions in the government face severe handicaps in discussing research as an instrument of administration. The apparent monolithic iceberg of administrative decisions obscures the fact that some of these decisions are based on ignorance, wisdom, seat-of-pants intuitions, logical conclusions based on facts, and current and obsolete knowledge. Because he is on the outside looking in, Derber's chapter on "Research as an Instrument of Administration" suffers from a lack of exposure and insight. Governmental and academic researchers have, for the most part, been unable to make greater contributions to administrators because of the difficulty, even in our longterm thinking, of anticipating problems and mounting projects which will provide the information needed for intelligent decision making. Derber's suggestion that, "In order to be sensitive to the problems of public and institutional life, academicians should be in constant touch with government agencies, unions, and employers-as consultants, as temporary employees, as contractual or invited researchers" can only be greeted with a hearty "Amen" by governmental administrators. Until this cooperative effort between governmental and academic researchers is strengthened, government personnel will continue to be plagued by the issue of how the results of research projects which they receive can best be applied and utilized.
