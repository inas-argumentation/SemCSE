Fashioning a Prince for All the World to See: Guaman Poma's Self-Portraits in the Nueva Corónica
One of the most famous manuscripts to survive from the Spanish colonial Americas is El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno (The First New Chronicle, and Good Government). The title is usually abbreviated, as it is here, to Nueva corónica. The indigenous Andean author, Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala (c. 1535–c. 1616), was nearly 80 years old when he completed the work in 1615.1 Written in Peru in Spanish, Quechua, and Aymara, it consists of 1,189 pages with 398 full-page ink line drawings, produced in the form of a letter to Philip II (r. 1556–1598), who was the Spanish king when Guaman Poma began drafting it, and later to Philip III (r. 1598–1621). While Guaman Poma also intended the Nueva corónica to be published “for all the world” to see, he makes his intent to address the king explicit through an illustration titled “His Majesty Asks, the Author Responds.”2 The illustration depicts the author kneeling before Philip III, offering a book and gesticulating with his free hand (Figure 1).3
