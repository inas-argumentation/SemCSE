In methodological terms, this is an unusual study of dramatic representations of selfslaughter in just how focused it remains on interpretations of character and action, and how little time is spent on larger period conceptions of suicide, which are almost exclusively encountered secondhand. Although the study opens with “A Brief History of Suicide,” historical sources or other Renaissance voices—Sym, Donne, Burton, Capel, Denny, and so on—are thereafter either unmentioned or unquoted, while Shakespeare’s dramatic contemporaries and literary influences remain effectively offstage. Despite the suggestive claim that “suicide remained controversial, which made it a tricky but simultaneously productive topic for any playwright to engage with” (8), only Kyd is mentioned. There are, in Du Bartas’s unheard words, “burning Coales and Cordes, / Aspes, Poysons, Pistols, Haltars, Knives, and swords” (trans. Sylvester, 1605) being wielded throughout so many early modern literary and nonliterary texts that it seems a missed opportunity to have incorporated so few. Indeed, as this book has compelling things to say about how Shakespeare may challenge period assumptions and attitudes, it would have been rewarding to see Tronicke in detailed interaction with their specifics. Early on, Tronicke asserts that, having summarized a trajectory of attitudes toward suicide from ancient Rome to the early modern with reference to key players such as Seneca, Plato, Augustine, and Donne, attention will now turn to “more pertinent questions with regard to compelling drama or dramatic characters”: “I consider,” the author continues, “these questions of philosophical contemplation, theological denunciation, social stigma, and legal persecution mere historical background information” (8). This is a provocative move but does indeed allow this engaging and thesis-led study to turn its attention to character analysis, audience response, the play text, and its performance.
