With such a collection, therefore, we are inevitably limited to asking what has been included and what omitted, and, overall, to commenting on what the collection tells us. On the former, most of the key speeches (‘key ’ because they marked a turning point or tell us something about the evolution of Castro’s thought or of the Revolution’s trajectory) are included, which is virtue enough in this essentially reference work. These include his 1953 defence speech, ‘History will absolve me’ ; the April 1961 declaration of socialism and the June 1961 Palabras a los intelectuales, thankfully with the correct wording of the (in)famous phrase ‘Within the revolution, everything ; against the revolution, nothing ’ ; the Second Declaration of Havana (1962) ; and several other notable speeches from 1965 to 1971 (not least the announcement of Che’s departure from Cuba and Cuba’s commitment to armed struggle in Latin America, made at OLAS). However, there are curious gaps. The First Declaration of Havana (1960) is omitted, for example, although, when compared to its successor two years later, it demonstrates well the Revolution’s journey from passionate ideological uncertainty to a more clearly anti-imperialist stance. Likewise the December 1961 ‘I am a Marxist-Leninist ’ speech, which, if included, could have helped to settle an argument once and for all. Neither of his significant speeches against ‘ sectarianism’ (against the PSP and Escalante in March 1962 or against the ‘microfaction’ in 1968) are included, which is regrettable, as they do indeed tell us much about the Cuban ‘heresy ’ of those years. Then there is nothing between 1970 and 1979, except for his farewell speech in Allende’s Chile ; hence no 1970 zafra speech (admitting failure), and no speech from the notorious 1971 Education and Culture Congress. Conversely, however, what is included beyond the ‘canon’ is often useful : for example, the 1986 ‘ rectification ’ speech and a 1999 speech in Venezuela foreshadowing the post-2000 ‘Battle of Ideas ’. After 2005, however, complete speeches give way to rather frustratingly short extracts, most notably from his November 2005 speech to Havana students, when he warned of the danger to the Revolution from within, from complacency and the corrosive effects of neoliberalism. The overall result is classic Ocean Press material : a very useful reference work (not least in its compilation of such material into one vast volume), adding to our documentary stocks on Cuba, but unfortunately adding little in the way of analysis of the Revolution. Taken together they tell us more about the 1960s than any other period, but – not least because the more orthodox 1970s and 1980s are largely ignored – curiously highlight the consistency in Castro’s thinking and the Revolution’s development over the five decades, which, given perceptions of his inconsistency, is no bad thing. Overall, it adds to our knowledge but, as ever, leaves us to infer analysis and interpretation.
