The latter focus, emphasized in the introduction’s thoughts about “comparability,” “doubling,” and “palimpsestic readings” as responses to “asymmetrical power relations,” was the theme of a precursor event held at Johns Hopkins University in 2009: “There Is Only One World: Garcilaso in Dialogue with Today’s World-Making.” Although more could have been made of the tension between Garcilaso’s intentions and how they are conceptualized today, this dual focus is a strength of the collection. It is nevertheless unfortunate that each contribution appears to have been published much as it was received, with little if any editorial revision. Each has its own list of references and its own manner of documentation, leading to inconsistencies in names, titles, dates, quotations, and translations. Lexical, grammatical, and syntactic errors also abound, together with more serious faults that suggest some authors’ indirect knowledge of sources, such as the confusion of Luis de Granada and Luis de León, or the claim that poetry—Aristotle’s term for fiction—signifies “the kind of writing we generally refer to today as ‘literature’” (316). Amid such problems, three essaysmerit commendation. The rigorous, well-documented analyses by José Antonio Mazzotti, Margarita Zamora, and James W. Fuerst not only show that one can foreground Garcilaso’s affirmation of mestizo and Amerindian concerns without denying the depth of his integration in European and Christian culture, but also prove that this critical act can be expressed in terms that Garcilaso himself could understand, that is, without the arcane jargon of postmodern theory.
