The Liberal Matrix: Pluralism and Professionalism in the American University
It has been a commonplace, at least since God and Man at Yale, that the American university is a liberal institution [8]. We might not have appreciated, however, that it is a liberal institution because it is a characteristically modern institution. As Benjamin Barber has remarked, "liberalism has been the political philosophy of modernity. It has celebrated modernity's victoriesemancipation, science, tolerance, reason, pluralism, rights and it has been diminished by modernity's vices alienation, deracination, nihilism, meaninglessness, anomie" [2, p. 177]. In our own day, it appears that the American university has become something of a test case for modernity's intertwined commitments to rational inquiry and democratic culture, for the impassioned arguments of late over higher education reveal an institution simultaneously defended as the embodiment of modernity's achievements and excoriated as the repository of its vices. Stated otherwise, the recent debates about university curricula represent yet another example of how our "grand narratives" are steadily weakening [28]: the rejection of the canon and the countercharge of nihilism represent different attitudes toward the same postmodern condition. Liberal theorists face a similar predicament. On the one hand, the late-modernist program of autonomous individuals, social sciences, competitive markets, and bureaucratic states continues to offer an immense apparatus for better approximation of the ideals of truth and freedom; on the other hand, each element in this program is becoming increasingly discredited within liberalism itself.
