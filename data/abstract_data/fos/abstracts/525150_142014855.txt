The Structure, Objectives, and Benefits of a Citizenship-Centered, Community-Building Student Judicial System
The fundamental concepts of a citizenship-centered, community-building student judicial system are presented. The system's cornerstone is the random selection of students, faculty and staff for service on judicial hearing boards. Consequently, the entire university community, rather than just a select group, is eligible to receive the educational, community-building, and citizenship-enhancing benefits of hearing board training and membership. Introduction Since the founding of the United States, citizenship development has been one of the fundamental purposes of education (Ford, 1904; Terenzini, 1994). For the past several decades, student affairs professionals have recognized the educational benefits of student self-governance in at least certain aspects of academe (Remley & Ruby, 1993). These observations have been confirmed by studies showing that participation in student leadership improves the citizenship skills of student leaders (Kuh & Lund, 1994). However, the principles of democratic self-governance which have entered into other aspects of student life have not been incorporated into campus judicial systems. This is particularly unfortunate since students view judiciary proceedings as the second most appropriate university component for student self-governance (Boyer, 1987). While students do sit on discipline committees at many universities, their selection process bears scant resemblance to accepted democratic selection processes outside of the Academy. Instead of using the American judicial model of being judged by a randomly-selected group of peers, American universities are encouraged to carefully screen and train judicial hearing board members (Cordner & Brooks, 1987). Consequently, even though student judicial boards can be powerful instruments for constructive student growth (Ardaiolo & Walker, 1987), these benefits are deemed best restricted to a privileged few. It should not be surprising that Letzring & Holcomb (1996) find that their ideal -- "a [student] conduct code should engender a sense of responsibility, not simply a following of the rules and regulations" (p. 74) -- is difficult to meet on contemporary college campuses. This paper presents a citizenship-based, community-building judicial system that makes the multifaceted benefits of campus judicial process participation open to the university community at large. Basic System Process We begin by briefly describing relevant structural and procedural aspects of the citizenship-based, community-building student judicial system (SJS) at Truman State University. (For more detailed information on system logistics, see: Northeast Missouri State University Dean of Student Affairs Office and Student Senate, 1994, from which much of this paper was adapted). Truman State University is a public, highly-selective, rural Midwestern university dedicated to undergraduate liberal arts and sciences education. About half of the university's students live on campus, and nearly all are traditional, full-time students. At the start of an academic year, a pool of seventy potential hearing board members is formed. Two mechanisms are used to select the fifty students, 10 faculty, and ten university staff (which includes administrators) that comprise the pool. First, those who were pool members during the previous academic year but did not sit on a hearing board are contacted and given the option to return to the pool. Second, the computer services office produces a randomly-selected list of students, faculty, and staff, drawn from the university community's entire membership. The Judicial Advisor (JA), who is responsible for administering the judicial system, uses this list to complete the pool's membership. When contacting prospective pool members, the JA explains the judicial system objectives and the responsibilities and time commitments of hearing board pool membership.
