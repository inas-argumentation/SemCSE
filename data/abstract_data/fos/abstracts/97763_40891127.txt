Giant panda protection: a time to abandon the concept that mainly relies on nature reserve
Giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) is a national treasure of China; to protect this iconic species, the Chinese government established 67 nature reserves within the last 50 years, covering 66.8 % of giant pandas and 53.8 % of its habitats (State Council Information Office of China 2015). This is a remarkable achievement in species protection, because it is rare for a country to establish such large number of nature reserves for the protection of only one species. As early as the 1960s, protection of giant pandas by establishing nature reserves has been increasing (Hu et al. 2011) and this has played a key role in giant panda protection. However, with the rapid increase of the number of nature reserve, this method is becoming debatable. For example, according to the latest report by the Sichuan Forestry Department, more than 3000 records of human disturbance were recorded in 87.0 % (40 of 46) of giant panda nature reserves in Sichuan Province (the province with the largest number of wild panda), and the encounter rates of grazing, herb gathering, tourism, evidence of the use of fires, and hunting inside nature reserves were all higher than those outside the nature reserves (Sichuan Forestry Department 2015). This phenomenon suggests there is a need for the re-appraisal of current policy. In China, the concept of protecting species by establishing closed protected areas, such as nature reserves and forest parks, is deep-rooted. It usually involves defining the scope of protection and active species protection, depending primarily on the allocated staff and the financial support received from government. The protection of giant pandas with a limited availability of staff, space, funds, and other resources, is a challenging task.
