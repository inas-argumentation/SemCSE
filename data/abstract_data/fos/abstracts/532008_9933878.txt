Look in the looking glass, not through it.
To the Editor: Lucinda Maine makes the palpable observation in her December 2008 AJPE Viewpoint, “Election 2008: Projecting the Winners,” that “real solutions” are needed for the present, less than optimal US healthcare system. Implicitly, solutions are needed to address the questionable quality of care received by patients in this country compared to healthcare in other economically developed nations and the limited access to covered services for a sizeable proportion of the population. Access to care is arguably an important quality measure that is basic to any healthcare system.1-3 
 
Insurers, pharmaceutical companies, and physicians are highlighted as those with the biggest financial stake in the system and have collectively presented barriers to finding real solutions for positive change. Groups with smaller financial stakes such as national pharmacy organizations may also be included in this category, but pharmacy students and individual pharmacists should be excluded. 
 
The first step in seeking real solutions for the system's ills and pharmacy's role in a rational system should engage a basic principle of the scientific method – an unvarnished literal interpretation of the historical and present reality while avoiding sound-bite evidence-free analyses that are based on political philosophies. 
 
As noted in the Viewpoint, the collapse of the financial system has changed the landscape for debate and opens the possibilities for real solutions for healthcare in this country and discussions of economically viable non-distributive roles for a much larger percentage of pharmacists in a different type of healthcare system. The collapse of the financial system should once again be a reminder that unregulated markets are not infallible. 
 
It is heresy to be critical of lightly regulated “free markets” and capitalism for fear of being an outcast or labeled as a “socialist” or worse. A rational dialog can now ensure about solutions, perhaps beginning with: “There has never been any such thing as a healthcare market in the traditional economic sense. What is the commodity produced in a market for healthcare?” 
 
A few examples may help stimulate some debate about seeking real solutions for the healthcare system. Richard Harris' The Real Voice chronicled Senator Estes Kefauver's 1959 Senate hearings that led to the passage of the Kefauver-Harris Amendments to the Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act, requiring for the first time evidence of efficacy in addition to safety testing before new drug are approved. The law was viewed by some as oppressive over regulation that would cost business. “Retail-drug associations” and Tennessee pharmacists opposed Senator Kefauver and pressured him to drop his investigation of the pharmaceutical industry during his1960 reelection campaign.4 
 
Devotion by national pharmacy organizations to free market philosophy (minimal or no regulation) has cost pharmacists dearly in an important professional role. The only pharmacist to be Food and Drug Administration (FDA) commissioner, Jere Goyan, PhD, proposed regulations that would have required pharmacists to distribute agency-approved written information that was consistent with a drug's approved professional product labeling with each new and refilled prescription.5 Opposition by the profession was ferocious. 6 Goyan envisioned pharmacists as patients' primary source for useful scientifically accurate drug information.7 To use the economic phrase, pharmacists would have had a monopoly on drug information for patients. Dogma intervened with abhorrence for FDA regulation and now pharmacy technicians stuff prescription bags with information leaflets that have consistently failed to meet voluntary minimum quality guidelines.8 Some have suggested that FDA-regulated written information be distributed at the point of prescribing, potentially bypassing pharmacy altogether.9, 10 
 
A reality check: PharmDs with nationally accredited residencies filling dispensing roles in corporate pharmacies. In a parallel universe, 5-year baccalaureate pharmacists (6 years with required experience for licensure) receive full salary and benefits for reviewing patients' drug profiles, counseling them, and interacting with prescribers. And the owner of the pharmacy receives $50.00 for each pharmacist-patient interaction from the government. Can this be reality?11 Pharmacists practicing in the northeast only need to travel to the province of Ontario and ask “why not in the US?” 
 
No, the Canadian healthcare is not a socialist system…it is an oligopoly, a single purchaser or payer system. The difference is that healthcare in Canada is a public service; here it is an enterprise that has implications for the practice of pharmacy. In a market driven system, the number of prescriptions filled per hour is the economic gage of success. Reviewing drug profiles, patient counseling, and a recommendation not to fill a prescription are economic liabilities. When healthcare is a public service, these activities are not economic liabilities but can improve the quality of pharmacotherapy while controlling or reducing drug and other associated costs. 
 
Comparative studies have consistently suggested that overall, based on objective measures, the highest quality healthcare provided in the US is by the Veterans Administration (VA) system.12,13 This healthcare system residing within the US meets the classical definition of a socialist system. Pharmacists' important contributions in non-distributive patient centered care in the VA system are well known. Until our market-based healthcare system is changed to a public service, patient care will remain profit-centered care. 
 
National pharmacy organizations have a vested financial interest in the status quo. They depend on revenues and support from hospitals, insurers, and pharmaceutical companies for their operations. To believe that these groups only function behind an influence-proof firewall and only in the best professional interests of members and patients is not a literal interpretation of reality—instead, it is looking through the mirror and seeing the healthcare market wonderland. 
 
 
Sana R. Sukkari, BSc Pharm, MPhil, PharmD
