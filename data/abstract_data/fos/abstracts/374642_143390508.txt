Using Multiple Outcomes to Validate Student Ratings of Overall Teacher Effectiveness.
Introduction Many reasons have been offered for questioning the validity of student evaluations of faculty teaching performance or for minimizing their importance in decisions on faculty promotion and tenure. However, judging by the views of those scholars who have most thoroughly considered these evaluations, including Centra [13], Cohen [16], Costin, Greenough, and Menges [19], Feldman [21], McKeachie [31], and Murray [37], and especially by the recent, comprehensive review of the subfield by Marsh [33], few of these objections represent important challenges to the validity of student instructional ratings. For purposes of personnel decisions about faculty, the objection probably most in need of further study pertains to the amount of unexplained, between-teacher variation that remains in mean student ratings of overall instructor effectiveness after discounting the variance shared with objective measures of student learning gains. Based on estimates provided by Cohen's updated meta-analyses [17, 18], this unexplained variation consists of about 80 percent of the total. Although some of this unexplained variance undoubtedly will be due to random error, there remains a lot of room for systematic bias. To cite a well-known possibility, simplistic interpretations of early "Dr. Fox" studies [for example, 39] suggested that raters were easily swayed by show over substance. For refutations of this interpretation in light of further research, see Abrami, Leventhal, and Perry [4], and also Marsh [33]. The present article: (1) proposes that a more comprehensive set of course outcomes, including measures of student motivation as well as student learning of subject matter, should be used to explain between-teacher variation in mean student ratings of overall instructor effectiveness; and (2) empirically estimates the extent to which variation in mean overall effectiveness ratings can be explained by a combination of several important outcome measures. It should be noted that Abrami, Cohen, and d'Appollonia [3] were struck by the scarcity of multisection validity studies that included multiple outcome measures. Political and Methodological Context Before proceeding we need to consider the political and methodological context of student evaluation of teaching in faculty personnel decisions. According to Marsh, "Nearly all researchers argue strongly that it is absolutely necessary to have multiple indicators of effective teaching whenever the evaluation of teaching effectiveness is to be used for personnel/tenure decisions" [33, p. 302]. While the use of alternative measures in addition to student evaluation of teaching is essential to estimate construct validity and usually strengthens the validity of assessments, it should be noted that summarizing across a set of multiple measures will actually attenuate validity under conditions where the measures are not closely enough fitted to the target construct [7, 29]. For example, if a measure of research performance were included among a set of criterion measures to estimate quality of teaching, it would introduce systematic but extraneous (biased) variance into a combined measure, because measures of research tend to share little or no variance with measures of teaching [20]. When we estimate construct validity, we focus on various aspects of the shared variance; but when we evaluate individuals with the same measures, each measurement still includes the variance that was ignored in the construct validity analysis. At best, the variance that was disregarded is randomly rather than systematically distributed. At worst, it produces measurements of teaching effectiveness that differ among faculty members as a function of extraneous factors rather than as a function of actual teaching performance. Peer or colleague evaluation is one possible component of a multiple-indicators approach to assessment of teaching.
