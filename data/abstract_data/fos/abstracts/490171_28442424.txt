Family background in prison groups: a comparative study of parental deprivation.
Offenders from two prison populations were examined. A sample of first offenders mostly charged or convicted with less serious crime and a sample of long term prisoners consisting of recidivists and those convicted of serious crime were matched with a control group derived from the population at large with respect to sex, age, marital status, socio-economic status and social isolation. Parental loss was common and statistically significant especially amongst the long term prisoners. The offenders had mostly been reared in an institution subsequent to the loss. Parental deprivation amongst the prison populations was the result of failure of infra-familial relationships leading to compulsory institutionalization; whereas among the normal population controls the parental loss was largely a passive process which led to being reared by the remaining parent.

While no differences were detected with regard to birth order and ordinal position, family size of the offender group was large, and an excess of male children in the families was noted. Those offenders who had experienced parental deprivation had younger parents and were themselves younger than their counter-parts who had suffered no loss. These results are discussed, especially with regard to the influence of institutions and contemporary family life on subsequent behaviour disturbance.
