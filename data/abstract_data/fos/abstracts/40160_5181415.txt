Conservation activities in Kaokoveld (north-west Namibia)
There are few areas remaining in Africa where the black rhinoceros is found outside national parks, game reserves or private ranches. One such area lies in the north west corner of Namibia where there are no electric fences or militaristic anti-poaching units, just small communities of people eking out a living by livestock farming. This fragile desert and semi-desert region of such dramatic beauty is also home to a number of other large mammals including elephant and giraffe which have specifically adapted, along with the rhino, to thrive in this harsh environment. In 1970 two national homelands were established in north-west Namibia, Damaraland and Kaokoland (Kaokoveld). Their creation involved the deproclaiming of 16000 km 2 of Etosha National Park and the loss of status of an adjoining game reserve, and the Directorate of Nature Conservation (DNC) lost all jurisdiction in the area. This was followed by a period of heavy poaching enhanced by the instability of Angola bringing in refugees and arms into northern Namibia, and by a severe drought where a lot of livestock and wildlife perished, forcing locals to resort to poaching. The trade in ivory, rhino horns and skins boomed, while the wildlife populations were decimated. The saddest prospect was the extinction of the desert-adapted elephant and rhino. In 1983, through Garth Owen-Smith, who had won the confidence of the local people, an Auxiliary Game Guard network was established. This system involved each community selecting its own game guard who would patrol the surrounding area and report any signs of poaching to the DNC, who by this time were recovering jurisdiction. Initially the main roles of the game guards were to involve the locals in conservation and build up a positive relationship with the DNC, who the locals thought owned all the wildlife. Due to the Ministry's lack of money and manpower they were prepared to try this radical method in combating such a huge problem. Despite the game guards only receiving a food allowance, the community's support could be attributed to the fact that they were now involved and given the responsibility of the wildlife. Today the Community Game Guard system is still functioning under the coordination of Garth Owen-Smith (Conservation Director of Integrated Rural Development and Nature Conservation, IRDNC WWF) who now pays them a wage and food allowance. Ironically, a number of the game guards are ex-poachers chosen by the communities for their tracking skills and other qualities necessary for patrolling the bush. It is not, however, only the patrols that are essential for wildlife protection: communication among all the people is vital if a stranger's footprints are observed in the vicinity by an individual it is not long before all the community knows. The predators that are a very important part of the ecosystem are, understandably, not tolerated so easily, and often when livestock is taken the animal responsible is tracked down and killed.
