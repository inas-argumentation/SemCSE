Sources of Stress among College Students--The Case of a Credit Transfer Program.
This exploratory study intends to identify the types and nature of stressor experienced by students in an American Degree Transfer Program at a private university college in Malaysia. Data collection was done by an open-ended questionnaire. The findings showed that the top five most frequently reported stressors were academic workload, too many tests, difficult courses, exam grades, and lecturer characteristics. Our findings suggest that the structure of the American education system probably elicit similar types of academic stressor in both the American and Malaysian setting. Each individual perceives and copes with stress in different ways. A person's response towards stress depends on whether an event is appraised as a challenge or a threat (Lazarus and Folkman, 1984). Challenging stimulus can lead to positive outcomes such as motivation and improved task performance while threatening ones or distress can result in anxiety, depression, social dysfunction and even suicidal intention. Research interest in college students' stress reflects societal concern on the effects of distress on health, academic performance, psychological and social adjustments so that appropriate intervention approaches can be done within the campus environment Purpose of the Study An initial approach in stress management is often an analysis of the stressors, or events that have caused stress (Holmes and Rahe, 1967). In this regard, this study will identify the events that will result in stress for students enrolled in an American Degree Transfer Program, which is conducted in a private university college in Malaysia. Exploring the pattern of stressors would provide an understanding of how the education system and cultural norms could affect personal and academic tasks in college. Literature Review Researchers have attempted to identify stressors on college campus that could potentially cause or exacerbate student problems. Chronic stress arising from daily hassles that deal with academic and social adjustments have been reported more often than negative life events. The most significant academic stressors reported were items that are time-specific or subject specific which supports Carroll's contention (1963) that learning is a function of time allowed, aptitude, quality of instruction and ability to understand instruction. As such, American college students frequently reported their significant academic stressors to be excessive workload, exams, grades, professor characteristics (fastpaced lecturers, those who gave unclear course objectives / assignments), and new responsibilities (Kohn and Frazer, 1986; Ross et al, 1999; Archer and Lamnin, 1985). These core academic stressors were found to be relatively unchanged over time, as observed by Murphy and Archer (1996) who compared the academic stressors of their previous study with those experienced eight years later (Archer and Lamnin, 1985). When the academic stressors among American and Chinese universities were compared, Li et al (2005) found the general pattern to be quite similar, but Chinese students ranked low learning efficiency and competition as top stressors while American students ranked examinations. Likewise Korean students also ranked grades and competition, and career and future success before academic demands and deadlines (Li et al 2005). This was attributed to the social pressure exerted on East Asian students in general to perform well academically and to succeed in their career. …
