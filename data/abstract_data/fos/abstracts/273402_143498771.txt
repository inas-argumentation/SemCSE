Jeff Passe’s excellent summary of the challenges facing elementary social studies educators in the present high-stakes testing environment seems to indicate that danger—perhaps even crisis—is in order. My response to Passe’s article will focus on invoking the optimistic paradox that the Chinese character for “danger” can also stand for opportunity, and note that in these rather dark times for elementary social studies educators, elementary social studies teachers still have much power to make positive change. Our commitment to do so will require us to jump into the testing fray, spend much more time with elementary teachers in the field—supporting their work, in terms of both content and pedagogy—and reexplore the possibilities for interdisciplinary instruction. Passe’s fifth recommendation, the call for the development of “authentic testing for elementary social studies,” is predicated on the notion that, for better or worse, testing today drives instruction. If we accept this equation (and I do), then the type of test given is enormously important. Therefore, the key word in Passe’s recommendation is “authentic.” Much of the antitesting literature focuses on the most dreadful (predominantly high-stakes secondary) tests that require rote-learned information from students. The most prominent opponents like Kohn and McNeil argue that these tests measure what matters least; that they focus on what is easiest to measure—efficiency—at the expense of what is more difficult to measure— effectiveness. Tests do not assess the skills and dispositions that matter most —the ability to carry out extended analyses, solve open-ended problems, and display command of complex relationships; in short, tests punish the thinking test taker. High-stakes tests tend to be the worst tests: multiplechoice, timed, and with dull “spit-back” essays. These tedious and predictable high-stakes tests produce teaching to the tests—when teachers’ knowledge of past tests informs their instructional emphases when reviewing for upcoming tests—also known as legal cheating. Kohn makes an effective analogy of giving patients aspirin and cold drinks right before their temperatures are taken. Test-driven instruction that takes place as a result of accountability reforms, then, may reinforce what the worst instructors have been doing; time spent preparing students for tests could be time spent encouraging critical-thinking activities.
