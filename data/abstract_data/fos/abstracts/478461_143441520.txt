The 1989 Presidential Election: Electoral Behaviour in a Brazilian City
The two and a half years of the Collor de Mello government were marked by so many dramatic events that the significance of the election which made him the first directly elected President in 29 years was not accorded the attention it deserved. A succession of events made the 1989 election results lose much of their interest for debate and study: the drastic economic stabilisation measures embraced by the Collor Plan, issued just after the inauguration of his government; the marketing campaign every time Collor appeared in the media; and, above all, the serious accusations of corruption involving his wife, relatives, close friends and Collor himself leading to his impeachment in December 1992. Nonetheless, that election was certainly one of the most important events in Brazil's political history, not simply because of its participatory and democratic character, but also due to the fact that, in the second round, it brought into dispute two candidates with quite singular careers. On the one hand was Fernando Collor de Mello, who emerged from being an unknown politician on the periphery of Brazilian politics, to the position of leader in the popular vote and winner of more than 3 5 million votes. On the other hand was Luis Ignacio Lula da Silva who, from being a metalworker and leader of the new union movement in the 1970s, ascended to the position of second electoral force in the presidential contest. Two candidacies which appear to generate a paradox: the first, victorious, without any party base or defined support in civil society; the second, the loser, anchored in a political party the Workers' Party (PT) with a relatively clear programmatic-ideological profile and characterised by relatively strong links with the social movements.
