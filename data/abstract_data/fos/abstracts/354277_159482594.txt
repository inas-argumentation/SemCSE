The second third of the book explores Elizabethan joint-stock companies, and considers how the commercial corporation impinged on the early modern political thought and practice. Turner’s discussion of the Elizabethan playing companies, and Shakespeare’s company and plays in particular, in chapters 5 and 6 offers new insights into the role of the theater and dramatic literature in imagining and modeling new political forms in the period. Chapters 7 and 8 move into the seventeenth century with extended discussions of Bacon’s and Hobbes’s respective views of corporate bodies as refracted through the premises of natural philosophy. The book closes with an extended coda reminding us of the utopian premise of the university, which as an “open unity” (xxi) potentially offers an instrument of critique and guidance for new understandings of political culture in our present moment. Throughout The Corporate Commonwealth Turner meticulously assembles a conceptual framework for regarding politics as a corporate enterprise, whose unity is always contingent and recombinatory. By considering those collectives that thrive below and beyond the scale of nation, we see how the actions of various polities supplement and exceed the state. Turner’s work unveils myriad, vital political forms that are flexible and ontologically disparate, and this rich legacy suggests the possibility of an open totality, that is, a network of associations that is ever more homogenous and perhaps inevitably more progressive.
