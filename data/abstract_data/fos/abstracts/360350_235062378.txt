While we may not be surprised that “The Passionate Shepherd to His Love” is included under the umbrella of the carpe diem theme, or even that Leander’s disquisition on virginity in “Hero and Leander” features prominently, that Doctor Faustus becomes such an important part of the discussion is both unexpected and illuminating. That play amplifies the lyrical compression of time as it enacts the sense of urgency conditioned by the summa brevis and the horror toward which all human beings careen, even those who have not sold their souls to the devil. Having had access to all knowledge and, on the carnal plane, to the body of Helen of Troy, Faustus encounters the ultimate limit. But then, as Hyman astutely demonstrates, he expresses what may be the quintessential human desire—namely, the desperate urge to supersede the limitations of mortality by asking for more time. As in the carpe diem tradition, this is the impossible desire.
