The English Land Market in the Eighteenth Century: the Case of Lincolnshire
T tHE work of Mr H. J. Habakkuk, which has done so much to illuminate the mechanism of the land market in eighteenth-century England and to inspire the revival ofinterest in the structure of landed society, has in fact been the subject of so little criticism that his suggestive hypothesis stands virtually as received opinion.' His argument is that "the general drift of property in the sixty years after i690 was in favour of the large estate and the great lord," which, while it did not prevent rich newcomers from entering the land market and buying out the ancient squirearchy, increasingly favoured the great magnates.2 A very active land market early in the eighteenth century increased territorial concentration, as the economic gap between gentry or small freeholders and the aristocracy widened. This was followed by a decrease in the volume of land sales, so that the great estates, swollen at the expense of smaller proprietors, maintained and even consolidated their position in the later eighteenth century. The political and economic oligarchy which thus developed protected their territorial possessions with strict settlements and enhanced their financial predominance in the countryside by exploiting the marriage market and by reorganizing estate management through their emphasis upon large capitalist farms. Prof. Mingay's findings of the much greater concentration of landed property in the hands of the aristocracy during the eighteenth century, after careful consideration of available data, have tended to corroborate Mr Habakkuk's thesis, and subsequent work has modified the details, not the profile, of the argument.3 It is, on the face of it, an eminently reasonable argument, which conforms with the principal trends of political development in the century or more after the Restoration as understood by the post-Namierian generation of English historians.4 However, the correlation between an active land market and the aggrandizement of the great estate is not everywhere as pronounced as appears in the studies mentioned above.
