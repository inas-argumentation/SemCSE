Anthropologists, literary scholars, cultural critics, and historians across the study of the Americas should find multiple aspects of this work engaging and useful. However, combining historiography, the study of memory, and the roots of religious practice with the history of enslavement, Saint-Domingue/Haiti, and the Black Diaspora can be daunting for the reader at times, particularly in such a short text. Stylistically, the predominantly nonlinear approach, provides snapshots of different religious beliefs, ancestral stories, and cultural practices, as well as the convergences between Haiti, Dahomey, and Kôngo, in ways that are methodologically engaging if occasionally practically difficult. Also, an expanded explanation of Vodou would be useful for the uninitiated. Still, there is much to be gleaned from this innovative work, both in its interdisciplinary scope and approach and its re-centering of Haiti and Vodou in the hi/stories of the Africa Diaspora.
