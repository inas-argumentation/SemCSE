Six Rules for Economizing in Public Higher Education: A Guide for State Legislators
T HE procreative binge of the Second World War has not yet slackened. Soon the war babies will be having babies of their own. The bambini who were born on university campuses in the late I940'S will crash the gates of the colleges in the middle I960's. Everybody knows that the state schools and the local public junior colleges will have to burst their seams to accommodate the inrush. College population will double within a decade in most states, and triple in a few, such as Florida, California, Nevada, and Arizona. This prospect generates gigantic headaches for state legislators, who find themselves urged to make large increases in annual or biennial appropriations of state-tax funds for operating expenses of state universities and colleges and to provide unprecedented sums of money for the necessary expansion of campuses and buildings, or in some cases for the construction of whole new college plants. In such a dilemma, it always helps to reduce the matter to rules of thumb as far as possible. This numbs the pain of thinking. As Henry Wriston says, "Formulas make decision easy. But hold! We would be unfair to Wriston if we did not quote his entire sentence: "Formulas make decision easy but rob it of wisdom."
