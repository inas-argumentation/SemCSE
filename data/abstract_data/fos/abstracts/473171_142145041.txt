Understanding Ethnocultural Differences Related to College Adaptation for First-Year Undergraduate Students
The number of ethnocultural minority students pursuing a college education is not only currently increasing but also likely to continue to increase over the years. As a result, university affairs professionals and college counselors have to continue refining their awareness, knowledge, and skills (Sue & Sue, 2004) to more competently work with a diverse student population. Since it seems much remains to be learned regarding the process of adjustment to college for ethnocultural minority students, the purpose of this study is investigate whether there are: (a) differences across ethnocultural majority and minority students on psychosocial variables related to adaptation, and (b) predictive relationships between psychosocial variables to adaptation for each group. Results indicated ethnocultural differences across perceptions of academic self-confidence, attitudes toward the university, barriers, ethnic identity, and P-E fit. Moreover, different adaptation models emerged for students based on ethnocultural group membership. ********** Although individuals from a diversity of backgrounds have formed this country for many decades, presently the population in the United States is becoming even more ethnoculturally diverse. By the year 2030, census projections estimate that the majority of the population in the United States will belong to ethnocultural groups other than European Americans (Sue & Sue, 1999). Additionally, by the year 2020, students who belong to today's ethnocultural minority groups will constitute 30 to 40 percent of all students enrolled in public schools (Sue & Sue, 1999). Thus, the number of ethnocultural minority students pursuing a college education is not only currently increasing but also likely to continue to increase over the years. Therefore, it is valuable to investigate college student adjustment, a variable that has been shown to predict whether or not a student chooses to leave the university (Martin, Swartz-Kulstad, & Madson, 1999) from the perspective of the ethnocultural minority student. College student adjustment has been defined as multifaceted, requiring adaptation to different domains of college life such as academic, social, personal-emotional, and institutional attachment (Baker & Siryk, 1986). Currently, the majority of students in most college campuses in the United States tend to be European Americans, thus those who belong to a minority ethnocultural group face unique social adjustment challenges (Kenny & Stryker, 1996). However, it is interesting to note that Tomlinson-Clarke (1998) found no significant differences between White and Black women college students on three (ie., Academic, Social, and Institutional Attachment) of the four dimensions of adjustment, as measured by the Student Adaptation to College Questionnaire (SACQ). However, somewhat surprisingly, the author found differences across Personal-Emotional Adjustment suggesting that Black female students, at a predominantly White university, seemed to display higher levels of "psychological and physical well-being and less general psychological distress" (Tomlinson-Clarke 1998; pp. 368) than White women. Hutz, Fabian, and Martin (2003) found that when perceptions of ethnocultural P-E fit (i.e., students perceptions regarding their ability to meet the university environment's needs, the extend to which the environment meets students' needs, and whether students feel a sense of being ethnoculturally different from or similar to the immediate environment) are equalized for minority and majority first-year students, minority students are adjusting at higher levels than their majority counterparts. Ethnocultural P-E fit may be an important variable to consider as student affairs professionals and counselors purport to increase minority students' perceptions of adaptation to college. Other psychosocial variables have also been linked to adaptation.
