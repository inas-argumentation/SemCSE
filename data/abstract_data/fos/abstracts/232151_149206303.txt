First Year Experience for At-Risk College Students
The first year of college (particularly the first few months) is a critical period during which students' attitudes, identities, goals, values, beliefs, adjustments, and future successes in higher education are most influenced (Rentz, 1988). Starting college is difficult for most students but those who are identified as at-risk for failing out in their first semester are even more challenged. Recognizing the importance of the first year specifically for at-risk students, we developed and implemented a unique First-Year Experience (FYE) class to support their success. Newly admitted undergraduate students at our university were identified as at-risk for failing out of college in their first year. The criteria for identification were high School GPA of 2.8 or lower (on a 4-point scale), and a combined SAT score of 800 or less. Because we considered these students vulnerable, we wanted to offer them a structured approach for academic success through our FYE class. The goals of the FYE class were to assist these students with the transition from high school to the university environment, to facilitate social engagement with other students, the lead instructor, and assigned peer advisors, and to promote effective learning skills and strategies. In addition, our FYE students were required to participate in at least three campus activities during the semester to promote a sense of community. Topics covered in the FYE class included: lime management, financial responsibility, and personal health and self-care. Practical application of the basic academic skills; such as, reading comprehension, note taking, and effective study strategies were also stressed during class time. In short, the underlying objectives of the FYE class were to help the identified at-risk first-year students adjust responsibly to the challenges of university life, and to succeed academically. Recognition for the impact and importance of FYE classes has long been acknowledged in the research (e.g., Barefoot, 1998; Cuseo, 2010; Shanley & Witten, 1990; Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005). Studies indicate that new students need support in managing their transition into the academic community, and adapting to the rigors of college life (Astin, 1984; Bean, 1990; McPherson & Schapiro, 2006). Results show positive correlations between participation in the FYE classes and various outcomes; such as, student retention (Braxton, Hirschy & McClendon, 2004; Titus, 2004), graduation rates (Bean, 1990), academic success (Barefoot, 1998; Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005), and social and academic integration to college (Astin, 1993; Kuh, 2003; Tinto, 2012). Many researchers have demonstrated that new students' non-cognitive factors are also influenced by participation in the FYE class (e.g., McPherson & Shapiro, 2006). These factors include: an increase in peer group interaction (Astin, 1984), participation in clubs and campus organizations (Carini, Kuh & Klein, 2006), a sense of belonging to the college community (Kuh, Kinzie, Schuh & Whitt, 2005), and frequent use of student support services (Martin, Swartz-Kulstad & Madson, 1999). These outcomes support Tinto's (1993) theory that student success is largely determined by a student's full integration and involvement in the academic and social communities of the institution. Successful studies have supported a particular format, structure, and design for FYE classes (e.g., Keup & Petschauer, 2011). We attempted to follow that design by keeping the class size small (Wilkie & Kuckuck, 1989), instituting a more student-centered focus to classroom discussions (Keup & Petschauer, 2011), and utilizing peer advisors as small group facilitators within the class (Hamid & VanHook, 2001). Peer advisors seem to provide a comfortable and non-threatening classroom atmosphere for new students.
