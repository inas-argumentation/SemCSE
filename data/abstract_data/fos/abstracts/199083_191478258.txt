Artists, No-Curators, And Other Intellectual Jargon: The International Cairo Biennale
ike Egyptian soap-operas or Egyptian TV commercials where everybody dances to oriental percussions, selling everything from mobile phones to margarine the International Cairo Biennale is unclassifiable according to any known existing biennale criteria. The Seventh Cairo Biennale arrived thisyear with a diversified yet somewhat incoherent selection of art and artists, few of which could be classified as interesting. The majority of the works did not rise to the "International" level of the title, and most of the speakers at the Biennale's Satellite Symposium, suggested by their presentation that talking about the art wasn't going to make it any better. Perhaps the most successful achievement for the organizers was that they managed to attract interesting names, such the notorious conceptual artist |oseph Kosuth; video/performance and conceptual artist Mona Hatoum (the only internationally-famous artist of Arab origin); and the Italian sculptor/painter Gio Pomodoro. Also in attendance was Nancy Spero, the official representative of the United States, who produced one of the only three sitespecific installations in the show. The others came from Kosuth and Hatoum. The Biennale hosted some 54 countries as well as other atists who were invited by the show's president. The arrangement of the works was done in pavillions distributed over the three venues (The Palace of Arts, The Museum of Modern Art and the Akhnaton Galleries), though some of the special guests, like Kosuth and Hatoum, were allocated specific sites at the Akhnaton Galleries, without regard to their nationality. Two days after the opening, the four-day-long Satellite Symposium began which presented some valuable dicussions from Adel El Siwi (Egypt), Beral Madra (Turkey), Gilane Tawadros (England/Egypt), Bedri Baykam (Turkey), Cornelia Lauf (USA), Salah Hassan (Sudan), Nancy Spero (USA), loseph Kosuth (USA), Ibrahim Salahi (Sudan), Olu Oguibe (Nigeria), CarmilloStrano (Italy), among a few others. Some of these talks were lost in a sea of recycled intellectual jargon delivered by invited local artists, critics, or pseudo-intellectuals, who were either overtly paranoid, daunted, terrorized by issues of globalization, or saw ethno-cultural vandalism in every action of the West. The prizes were no more successful or surprising. As one invited artist said "What doyou expect? At the Cairo Biennale they should be always written SUR-PRIZES!" The grand prize went to a work by a Palestenian artist, a gesture provocative to many Egyptian artists who claimed that there was a political drive behind the award. A piece by an Iraoj artist was granted two prizes, one Biennale prize and one of the special prizes donated by a private institution. According to a cultural official from a Mediterranean country, who decided to remain anonymous: "Why do they have to mix politics with prizes? We might all be provoked by what happens in lerusalem or Baghdad, but this does not justify attributing such prizes to such works. Mixing politics with prizes is like mixing potatoes with carrots!" Apart from the debates around prize and nominations and beyond the fact that the Biennale lacked any theme and any curator's touch (perhaps with the exception of Nancy Spero's project, and the slide-projection installation of the Swiss Heinrich LOber), the exhibition contained sporadic works by other lesser-known artists.
