The Soviet Union and the Persian Gulf
Much has happened in the Middle East and the Persian Gulf in the early 1980s. Jordanian King Hussein's decision not to negotiate the future of the West Bank and the failure of the Israeli-Lebanese agreement on withdrawal are only the latest in a series of events that have shaped the choices and behaviors of local actors and affected the positions of the superpowers in the region. The Shah of Iran's fall and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan created a sense of imminent danger about the Soviet threat to U.S. vital interests in the Gulf, but the Iran-Iraq war, the deterioration of Soviet-Iranian relations, and the conflict in Lebanon reassured many Americans that indigenous factors in the area would constrain the Soviets and limit the threat they could pose. Hussein's decision and the Syrian undoing of the Israeli-Lebanese agreement are seen by many as further proof that local political realities have a way of frustrating superpower objectives. But are the factors that constrain the Soviets real and enduring? Are those factors that so concerned the U.S. about the Soviet threat in the early 1980s no longer relevant or evident? Are the Soviets showing any less interest or determination in achieving their regional goals? Are they indicating in any way that they believe their strategy is not working and needs to be redefined or changed? Notwithstanding a number of setbacks and related difficulties in the region, the Soviets remain determined to pursue their regional goals and also remain generally satisfied with the strategy they have adopted for achieving these goals.
