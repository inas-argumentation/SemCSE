In both the groundwater and petroleum industries, wells must be drilled, completed, and produced, and a study has to be made of the flow of fluids through porous, permeable media for an understanding of the behavior of the wells themselves, as well as of the reservoir or aquifer as a whole. In some studies, the approaches used by the two industries in building up their understanding, or in solving their problems, are the same, with the minor exceptions of nomenclature, symbols, and units. In others, each industry has its own approach to the particular problem, and has developed that approach into a practical tool which may be employed with a considerable degree of success. It is thus instructive to examine in some detail the separate methods used to determine whether the differences are necessitated by real differences between water production on the one hand and oil or gas production on the other, or whether there might be an advantage in combining the approaches. Such a problem is that of defining and measuring those losses in head which originate within the formation adjacent to a producing well. The petroleum industry uses the skin factor, which is a measure of the degree of formation improvement or damage which results from the actual drilling and completion of the well. Little emphasis is placed on losses that are due to turbulent flow. In groundwater work it is usual to define a turbulence factor, and to try to isolate and measure the turbulent flow losses occurring in the neighborhood of the well bore. To this end, step-drawdown tests are run, and standard and simple methods for interpreting the results of such tests have been devised.
