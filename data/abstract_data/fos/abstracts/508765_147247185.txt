Machiavelli and Madison: Perspectives on Political Stability
One of the central concerns in the history of Western political thought-from Plato's early attempt to institute a state which because it would embody absolute truth would be impervious to all change to modern efforts to discover factors which tend to produce social stability-has been the founding of a stable and enduring political system. Despite this common goal there are obvious differences between Plato and, for example, Talcott Parsons. Plato's effort was avowedly normative-his state would permit, indeed assure, that men lead the good life in pursuit of truth and justice. Modem social theorists attempt to isolate those factors conducive to stability and to develop theoretical models of stable polities. Thus they posit certain functions that all societies, irrespective of their ideological goals, must perform in order to continue merely to exist. Such a position does not preclude the possibility that some cultural goal or "good life" can be achieved, but does suggest that the attainment of some degree of societal stability is a prerequisite to this achievement. If this is a fair representation of the general purpose of modern theorists, the two thinkers under discussion here can be viewed as quite "modern."
