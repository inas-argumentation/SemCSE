The Economy and the University: Economic Aspects of Declining Enrollments.
Those in higher education look nostalgically on the decade of the 1960s as the "Golden Age." Golden certainly in the sense that vast amounts of money were showered on the higher education industry by the federal and state governments, but also because the growing enrollment of students led to an almost unrestrained optimism about the future. As the 1970s began, the gold turned somewhat, but educators on the whole still predicted modest increases in student enrollment. In 1973, however, many institutions of higher learning found that even this guarded optimism was misplaced; that enrollment rather than increasing, albeit at a modest rate, was declining. The crunch has begun. Higher education now finds itself caught between declining enrollment on the one hand, and increasing demands for new and varied services on the other. Declining enrollments have had a number of immediate effects. First, in those schools, most notably state supported, where allocations are tied directly to FTE enrollment, faculty and staff must be reduced to compensate for fewer students.
