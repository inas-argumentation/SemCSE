For example, Long argues that the "passport represents a kind of archiving that acts on the body of the individual in an immediate and clearly-defined way" (77). The document represents the categorizing of an individual in terms of nationality and the clear fact that the person exists in the bureaucracy of a particular country. It enacts power by allowing or disallowing someone to travel across borders. Maps have their own form of power as they are "repeatedly linked in Sebald's work to state oppression, colonial exploitation and industrialization" (77). Mapping exerts control by making claims to land providing proof of ownership, even before political disputes over the territory are settled. Furthermore, newspapers exert their own disciplinary control in their very structure. Long argues that newspapers pose a "hermeneutic problem of relating individual events and pieces of information to the wider whole" (93). The prevalent role of newspapers in Sebald's work brings this dilemma to light. These three examples amount to disciplinary measures that Sebald calls into question. Long's research provides a unique contribution to the study of Sebald's work and pushes the field into new territory. By limiting the focus on the trauma of the Holocaust, Long allows for new terms to enter the discussion. The articulation of modernist themes comes through clearly in Long's reading and broadens the discourse by linking it to other contemporary themes. The argument serves as an opening in the study of Sebald's work for new concepts to shed fruitful light on one of the twentieth century's more enigmatic writers.
