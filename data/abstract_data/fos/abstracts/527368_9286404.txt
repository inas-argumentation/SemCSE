Commentary to "Evaluation in the United States pediatric urology workforce and fellowships: a series of surveys during 2006-2010".
The future of the subspecialty of Pediatric Urology depends on the ability to attract the best and brightest to our specialty every year. Surveys such as this one ask more questions than they answer, but the authors deserve accolades for bringing the dialogue to the forefront. Cooper, of the Leonard Davis Institute of Health Economics at the University of Pennsylvania, makes a cogent argument that specialty demand parallels economic growth. Cooper’s model predicted a shortage of 1000 physicians each year beginning in 2000 [1]. The major drivers for this include the aging physician population, differences in work hours as a greater percentage of women graduate from medical schools, the lifestyle demands of current and future generations of physicians, and modified career paths of newly graduated physicians. This will result in intensified competition to attract the best and brightest medical students into Urology and especially Pediatric Urology over the next two decades. From 1987 to 2003, we witnessed a gradual progression toward primary care in our pediatric urology clinic. We now see five to six children for each surgical case that we schedule. I believe this reflects a greater willingness by busy pediatricians to refer nonsurgical patients to the pediatric urologist. This means that the young resident we attempt to attract to our subspecialty manages a relatively large percentage of primary care patients.
