Equally important for these Royalist thinkers was the Jansenist critique of papal infallibility, which provided fodder for the Anglican effort to expose, and widen, rifts with the Roman communion, particularly for the English Catholics. However, Palmer is careful in his analysis of these English theologians and ministers not to overstate the significance of their connection to Jansenism. He rightfully avoids any attempt at an overly simplistic synchronization of the two theologies, in part by providing an insightful analysis of the Jansenist project unfolding in the 1650s. Palmer’s contribution to historical theology in Jansenism and England goes beyond identifying this formative influence in Restoration Anglicanism. In chapters on the Augustinian roots of Jansenism’s moral rigorism and a close study of Arnauld’s De la fréquente communion, Palmer acutely demonstrates how Jansenist moral theology was neither essentially Calvinist, Arminian, nor Tridentine Catholic. It was rather, according to Palmer, Thomistic, advocating something like “medieval tutiorism” (49). Both Anglicans and Jansenists wanted to avoid the potential moral laxity that could attend soteriological determinism. Both groups, from different theological directions, promoted a slow regeneration of the human will where, eventually, “the good” that individuals do “will not appear as the effect of an extrinsic cause, but as an expression of intrinsic freedom” (165). Palmer’s Jansenism and England adds to the growing list of excellent studies that identify bridges, theological and otherwise, across the Reformation rift between Protestants and Catholics. Palmer skillfully handles the works of Jansenists and Anglicans alike, careful not to exaggerate the connection between the two groups while, at the same time, setting out a compelling comparative theology.
