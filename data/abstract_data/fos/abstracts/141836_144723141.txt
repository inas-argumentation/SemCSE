Debates surrounding the law were dominated by fear on the part of centre-left and rightist politicians alike that recognising the Mapuche as a people would lead to ethnic strife and imperil Chilean national development. The resulting law recognised a watered-down national diversity, but did little to incorporate Mapuche participation into development planning. Haughney devotes the remainder of the book to an in-depth discussion of the two most emblematic conflicts between the Mapuche and the democratic Chilean state : the construction of the Ralco hydroelectric dam, which displaced scores of Mapuche-Pehueche families, and the intensification of commercial logging in ancestral Mapuche territory, which represents perhaps the most severe contemporary threat to small-scale agriculture, biodiversity, territorial sovereignty and the Mapuche way of life. Haughney’s analysis is based on fieldwork she conducted during eight years of residence in Chile (1992–2000). Though she conducted few interviews for the project and few details of her fieldwork methods are provided, Haughney aligned herself with a Mapucherun documentation centre, and painstakingly reviewed a plethora of primary and secondary documents. Despite the lack of formal interviews, Haughney makes certain that Mapuche voices are present in the text, mainly through excerpts from organisational documents and media interviews. Nevertheless, while Haughney provides an excellent account of current conflicts in Chile, the absence of interviews in which she asks her own questions means that the analysis sometimes doesn’t go much beyond a description of that situation and the official rhetoric of the actors involved. Although other English-language authors, including Florencia Mallon (in Courage Tastes of Blood : The Mapuche Community of Nicolás Ailı́o and the Chilean State, 1906–2001, Durham 2005) and myself (Pobladoras, Indigenas, and the State : Conflicts over Indigenous Rights in Chile, New Brunswick, 2004) have similarly pointed out the conflict between indigenous rights and neoliberalism in Chile, this is the first book length manuscript in English that is dedicated entirely to summing up the post-dictatorship contradictions faced by the Mapuche in Chile. In this sense, Haughney’s book fills an important void in the literature. This is an extremely useful text for scholars interested in indigenous politics as well as those concerned with the consequences of neoliberal policies more generally.
