Tradiiionally, i f parents are given any explicit role at all, i t is often onlyto seek counseling orpsychotherapy for themselves, in order thereby to help their child (Bijou & Sloane, 1 966). That teachers can effectively train and teach children with behavior excesses or deficits, is becoming a welldocumented fact. It has been possible to guide, or directly train, teachers to use operant conditioning procedures to modify aggressive (Brown & Elliott, 1965), isolate (Allen, Hart, Buell, Harris & Wolf, 1964), passive (Harris, Wolf & Baer, 1966), immature (Hart. Allen, Buell, Harris & Wolf, 1964; Harris, Johnston, Kelley &Wolf, 1964), and disruptive (Becker, Madsen, Arnold & Thomas, 1967; Zimmerman & Zimmerman, 1962) behaviors of children in the classroom. That parents can also be guided or trained to apply operant methods to produce changes in the deviant behavior of their child is receiving increasing recognition and study. In a general review of his work with schizophrenic and/or autistic children, Lovaas (1967), for example, stressed that generalization and persistence of positive behavior changes achieved through behavioral modification approaches are not likely unless the child’s parents learn to employ the training procedures in the child’s everyday life.
