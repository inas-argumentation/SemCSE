This is a remarkable achievement, in my view, given the complexity and layers of NATO politics. Through the authors’ description of the force-generation process, one immediately understands that NATO cannot just draw on readily available troops. NATO must solicit contributions from its members and partners. This process is cumbersome and time consuming, but it’s the price of doing business with allies and partners. Still, NATO has advantages that make alternative arrangements less attractive: it has a well-tested organizational capacity and it can rely on American capabilities, but it confers more legitimacy than US-led coalitions do. A final comment on the book is that it covers several mini case studies (the United States, France, Poland, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and New Zealand), providing a comparative lens that illustrates to the fullest the interplay between alliance pressure and domestic constraints, a two-level analysis that other recent books explore as well (Davidson 2011; Kreps 2011; von Hlatky 2013). Parliamentary and presidential regimes, in the end, might be the most difficult to predict when it comes to decisions on the use of force. In those political systems, much of the decision-making power resides in the hands of one or a few high-level people, which means we have to make assumptions about how risk-acceptant or risk-averse these individuals are. This is difficult to determine ex ante, and there is no easy way around that. This is the minor weakness of Auerswald and Saideman’s theory. The book nonetheless provides a way of ordering the mess of alliance politics with clear policy implications for NATO states and partners. The key lesson of the book is actually a warning: your friends, even if they are by your side, may not be willing to help you.
