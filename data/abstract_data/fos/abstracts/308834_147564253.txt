Computers on the Campus: A Survey of Computer Activities in the Liberal-Arts College
A UNIVERSITY without an electronic computer is rare nowadays. Many large institutions maintain two computers, one for academic, the other for administrative, purposes. Commenting on the recent purchase by the University of Massachusetts of one of the largest computer systems in the country, Dean Edward Moore noted that in view of "the remarkable strides being made in scientific research today, a top university must have a high-powered computer." How valid would his statement be had he replaced "university" by "college," specifically, the small private liberal-arts college? Vassar College, with i,6oo women undergraduates, is a case in point. Six years ago, at the request of the Department of Mathematics, the faculty reluctantly approved a course in computer theory to be taught at the advanced level by a research mathematician from the neighboring International Business Machines Corporation (IBM). Three years later the faculty enthusiastically endorsed this course as a standard curriculum offering. Its popularity, combined with demands from students variously oriented to computers and from young faculty members accustomed to using a computer for research, has brought Vassar College to a crossroad: Should we acquire a computer on campus? A number of questions follow the initial one. Are other colleges similar to ours maintaining computers? Is computer science a valid discipline in a liberal-arts curriculum or is it vocationally oriented? Should programing be taught? Can the high cost of even the most modest computer be justified for either academic or administrative usage? What kind of computer is most suitable for a small college? How can the purchase and upkeep of a computer be financed? Should academic or administrative usage have priority? Where would a computer be housed? Would a full-time director of the computer center be required, or could it be run by a faculty member or a faculty committee? What clerical staff would be needed? These are the questions that Alan Simpson, Vassar College's newly inaugurated president, is facing.
