Contracts and wills can be surprisingly vague even for highly elaborate works as demonstrated by some which have survived. Every commission was unique, responding to a particular set of circumstances, and each artistic project was the result of compromise. It would have been valuable in the discussion of contracts to mention the rich documentation for the marble high altarpiece (1388–92) at S. Francesco, Bologna, by the Venetian sculptors Pierpaolo and Jacobello dalle Masegne (the subject of a recent detailed analysis in Zeitschrift für Kunstgeschichte 67, no. 2 [2004], 153–82), and that for the bronze altarpiece by Donatello in the Santo at Padua, although the latter does lie outside the chronology of Bourdua’s study. Both were elaborate and extremely costly works, whose funding can only be partially accounted for through lay donors, and both were the subject of litigation between the friars and artists. In the light of the conclusions proposed by Bourdua, there is little to support Blume’s thought-provoking thesis, because, at least on surviving evidence, even if there was a policy for promoting the Order, it was almost inevitably inconsistently and selectively applied. Despite the recorded use of preparatory drawings, and of cartoons and reusable templates (patroni) during the execution of murals in the thirteenth and fourteenth century in Italy, there are no surviving carbon copies of the murals at Assisi, and even within the group of churches examined by Blume, few murals were overtly dependent on Assisi, and many were quite manifestly independently conceived. The fact that the Franciscans often inherited ready-made churches such as San Fermo, with existing decoration in place, would make any such policy impractical. The symbiotic relationships between the friars, the patrons and the artists presented in this well-researched study give perhaps a more realistic picture of how the Order operated in the real world, with its broad interpretation of rules, mutually advantageous arrangements with lay patrons, conflict of interests, and inevitable compromise.
