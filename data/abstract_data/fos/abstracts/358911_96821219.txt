Analytical study of the basic properties of lead tetraacetate as oxidizing agent
INTRODUCTION Among tetravalent lead compounds having the character of lead(IV) acids, lead tetrahalides of the type PbX4 (X = halogen), lead(IV) sulphate and Iead tetraacetate are currently known. For oxidation purposes particularly in the case of organic compounds, lead tetraacetate has found the widest use; it has replaced oxidations with, for example, lead dioxide not only in preparative reactions but especially in the case of the analytical applications of such processes. Lead tetraacetate was prepared for the first time more than a hundred years ago by Jaquelainl, and studied as far as its oxidation effects are concerned, by Dimroth and collaborators2, 3, who found that lead tetraacetate is a more selective oxidizing agent as compared to the lead dioxide used previously. Criegee and coworkers4 -lo have reported the characteristic effect of Iead tetraacetate on a-glycols and substances of related structure, in which the bond between the carbon atoms is split. Lead tetraacetate oxidizes the same types of compounds as periodic acid and its salts, the products obtained with these two agents being also the same. Oxidations with Iead tetraacetate have beendealt with in a review by Fleury and Courtoisll. Lead tetraacetate and periodic acid react differently with a-monohydroxy acids and some cis -trans isomers of a-glycols. Whereas periodic acid oxidizes a-monohydroxy acids at room temperature slowly, their oxidation with lead tetraacetate is generally rapid and quantitative. Periodic acid oxidizes cis and trans isomers of a-glycols equally rapidly. On the other hand, lead tetraacetate oxidizes the cis isomers more rapidly than the trans forms, and for this reason it is used frequently to distinguish between the two forms. Since oxidations with Iead tetraacetate proceed very rapidly, they may be used for indirect determinations of a-glycols and of substances of similar character, either the reagent excess or the oxidation products formed being determined, similarly as in oxidations with periodic acid and its salts. Unlike periodic acid and its salts, Iead tetraacetate oxidizes, in many cases, even the oxidation products, although at a slow rate. Another fact which must be considered in lead tetraacetate oxidations is the necessity of using an excess of it in a non-aqueous medium (mainly in glacial acetic acid) in order to eliminate hydrolysis of the lead(IV) salt.
