Whether the stories are tall tales, creation stories, or fairy tales, they reveal insights into the make-up of the populace—what they believe, what motivates them, what their attitudes are, how they behave, and how time and place shape the stories. An amazingly rich body of folk literature is available for children, and in this article, I focus on picture-book-format folk stories set in the United States—all of which have the potential to enrich social studies concepts and to teach young children about different U.S. geographic areas. I have divided the United States into five regions—Northeast and New England, South, Midwest and Plains States, Southwest, and West and Pacific States. I focused on books published since 1994, thus making them easier for teachers to find and share with children. As I examined close to ninety books for this article, certain themes emerged in the literature. The themes include simple, well-told folk tales set in various time periods and different regions of the country and tall tales full of heroes with superhuman strength. A new trend, which appeared in the past decade, is the retelling of traditional fairy tales, such as “Cinderella,” “Little Red Riding Hood,” and “Goldilocks and the Three Bears,” with distinct regional American settings. Native American tales are prevalent across the entire nation—pourquoi tales explaining natural phenomena, stories of the disparity between large and small, amusing trickster tales about coyote, and many stories centering on the relationship between the human and natural worlds. Although books featuring Native American stories have dominated this genre of picture book folk tales in the past ten years, each region of the United States has an ample treasury of folk stories emanating from the broad cultural and ethnic foundations of its population. The 1998 picture book This Land Is Your Land is an ideal preamble to learning about the United States. Folk musician and social activist Woody Guthrie wrote that song in 1940, after hitchhiking from Los Angeles to New York City. The words of the much-beloved national song, coupled with folk artist Kathy Jakobsen’s brilliant and detailed illustrations, make this a “must” for all teachers to share with their students. The book contains both the admirable and abhorrent in our nation and can serve as an overall springboard for introducing children to the numerous regions of our nation and the regionally based folk literature.
