Guest Editorial: Listening to Leisure Voices: Getting Engaged in Dialogues, Conversations and Entanglements
In this last issue of 2006, we have included six ‘thought’ pieces offered by Advisory Board members and others associated with the journal. These comprise an invited essay from Peter Bramham and five shorter pieces offered by their authors as personal insights into particular areas and interests with leisure studies. These papers provide the ideal opportunity to reflect on the development of the field of leisure studies, its structures and the ideologies that underpin its ways of knowing leisures. Of course, any engaged, reflexive researcher should be aware of the nature of their own academic collectives so that they are conscious of the epistemic traditions and power structures which shape knowledge production in their particular disciplines. In our field of leisure studies there have been suggestions that it has been in a state of crisis for some time (e.g. Deem, 1999; Jackson & Burton, 1999), a suggestion prompted by: falling enrolments on leisure courses; disappearing public sector leisure departments; a less than visible leisure professoriate (at least here in the UK); the ‘meaning’ and relevance of leisure studies/ sciences; a failure to engage in constructive academic dialogue with other fields and disciplines; and inadequate funding for leisure research. Indeed, these are concerns which all of the ‘thought piece’ contributors in this issue address in some shape or form. Yet in spite of these undoubted challenges that confront leisure research, as these contributors also suggest, the field has much to build on and to be optimistic about – if we as leisure researchers have the confidence to reach out to new coalitions, alliances and agendas. It is perhaps salutary to begin this editorial with the issue of naming, for as Peter Bramham reminds us, ‘naming is everything and this is to enter the dark Foucauldian world of discourse and power’.
