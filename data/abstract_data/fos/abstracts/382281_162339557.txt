From Modernist Entombment to Postmodernist Exhumation: Dead Bodies in Twentieth-Century American Fiction (review)
The Tunnel and DeLillo's Underworld garner only four pages of commentary, the same number accorded Roth's The Human Stain. This imbalance would be less glaring were it not for the fact that O'Donnell calls special attention to the importance of the encyclopedic gesture in contemporary American fiction. Still, some texts in this tradition get their due: in Part 3, discussion of the tension between imagined community and performed racial caricature in Kingston's Tripmaster Monkey serves as a particularly effective spur to analysis of this complex text. I suspect every reader of O'Donnell's book will wish certain authors had received more attention or been represented by a different novel. I can't imagine any reader failing to appreciate O'Donnell's compendious handling of such a wide range of novels meticulously organized to promote scholarship and debate. Even as a specialist in the period I have not read all of the novels addressed in the book, and much of the value of The American Novel Now lies in its compelling demonstration of how much work remains to be done in the field of contemporary American fiction. For this reason, among many, I predict it will become an important touchstone for teachers and researchers alike.
