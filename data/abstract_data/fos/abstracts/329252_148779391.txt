Such data as are presented, furthermore, do not always agree with the interpretations made by the authors. A considerable number of the interpretations as to forces affecting wage structure put forth in these industry studies are open to challenge or criticism. Chapter 7, "The Impact of Unionism on Wage Structure," presents no more than a summary of generally accepted views as to the major forces influencing personal, interplant, geographical, occupational, and interindustry differentials. It does not resolve the unanswered questions, such as the influence of unionism on interindustry wage differences. Disappointingly, it does not draw very heavily upon the case studies in the preceding chapters, nor does it contain many references to other research on wages, despite the authors' indication that this would be the case. The notes on sources and the selected bibliography at the end of the volume contain no material to support this chapter. The studies of national wage structures in Part 2 analyze for each of the five countries the influence in wage determination of collective bargaining, government policies and regulation, and market forces. This analysis is followed by discussion of economic factors affecting the general wage level and examination of occupational, interindustry, geographical, and other wage differentials. Well organized and well written, these studies not only provide much valuable information, but also raise many provocative issues in regard to the roles of economic forces and group action in economic affairs. Unfortunately, a criticism similar to that made of the industry studies must be directed toward these chapters. Their briefness makes them inadequate to support the intended analysis. Essentially, they supply only a description of wage structure, for the European countries and Canada since the start of World War II, for the United States over a longer period. The historical development of wage structure cannot be adequately derived from such a short time span, nor can the influences molding the wage patterns in the various countries be evaluated without much more extensive information on labor market characteristics than is supplied. The very fact that the findings of this analysis of wage structure are tentative and incomplete, however, emphasizes the significant contribution which the authors have made, for they have perceptively and boldy identified the issues which require investigation if we are to develop an articulated, meaningful concept of wage structure. It perhaps is unfortunate that the authors felt tempted to answer nearly all of the questions, large and small, which they posed in the volume. As has been indicated, the case studies are too limited in scope to permit success in this endeavor. Indeed, it will require years of extensive research before many of the issues, here so challengingly posed, can be resolved. A more restricted endeavor might have produced more satisfactory answers. But while the authors might have made a safer journey had they chosen to fly less far into the unknown, social research, like aviation, cannot progress if no one dares leave solid ground. The era of navigation by intuition and dead reckoning necessarily preceded that of flight instruments and radar. If other economists take up the challenges (intended plural) posed by the team of Reynolds and Taft, wage analysis will also pass the sound barrier (of empty arguments).
