Family history of alcoholism and brain activation: commentary on "Increased forebrain activations in youths with family histories of alcohol and other substance use disorders performing a Go/No-Go task".
ALCOHOL USE DISORDERS (AUDs) are at the foundation of major societal and health problems within the United States and around the world. Initiatives such as the Collaborative Studies on Genetics of Alcoholism (COGA) funded by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) continue to provide a rich data set highlighting linkages between gene variants and specific endophenotypes characteristic of AUDs or elevated risk for developing an AUD. COGA and other studies, largely using electrophysiological measures, have shown that children and youth, na€ıve to alcohol, but with a positive family history (FH+) of an AUD, show differential brain activity compared with those who are family history negative (FH ). The differential activation patterns are typically in the direction of those seen when comparing AUD sufferers to controls, raising the possibility that such differences in brain activity are premorbid to alcohol abuse and dependence and may even predispose individuals to developing an AUD or at least act as a biomarker of such predisposition. The paper by Acheson and colleagues (2014) published recently in this journal adds to this literature by investigating functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) activation in FH+ youth for AUD or another substance use disorder compared with FH youth. Acheson and colleagues (2014) investigated activity in the context of a GO/NO-GO task, a validated measure of inhibitory control. Inhibitory control is a key component of the executive functions mediated by the prefrontal cortex and modulated by a network of other regions including the anterior cingulate, motor, supplementary motor, and parietal cortices (Crews and Boettiger, 2009; Oscar-Berman and Marinkovic, 2007). Previous studies using electroencephalogram measures in GO/NO-GO paradigms have shown that the P300 (P3) response to the NO-GO stimulus is diminished in those with a predisposition to alcoholism (Cohen et al., 1997a,b; Hada et al., 2001; Kamarajan et al., 2005a,b; Rodriguez Holguin et al., 1999b) as it is in alcoholics (Ceballos et al., 2003; Cohen et al., 1997b; Colrain et al., 2011; Ford et al., 1991; Hada et al., 2000; Kamarajan et al., 2005a,b; Rodriguez Holguin et al., 1999a). This effect has been hypothesized to be due to disruption of the neural circuit responsible for inhibitory processing and is considered to be an endophenotypic marker of alcoholism (Kamarajan et al., 2005a,b). A converging fMRI literature is highlighting the importance of connected networks of brain regions that support a variety of brain functions. These include a Default Mode Network (DMN), Executive Control Network, a Salience Network, and an Attention Network. Primary sensory networks exist for visual, auditory, and somatosensory modalities and those involving the limbic system and particularly important in drug and alcohol dependence include a Reward Network and an Emotion Network. Activity in these networks has been investigated recently in the context of alcoholism (e.g., M€uller-Oehring et al., 2014) and comparing FH+ to FH , alcohol na€ıve adolescents (Cservenka et al., 2014; Spadoni et al., 2013; Wetherill et al., 2012). Functional connectivity between brain regions is subserved by white matter tracts, and as indicated by Acheson and colleagues (2014), the integrity of white matter pathways has been shown to be altered in those FH+ for alcoholism (Herting et al., 2011; Squeglia et al., 2014), as well as in alcoholics themselves (Pfefferbaum et al., 2009). Several of the regions identified by Acheson and colleagues (2014) as showing greater activation in FH+ versus FH individuals for the GO and GO/NO-GO conditions are involved in 1 or more of the networks that have been implicated in either alcoholism or alcohol risk. In particular, the posterior cingulate/precuneus, superior temporal gyrus, medial superior frontal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus are all part of the DMN.
