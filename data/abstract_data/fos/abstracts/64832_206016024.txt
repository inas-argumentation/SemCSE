Editor's corner "good enough" software - A unique American advantage
Ed Yourdon has a relatively new book out. And when Ed speaks, it’s always worth listening. This new book is a sequel to his De&e and Full of the American Propmmer, and, in fact, it reverses the theory that book espoused. The new one is The Rise and Reswrection of the American Programmer, and it is upbeat in its view of the future of American software people. One of the advantages that American programmers uniquely have, Yourdon points out in his book, is the notion of “good enough software.” Software need not be perfect; it just needs to solve the problem at hand in a reasonably clean way. It is a unique advantage of American programmers, Yourdon continues, because the American worker and his or her manager may be comfortable with freewheeling management styles. By contrast, European and Japanese companies tend to be run in a top-down, authoritarian way. Of course, many American companies are run that way too. But, especially in the micro software world, it is possible for the American programmer to be responsible for his or her work, and management will trust them to find truly “good enough” solutions. Yourdon didn’t seem to know it in the draft version of the book I saw when I acted as a reviewer for his publisher, but there is an underlying principle here, one commonly called “the BIEGE principle.” BIEGE is an acronym, and it stands for The Better Is the Enemy of the Good Enough. What the BIEGE principle means is that, when you have a problem where a reasonably accurate solution is acceptable, attempts to provide a better solution are a waste of time and money. Not all problems are of that kind, of course. Sometimes only a “best possible” solution will do. But in the world of rough-edged, real-world problems, there often isn’t any way (except for expensive trial and error) to find a best solution. BIEGE is about finding problem solutions that are not necessarily optimum but are at least satisfactory. In fact, in Herbert Simon’s wonderful book The Sciences of the Artificiul, he invents a term for that goal-“satisficing.” One satisfices when optimizing is too expensive or unlikely to converge on a useful result. And how does Simon say we know a satisficing solution when we see one? When it is “supportable,” he says; that is, when there is a credible argument that the solution is satisfactory. Yourdon’s discussion of “good enough” is mostly from the point of view of software reliability. Goodenough software can be released with errors if the errors are clearly not showstoppers. He mentions the rumor that an early version of Microsoft Windows was released with XKKl known errors. And, although he didn’t point it out, Space Shuttle missions have always been flown with known software errors. In each case, enough is known about the errors in question to know that they will not harm the mission of the software. “Good enough,” management says, and into production the new product goes. In his essay, Yourdon discusses tradeoffs between functionality and schedule and quality. With “good enough” software, one of those goals is subordinated to one or more of the others. It may be that a release is on schedule with fewer features or more bugs. Or that schedule is delayed in order to reduce the number of bugs and to strengthen functionality.
