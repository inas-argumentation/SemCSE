CONTEMPORARY CHILDCARE ISSUES FACING COLLEGES AND UNIVERSITIES
Although childcare/child development centers and higher education have a history dating back to the World War II years, current conditions have precipitated a cut back in availability. The authors maintain that such facilities belong on college and university campuses and contend that well-run programs benefit children, institutions, and society. Beginning with the community outreach stimulated by involvement in World War II civil defense efforts, childcare centers and child development programs found their way, in ever-expanding numbers, to the campuses of the nation's community colleges. During the 1960s and 1970s the concept was expanded to encompass colleges and universities. Faced with rising start-up costs, insurance problems, and dwindling resources, today's higher education administrators have instituted cut back measures which negatively impact many such facilities. Early childhood education/childcare centers provide a safe and quality program for children of students, faculty, and even parents from the community. Advocates, maintaining that such programs fill an important societal need by allowing student parents who might otherwise unnecessarily suffer loss of educational opportunity to attend class, contend that the availability of quality childcare is not presently adequate to meet the needs of mothers joining the workforce. Florida's WAGES (Work and Gain Economic Self-sufficiency) program, which requires that mothers go to work when their child is three months old, may place children who receive less than adequate care at risk. Many other states have similar requirements. Even more compelling reasons for campus-based childcare may reside in far-reaching issues facing all of society. Americans cope daily with problems involving welfare reform, racial and gender inequities, and learning deficits in children resulting from lack of nurturing and appropriate stimulation. Absence of quality childcare becomes more than just a local concern. The inability of government to bring about adequate welfare reform has produced conditions under which colleges and universities must resume their historical role. An ounce of prevention used to be worth a pound of cure. Experts in Early Childhood Education agree that the first three years of life are crucial. Children who receive appropriate nurturing and opportunities to participate in age-appropriate activities are likely to be successful in school and in adult life. Providing quality childcare at the earliest possible age has the potential for saving billions of dollars. According to the Children's Defense Fund (1997), "Two-thirds of the states spend more than 10 times as much on corrections and prisons than on early childhood education." By setting standards for the district and nation, higher education childcare programs can provide a valuable service to students and faculty, help ensure children's potential for later success, and make a profound statement. Although early childhood professionals have long realized the importance of quality adult/child interactions, current technology provides actual proof. Research reveals that an infant who does not receive adequate attention will develop a brain that is 20-30% smaller than that of a child who does. While 100 billion neurons, more than an individual will ever need, are present at birth, critical connections develop as the infant receives nurturing and stimulation. Areas that are seldom used are eliminated in the first 18 months of life (Nash, 1997). Childcare centers can provide leadership in dealing with both racial and cultural diversity. An anti-biased preschool curriculum presented by qualified personnel could serve as a model for reaching the ultimate goal of a bias-free society. College and university centers typically offer a variety of multi-cultural experiences and materials that promote positive self-esteem. Early exposure to a totally integrated, open environment provides a microcosm for a better America. …
