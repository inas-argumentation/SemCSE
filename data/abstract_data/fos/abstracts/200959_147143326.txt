Publications and Teaching Effectiveness: A Search for Some Relationship
D EBATES flourish concerning what relationship holds between quality of teaching and research. These debates may stem from the importance of the topic; they may also stem from the dearth of relevant data. The present studies provide supplementary data.' The studies help answer these questions: Are persons who do much publishing better teachers than those who publish little or nothing? Are they not as good teachers? Are the best teachers those who publish a little, but not very much? Or is there, perhaps, no relationship between publication record and quality of teaching? Is effective teaching related to neither quantity nor quality of research? To explore these realms we must estimate effectiveness of teaching. Three methods of estimating a teacher's effectiveness are commonly available: (i) comparison of his students' performance on some achievement test with the performance of other students; (2) evaluation of his teaching by colleagues and administrators; (3) evaluation of his teaching by a representative sample of his students. The first method is appropriate only when teachers offer courses with similar content and objectives. For this and other reasons, it is not used in this study. The second method uses ratings by persons highly experienced in making evaluations. Unfortunately, those raters seldom have observed the individual in the classroom. Therefore they base their ratings of his teaching on his performance in rather different situations and/or on statements made by some of his students. These students may or may not be a representative sample of the teacher's classes. Further, the sample may or may not be comparable from one teacher to another. The third method avoids those drawbacks. When student-ratings are used to estimate a teacher's effectiveness, the evaluations of teaching are made by those who actually experienced the teaching. Moreover, the evaluation can be based on the report of a greater number of students than the teacher's colleagues ordinarily receive information from in making their estimates. These students, furthermore, can be selected so as to constitute a representative sample of the teacher's total student population and to be comparable from one teacher to another.
