Flying Swine: Appropriate Targets and Goals of Monetary Policy
Traditionally, economists have thought that monetary policy involves use of tools (open market operations, discount rates) to hit operating targets (fed funds rate, reserve aggregates) believed to be related to intermediate targets (short-term interest rates, monetary aggregates) to achieve longer-term goals (low inflation, high employment, economic growth). Only the goals are thought important; the operating and intermediate targets are not important. If a target is not reliably linked to the goals, it is dropped in favor of another. If one of the goals proves to be outside the reach of policy because the Fed is unable to influence it, then the goal is dropped. In other words, if monetary policy proves to be powerless to achieve swinely flight, the goal should not be geared toward pigs flying-try as it might, the Fed will not enable pigs to fly. However, it is possible that the Fed's goals could be measured in a number of ways; on some definitions, policy might indeed be effective. It would make little sense to define a goal such as to preclude policy effectiveness. While pigs may never fly on their own, the Fed could certainly devise tools and targets to induce airlines to load them onto 747s! Whether the Fed could achieve the goal of pigs-in-flight depends critically on how the goal is defined. Current monetary policy is in a quandary. First, there is no consensus on the relation of operating targets to intermediate targets. In particular, the experience of the 1980s cast considerable doubt on the relation between reserves and money, with the Fed unable to hit monetary targets even though it continually adjusted its targets to accord more favorably with experience.
