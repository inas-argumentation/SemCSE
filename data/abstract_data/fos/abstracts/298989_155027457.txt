Constructing a middle power: Ideas and Canadian foreign policy
Introduction Over forty years ago, John Holmes argued for the acceptance of some “ambiguity” in the use of the term “middle power”—that to seek “a logical clarification...would serve only to raise hornets, large, middle, and small.” It was after all an “expression of convenience” (Holmes, 1966: 16). This “expression of convenience” has enjoyed continued salience over the postwar period with both policy makers and analysts. It has been referred to in a myriad of ways including middle power as an “ideology,” a “doctrine,” a “role,” a “guide,” and a “myth,” among others. This paper contributes to the ongoing debate concerning middle power by arguing that the middle power concept is important not because it has provided an accurate description of Canada’s foreign policy in the postwar period, but because it has guided policy makers in crafting Canada’s foreign policy. The paper revisits standard notions of Canada as a middle power by exploring the emergence, adoption, and embedment of middle power as the ideational framework within which Canada’s foreign policy was formulated between 1941 and 1956. The analysis is divided into four sections. In the first section, the extant middle power literature is examined. This is followed in the second section by a look at the importance of ideas to the study of Canadian foreign policy. The third part of the paper outlines a threestage process through which ideas come to influence foreign policy. Finally, the Canadian case examining the emergence, adoption, and embedment of the middle power idea is developed. The paper is exploratory in character, meaning that it attempts to outline a novel way of looking at the middle power concept and, in doing so, may raise more questions than it answers. What is sought is less “a perfection of consensus” surrounding the middle power concept and more a “refinement of debate” concerning middle power (Geertz, 1973: 29).
