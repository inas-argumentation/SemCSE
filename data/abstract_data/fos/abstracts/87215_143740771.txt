The framework must be strong enough to protect the rights of every citizen, yet be sufficiently flexible to allow for changes that result from including the nation’s diversity more fully and equitably. Like the checks and balances built into the three branches of government of the United States federal system, it is in the interplay between unity and diversity where the rich dialogues of democracy occur. Disagreement is built into the system. The tension itself is the very soil of a dynamic democracy, and to wish for its resolution is to wish for the end of democratic life. The ability and willingness to disagree constructively is fundamental to the ability to live in and welcome the unitydiversity tension. To disagree constructively means to disagree with ideas and perspectives rather than with the individual holding them, to suspend the rush to judgment so that an idea can have a genuine hearing, and to listen with the democratic commitment that one’s mind might change if presented with new and convincing data (Parker 2003). The opposite of disagreeing constructively is disagreeing destructively—insulting another person or resorting to ridicule or violence in response to a different point of view. Destructive disagreement destroys the potential for any kind of relationship or dialogue to develop, whereas constructive disagreement allows for shared inquiry to continue even while differences remain intact. In this article, I offer social studies educators a means to deepen students’ understanding of and skills for constructive disagreement by bringing together four areas of study that inform our awareness of the role of the body in democratic life. First, in recent decades, feminist theory has offered new insights and avenues for rethinking the importance of the body as a site of knowing and as a site of political resistance (Code 1991; Collins 2000; Gatens 1996; Shapiro 1999). Second, the suffragettes struggle and the Civil Rights movement of the United States (1954–68) were successful not only because of great legislative and judicial achievements but also because individuals put their bodies in the service of the democratic ideal (Harding 1995; Parkins 2000). Third, in earlier research about the potential of the body to inform understanding of ideas and concepts, researchers pointed to the need to consider how the body might inform social studies education (Boykin and Allen 1988; Gardner 1983, 2003; Guttentag 1972; Jensen 2001). Fourth, techniques drawn from a unique movement practice, Aikido (pronounced eye-key-doe) may offer physical simulations of constructive disagreement (Dobson and Miller 1993; Heiny 1998; Saotome 1993). I include specific curricular suggestions and classroom activities that illustrate how attending to the body might enhance the learning of constructive disagreement skills that are so critical to life in a multicultural society.
