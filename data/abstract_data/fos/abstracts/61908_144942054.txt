Planned Incapacitation: A Case Study of How Not to Teach Black Teachers to Teach.
It is by now a rather commonly accepted fact among members of both the black and white communities of this nation that the public schools are in serious trouble.' The "trouble" gets defined in many ways: inadequate financing, overcrowded classrooms, racial separation, antiquated curriculum, etc. In response to these conditions, all sorts of remedies have been proposed, depending upon the particular diagnosis. Yet the patient does not appear to be responding to the treatment. The schools continue to turn out docile, at times illiterate, and often alienated youngsters (see Henry [7], Silberman [15]). The social arrangements of schools, however, affect not only the students, but the teachers as well. Thus it may be no less accurate to claim that the schools also produce docile, often incompetent, and alienated teachers (Fuchs, [3]).
