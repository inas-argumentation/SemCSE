Correlates of Satisfaction, Intrapersonal Learning, and Academic Outcomes at Counseling Centers in a University System.
Examining the role of college mental health services in promoting student personal and academic success is essential to demonstrating their utility to university communities and administrations. Student learning outcomes following counseling and satisfaction with mental health services were assessed across 16 counseling centers in one university system. Following counseling, students reported overwhelming satisfaction with services, significant gains in intrapersonal skills, improved ability to focus on academics, and greater intention to stay in school. Older students and those living on campus reported more academic benefits as a result of counseling whereas women and those living off-campus reported greater change in overall well-being. Finally, students in their later years described better intrapersonal outcomes as a result of counseling than those in their early years of college. Findings suggest that student psychological well-being and academic functioning are intimately linked and have important implications for enhancing student outcomes. This project may serve as a model for other university systems who wish to share evaluation goals and join resources in assessment of student services. Keywords: student services, student client satisfaction, college student mental health; counseling in higher education; student satisfaction ********** The impact of college and university counseling center services on student functioning represents an important intersection of mental health and higher education research, but is understudied in both areas. The few existing studies suggest that counseling centers are likely effective at improving student mental health and academic functioning. DeStefano, Mellott, and Peterson (2001) compared scores of students who did and did not seek counseling using the Student Adjustment to College Questionnaire (SACQ; Baker & Siryk, 1989), a measure of academic adjustment, social adjustment, personal emotional adjustment, and attachment to the institution. The authors reported significant differences in adjustment between the counseling and non-help-seeking control groups prior to participation in counseling, with the students seeking counseling showing poorer adjustment. At posttest (6-8 weeks or 6 counseling sessions later), the gap between groups had diminished, especially on the academic, social, and personal emotional adjustment subscales. This is consistent with other studies comparing students seeking counseling services with non-clinical college student comparison groups on measures of academic distress (Lockard, Hayes, McAleavey, & Lock, 2012) and retention (Wilson, Mason, & Ewing, 1997), although the research on the relationship between counseling and academic functioning is mixed (Illovsky, 1997; Lee, Olson, Locke, Michelson, & Odes, 2009). Other researchers have also found that students at counseling centers experience clinically significant reductions in mental health symptoms as well as improvement in personal and academic functioning (e.g., Choi, Buskey, and Johnson, 2010). While the small amount of research on the role of counseling centers in academic functioning is promising, additional work is needed. In particular, those counseling centers that collect utilization and outcome data often do so unsystematically. Instruments are typically developed in-house and for use within a single center (Lewis & Magoon, 1987; Locke, Buzolitz, Lei, Boswell, McAleavey, Sevig, et al., 2011). These data are not and cannot be compared across institutions. When standardized assessment tools are used, they are often designed for community populations (e.g., Symptom Check List-90; Derogatis, 1994) rather than student populations. This leaves many of the concerns most salient to counseling center clients unaddressed (e.g., improvements in academic motivation and performance). Significant progress on data standardization has been made in recent years, allowing institutions to compare the wellness of their student body to other universities and nationally representative samples. …
