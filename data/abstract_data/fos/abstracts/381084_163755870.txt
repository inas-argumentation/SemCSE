Prominent among these major groups, of course, is women, who never find their way into Milton’s explicit political advocacy in the prose. One might well argue that the divorce tracts contain implicit advocacy, but given the predominantly male political audience for whom Milton writes such advocacy remains undeveloped. Even in Paradise Lost, Eve’s rise from her inherited biblical stature represents a mere ‘‘social and familial elevation’’ (62). The heart of the book attends to religion and theology. Shawcross reminds us of the younger Milton’s apparently untroubled orthodoxy, as evidenced in his signing of the University Subscription Books. Against this orthodoxy are evaluated the various scholarly analyses that have been made of later Miltonic pronouncements on such fundamentals as free will, salvation, and the nature of the Deity. Shawcross makes an important distinction between a miraculous doctrine such as the virgin birth, which Milton does not question because it is clearly attested in the Bible, and the no less miraculous Trinity, for which scripture provides more problematic evidence. His analysis of Milton’s logical analysis of the doctrine in De doctrina Christiana is impeccable, but I find less satisfactory his sometimes confident application of the treatise’s distinctions to the epics, where ideas generally unfold more dialogically without definitive resolution. He readily acknowledges, for example, the creative deployment of ‘‘confusion’’ (95) and ‘‘mystery’’ (96) in the poems, but then categorically insists that the Son’s ‘‘begetting’’ in book 5 of Paradise Lost ‘‘is, of course, not ‘generation’’’ (115). But is not the poem’s suppression of this distinction crucial to Satan’s fall, and therefore to the fallen reader’s necessary participation in the Satanic perspective? A chapter devoted to ‘‘a different kind of conceptual development,’’ the elaborate process of internal allusion within the poems that continually alters the reader’s understanding of words and concepts contains an abundance of subtle readings, even if it leads the book off course from its announced subject momentarily. When he expands consideration to inter-poem allusions, or examines the range of meanings in the prose that can inform our understanding of the poems, Shawcross returns to his overall, and very convincing thesis. A closing chapter is devoted to assessing where Milton’s changes and nonchanges locate him in the larger historical perspective. Shawcross argues that ‘‘Milton did not fully understand the changes that were occurring in philosophical (including religious) thought during his lifetime’’ (174). For Milton the writer of prose, a steadfast prioritizing of scripture as the source of revelation prevented his full participation in the emergence of deism and scientific rationality. But as a poet of Nature he appears to advance beyond his prosaic limitations. Paradise Lost, Shawcross shrewdly concludes, ‘‘greatly advances the movement that will bring about such thinking and such ultimately transcendental philosophy as the future held’’ (180).
