Government Inclusion in Porter's Diamond: The Case of Argentina
INTRODUCTION Development of international markets is crucial for the growth of businesses. Over the last three decades world trade has expanded from $200 billion to $7 trillion (Czinkota and Ronkainen, 2004). Porter's (1990) Determinates o f International Country Attractiveness model (or the Porter "diamond" model) has been the standard used to determine "where" firms should expand internationally. Porter's determinants are (1) demand conditions, (2) related and supporting industries, (3) strategy, structure, and rivalry and (4) factor conditions (Porter, 1990). One limitation of the diamond model is that the role of the international government is not specifically addressed. While Porter (1990) agrees government is important, he doe s not include it as a fifth determinant in the model. Government's r ole is to influence the other four points of the diamond and Porter's message for governments is to develop the diamond by using government only as an aid but not as the primary force. Porter agrees targeting industrial policies will be ultimately successful and governments should exploit these (Ankli, 1992). Government efforts, along with local actions, promote national advancement policy. In countries where industries are state owned, the role of government becomes quite important. State ownership results in industries being controlled by international government and thus changes the role and power of government in these instances. Porter called the role of government one of "catalyst and challenger" to push companies to raise their aspirations or move to higher levels of competitive performance. Porter is silent upon exactly how government can specifically act as a "catalyst and challenge". Porter's position supports the view that industries within countries are assumed to be privatized, yet in some countries, industries remain state owned. Chobanyan and Leigh (2006) feel the government's role should be to upgrade basic factors and improve the infrastructure and educational system to develop a technological base as well as create and develop industrial policy. Thus, government must take a larger role in the economy beyond the "catalyst" Porter (1990) originally recommended in the diamond model. Rios-Morales and Brennan (2007) adapted Porter's diamond to address Ireland's foreign direct investment and found it to be a key component to the country's growth. By lowering trade barriers, the country becomes more competitive because competition is no longer confined to domestic rivals. Waldron (1997) used Porter's diamond to study Taiwan's development strategy and found the country incorporates an extraordinary degree of governmental intervention in certain industries. Waldron (1997) believes this intervention is necessary to overcome Taiwan's structural and natural resource limitations. In a study of the commercial success of Brazilian aircraft manufacturer Embraer, Espana (2004) found a similar limitation of the Porter diamond. Embrarer was created by the Brazilian government as a mixed enterprise with private inventory owning 49% of the company. Privatized in 1994, the company became more market oriented and efficient after privatization. In this case, the role of the government acted as a "catalyst." Espana (2004) stated the case illustrates " the shortcomings of international competitiveness to explain the commercial success of many firms, especially in developing countries, that owe their very creation to government, not market forces" (p. 504). Hodgetts (1993), in his study of Porter's diamond framework in a Mexico, agreed the government is of critical importance to influencing a nation's competitiveness by controlling domestic rivalry. Some countries have differing economic strength or affluence than those studied by Porter (1990).
