Signposting these links would have tied the introduction, which uses Toni Morrison's ideas on white parasitic exploitation of the African American other, more securely to the main body of the text. For while The Angelic Mother and the Predatory Seductress introduces race as a major theme, race per se plays only a marginal role in Lancaster's argument. Other surprising omissions in this section include Walter B. Rideout's The Radical Novel in the United States, 1900–1954 (1956), which the bibliography cites but the book does not quote, and Fred E. Beal's Gastonia account in Proletarian Journey (1937). In addition, and as a possibility for future research, one cannot help but wonder where John Dos Passos fits into Lancaster's project. Although refused permission to cover events at Gastonia by the editor of The New Republic, Dos Passos wrote about the strike for the Labor Defender, a notable left-wing magazine of the era. Furthermore, if William Rollins's The Shadow Before (mentioned in passing by Lancaster) owes a debt to Dos Passos's modal modernism, do the female authors this chapter focuses on—Mary Heaton Vorse, Myra Page, Grace Lumpkin, and Olive Tilford Dargan—also draw on Dos Passos's technique? If not, why not? Such details would have added considerably to the sociopolitical, historical, and intertextual density of Lancaster's commendable investigation into the portrayal of the female proletariat during the Great Depression. Minor niggles over grammar and editorial rigor add to these frustrations. In closing, then, The Angelic Mother and the Predatory Seductress is at once an intriguing and frustrating monograph, a book well worth reading, but one clouded by the mysteries of cultural ontogeny.
