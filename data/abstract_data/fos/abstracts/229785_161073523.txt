Citizen Shakespeare: Freemen and Aliens in the Language of the Plays (review)
This fusion characterizes the islands of Othello, a play, like its Cyprus, that “images both the insularity and the fluidity of human identity, and which suggests, above all, that identity is collectively rather than individually constituted, and that it is subject to forces which may change and shape it in unpredictable and unwelcome ways which are themselves conditioned by and dependent on both time and place” (94). Concluding this chapter, she writes, “Islands function in the Renaissance imagination as places of danger, as reminders of divine power, and above all as emblems of the uncertainties of boundaries” (110). Like the islands of Othello, so the edges and borders of the other plays examined here are similarly, if unpredictably, dangerous and uncertain. The dust-jacket of Shakespeare on the Edge shows George Lyly’s extraordinary 1548 map of Britanniæ Insulæ, the British Isles. This, alas, is the only map or illustration the book offers: given its subject, more would have been helpful. MARK TAYLOR Manhattan College
