Understanding Obligations of the FSLIC
INCREASING MARKETING SUCCESS CPAs who thought hiring a marketing director or developing a firm brochure would relieve the pressure to sell their firm's services are finding they couldn't have been more mistaken. The evolution of marketing in CPA firms has, in fact, come full circle - smack back into the lap of the CPA. All firm marketing efforts funnel down to making the sale. And, by and large, the CPA must make that sale. Selling is the ability to stimulate other people - clients or the firm's own staff - to take productive action. Unfortunately, CPAs are rarely trained to deal effectively with the factors that motivate others to accept or reject their ideas, products or services. Clients will willingly buy into a service only if they see how they will benefit from it, but CPAs typically present information from their own points of view and respond ineffectively to objections raised by clients. In order to sell their services successfully and achieve and retain more satisfied clients, CPAs need to discover the communications techniques that are appropriate for them as professionals. This article describes some of those techniques and offers a case study of what marketing approaches did and didn't work for one CPA. CHANGING THE FOCUS Most CPAs are highly analytical. They are skilled in separating things into their component parts and reasoning through a problem or situation to come up with the answer. While they take pride in their analytical ability, they frequently are less than certain about their communications skills. To expand their practices, CPAs must learn how to present their services in a persuasive manner. There are three steps to greater persuasiveness: * Comparative analysis. This step ensures that an idea is sound based on one's own judgment. * Analytical process. This step examines the idea from the client's viewpoint. * Communications process. This step presents the idea to the client (or other listener) from his or her own viewpoint, thus increasing the chances for willing acceptance. It helps to deal successfully with resistance and to secure the client's commitment to action. (Some call this closing the sale.) Most CPAs already evaluate ideas from their own viewpoints. The following example illustrates why they need to do more. Smith & Jones, CPAs, recently completed the corporate and individual tax returns for a $10 million manufacturing company and its principal owner. It is clear that the company and Bob Miller, its president and chief executive officer, would benefit financially from tax planning. However, while Miller is intensely interested in his company's operations, he has never expressed any interest in tax planning services. Herbert Jones, the firm's tax partner, seeing an opportunity to provide planning services for this client, spends time and effort compiling a list of all the ways Miller could save tax dollars. The benefits to the CEO seem obvious. "When Bob Miller sees this I know he'll want to start planning right away," Jones tells his managing partner. Jones and the managing partner invite Miller to lunch and Jones brings out his list and starts describing the merits of various tax strategies, pointing out the steps the firm could take to save Miller and his company money. Miller listens patiently for a few minutes. Then, while Jones is in midsentence, the CEO turns to the managing partner and starts talking excitedly about the company's plans to build a facility to produce a new product it has developed. The CEO totally ignores Jones for the rest of the lunch. Jones, humiliated and confused, wonders where he went wrong in his marketing efforts.
