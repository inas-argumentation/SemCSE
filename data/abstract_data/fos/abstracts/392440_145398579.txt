Negotiating Paradise: U.S. Tourism and Empire in Twentieth-Century Latin America by Dennis Merrill (review)
Arguably, Garrigan's explanation of how these commodified objects served compensatory functions for the Porfirian state is a bit heavy-handed at times. Noticeably absent from the picture, too, are voices that contested the hegemonic dialogue, apart from those of the lettered elites who were immersed in the fine-tuning of its terms. On the one hand, Garrigan may flatten her terrain of inquiry a bit too much by strategically downplaying the differences between each of the discursive and disciplinary domains her chapters touch upon, thus clearing the way to focus on a set of overarching questions. On the other, her interdisciplinary focus examines unexpected and compelling intersections among these domains, intersections that more narrowly discipline-specific accounts of the same objects and events have missed. For instance, Garrigan questions whether the discursive value of statistics, as arrayed in government reports to exaggerate the state's precarious hold over natural resources, could exert such powerful compensatory effects as did the formerly unexhibited and very large phallic Aztec objects shown at the National Museum. While the statistics were arranged to assuage anxieties about the state's (mostly deficient) management of resources and territory, the exuberance of the pre-Columbian phallic objects metonymically made up for the same state's mismanagement of living Mexican 'Indians' in the realms that those very statistics themselves represented: the economy, infrastructure, and state policy, more generally speaking (p. 169). These are not the kinds of juxtapositions that histories of official culture, Mexican or not, tend to focus on. Garrigan nevertheless makes a very compelling case for how it is often in the seemingly chaotic encounters between disparate realms of knowledge production and bureaucratic practice that national formations are formulated over time.
