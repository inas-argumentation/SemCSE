But her sensitive account of the interplay between the ideas and programs addressed to changing economic and political circumstances also suggests that a once-triumphant paradigm may not yet represent the end of history. An Associated Press story this past summer (19 July 2007) described school children in Guinea gathering in large groups at the airport and at gas stations at night in order to do their homework under fluorescent lights. Despite significant rivers and mineral wealth, only about a fifth of Guinea’s ten million people have any access to electricity. With Phillips’s able help, stories like these may start to remind us of what the New Deal accomplished—and the potential power of politics and government, for all their limitations, to address serious economic and environmental problems.
