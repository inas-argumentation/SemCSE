College Adjustment among Freshmen: Predictors for White and Hispanic Males and Females.
The purpose of this study was to assess personal and interpersonal variables as predictors of college adjustment among a sample of 883 freshmen. Specifically, we examined the extent to which self-esteem, parental education, parental support, and peer support predicted adjustment during the first year of college. Furthermore, we were interested in determining if these predictor variables varied by race and gender. Regression analyses revealed that self-esteem was predictive of better adjustment to college among all groups except Hispanic males. In addition, parents' education level was predictive of overall college adjustment for females regardless of race. Moreover, White females who had supportive peers reported being better adjusted to college. ********** Adjusting to college is a major transition in a young adult's life. Unfortunately, a large percentage of students are unsuccessful in navigating this transition. In fact, approximately 50% of students depart college without obtaining a degree, the majority leaving during the first two years (ACT, 2002; Parker, Summerfeldt, Hogan, & Majeski, 2004). Moreover, research has found that students who withdraw often do so for personal reasons such as a lack of adjustment to their new environment (Kalsner & Pistole, 2003; Kerr, Johnson, Gans, & Krumrine, 2004). As a result, more recent research has begun to examine the pivotal role of adjustment, especially during students' first year, in predicting college completion (Grant-Vallone, Reid, Umali, & Pohlert, 2003-2004). Theoretical Framework As a guide in examining the influence of personal and interpersonal factors in predicting college adjustment, our study used social capital theory which posits that relationships allow individuals to gain access to resources they would not be able to access on their own (Coleman, 1988; Kao, 2004). Specifically, social capital is considered a resource that a person can accumulate over his or her lifetime and that can be operationalized in an effort to benefit the self (Wall, Ferrazzi, & Schryer, 1998). For example, an individual whose social network provides information about college, perhaps because both parents have attended college, may have a very different understanding of the strategies required to successfully adjust to college than an individual whose social network does not provide access to such resources. Further, Kao (2004) argues that there may be differences between ethnic groups in that some groups may be more limited in the networks of individuals from whom they can draw resources. Yet, it is important to underscore that being a member of a minority group does not automatically ensure that an individual has access to less social capital. For example, the familism that characterizes Hispanic families can be an important form of social capital. Yet, if the knowledge that is transmitted through those relationships does not provide specific information regarding how to navigate the college context, the individual, although emotionally supported by family, may not have access to relevant instrumental information that can assist him or her in adjusting to the demands of the college environment. Literature Review Based on social capital theory, the success of students' adjustment and subsequent achievement would rely on the resources they can access once in the college context. We will be examining the individual resources that students bring with them to the college environment as well as the interpersonal resources that they can access during the initial adjustment period. Specifically, we will be examining self-esteem, parental support, parental education, and peer support as predictors of college adjustment.
