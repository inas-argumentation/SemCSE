As María Celia Bravo shows in her excellent chapter, the existence of a small landowning peasantry dating from colonial times limited the expansion of sugar plantations (ingenios). By 1850, as demand for sugar grew, the peasants shifted their production first from subsistence to market agriculture—to supply the ingenios and the growing population of the province—and later to sugar cane growing itself. By the end of the century these small landowners (called locally cañeros independientes) controlled 42 percent of the lands devoted to sugar cane growing. In sum, the editors of the book should be praised for putting together pieces of extremely good quality that are solidly grounded in archival research. This book is an important addition to the growing literature on Argentina’s agrarian history that is renewing long-held assumptions and endowing its historiography with a sophistication it lacked 20 years ago.
