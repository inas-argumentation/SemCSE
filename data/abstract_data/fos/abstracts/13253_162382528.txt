Theorizing the Hyphen’s Afterlife in Post-Tiananmen Asian-America
It may seem odd to begin theorizing Asian-American fiction by way of observing that 2009 marks the twentieth anniversary of the 1989 Tiananmen Square massacre, but such are the oblique affilia- tions produced by globalization. This essay focuses on Tiananmen's ongoing diasporization as a new reality confronting contemporary Asian-American literary studies. In response, I propose we develop a bilateral hermeneutics of reading Asian-American texts geopoliti- cally and biopolitically, a model I will call the hyphen's afterlife. Al- though most current Asian-American scholarship tends to eliminate the hyphen, I have deliberately reincarnated it in this essay, not simply to deploy it as a visual metaphor for our reading practices, but also to highlight my argument that the conceptual premises of the hyphen's vanishing must now face new geopolitical realities, and to suggest that we in turn can productively reencounter the hyphen in its spectralized afterlife. In Tiananmen's Asian-American textual afterlife, we rendezvous again with history not only at its points and moments of occurrence but also in a year of remote and displaced convergences: 1976.
