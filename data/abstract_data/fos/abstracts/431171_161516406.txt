The Art of Scandal: Modernism, Libel Law and the Roman à Clef (review)
One theoretical quandary takes shape in these pages. Francis locates antiromantic female novelists, by virtue of their distance from anticolonial hope, as uniquely positioned to account for sexual violence, the overcoming of which could direct Caribbean societies to a more just future. Her text suggests that it is precisely their invisibility within the anticolonial nationalist milieu that untethers these women subjectively and endows them with the will to undertake their creative destruction. And yet in making romance the central referent of this transgressive cultural production, she situates the latter within the terms of the anticolonial debate from which she elsewhere claims women were exempted. The term "antiromance" highlights the novelists' antagonism toward historic modes of domination and suggests the desire to realize the unachieved vision of anticolonialism; but Francis's account of the dislocations of these women suggests that their efforts are directed less at critiquing the nation for the sake of ascertaining inclusion on its terms than they are at creating a new generic space in which Jamaicans, Dominicans, Trinidadians, Haitians, and others can recognize themselves as the arbiters of a destiny the terms of which were never before discernible. Readers may feel certain that this astute and uncompromising proponent of the "ethics of writing and reading" (145) will continue to develop her trailblazing account of what is evidently an exciting and radically innovative period in Caribbean feminist poetics and criticism.
