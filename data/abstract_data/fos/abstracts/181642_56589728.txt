Pharmacometrics: The Science of Quantitative Pharmacology.
Drug development continues to be time consuming, expensive, and inefficient, while pharmacotherapy is often practiced at a suboptimal level of performance. This trend has not waned despite the fact that vast amounts of data are obtained during clinical trials and patient care each year. Within these data, knowledge that would improve drug development and pharmacotherapy lays hidden and undiscovered. With the publication of the Guidance for Industry: Population Pharmacokinetics by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and recently the FDA Critical Path Initiative: The Critical Path to New Medical Products, the assimilation of pharmacometrics (PM) as an applied science in drug development and evaluation is increasing. Pharmacometrics is the science of interpreting and describing pharmacology in a quantitative fashion. It lives at the intersection of pharmacokinetic models, pharmacodynamic models, pharmacodynamic–biomarker-outcomes link models, statistics, stochastic simulation, data visualization, and computer programming. The application of pharmacometric principles and models to drug development and pharmacotherapy will greatly improve both. 
 
Although a great deal has been written in the journal literature on this research area, there is no major reference textbook that pulls all these facets of knowledge together in one volume for pharmacometricians in academia, industry, regulatory agencies, and graduate students/postdoctoral fellows who work/research in this subject area. Furthermore, the pharmaceutical industry is integrating pharmacometrics into its development program, but there is a lack of and need for experienced pharmacometricians since fewer and fewer academic programs exist to train them. It is for this purpose that this book is written. 
 
Edited and written by an international team of editors and authors who are experts in the field, this landmark text is the first to pull together and present in one volume the many facets of pharmacometrics. Readers gain the knowledge base needed to perform pharmacometric analyses, interpret the results, and communicate these results to key decision makers. In particular, this book emphasizes the role of pharmacometrics in drug development, reflecting the field's enormous potential to increase drug development productivity. Many examples have been provided to show how pharmacometric principles have been applied in drug development and pharmacotherapy with great benefit. 
 
Following an introduction that sets forth key landmarks in the history of pharmacometrics, this book is divided into 7 sections. Part I involves general principles that addresses topics such as the general principles of programming, pharmacometric analysis software validation, linear and nonlinear mixed effects modeling, estimation of the dynamics of compliance, graphical display of population data, the epistemology of pharmacometrics, and data imputation. 
 
Part II presents various aspects of population pharmacokinetics, pharmacometric knowledge discovery, and resampling techniques used in pharmacometric data analysis. Thus, various aspects of the informative design and analysis of population pharmacokinetic studies are addressed together with population pharmacokinetics estimation methods. Part III emphasizes the pharmacokinetics–pharmacodynamics relationship, which deals with biomarkers and surrogates in drug development, gene expression analysis, integration of pharmacogenomics into pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, empirical and mechanistic PK/PD models, outcome models, and disease progression models. Part IV addresses building on the knowledge gained from the previous sections to provide the basis for designing clinical trials. The section opens with a chapter on the design of first-time-in-human (FTIH) studies for nononcology indications. Part V is devoted to pharmacometric knowledge creation, which entails the application of pharmacometric methodologies to the characterization of an unexplored region of the response surface. The section opens with a chapter on knowledge creation, followed by the theory of clinical trial simulation and the basics of clinical trial simulation, and ends with a chapter on the simulation of efficacy trials. Parts VI discusses what a pharmacometric service is all about and how to communicate the results of a pharmacometric analysis. Parts VII presents specific examples ranging from applications in a regulatory setting, characterization of QT interval prolongation, pharmacometrics in biologics development, pharmacometrics in pediatric pharmacotherapy, application of pharmacometric principles to the analysis of preclinical data, physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling, characterizing metabolic and nonlinear pharmacokinetics, in vitro in vivo correlation, and the application of pharmacometric knowledge, discovery, and creation to the characterization of drug safety. 
 
A reader-friendly approach was taken in the writing of this book. Although there are many contributors to the book, the editors have endeavored to unify the style of presentation to aid the reader's understanding of the subject matter covered in each chapter. In addition to its straightforward and clear presentation of methodology and theory, the text features application examples with programming codes in NONMEM, S-PLUS, WinNonlin, and MATLAB. The reader is able to reproduce the examples themselves and gain a deeper understanding of both the theory and principles of pharmacometrics covered in a particular chapter. Before reading this book, the reader is assumed to be familiar with basic concepts of pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and statistics. If not, the reader is referred to some standard textbooks recommended by the editors (Preface xiii). 
 
Clinical pharmacokineticists/pharmacologists in drug development and pharmacotherapy, as well as graduate students or postdoctoral fellows in clinical pharmacology, clinical pharmacy, and pharmaceutical sciences, should take advantage of this text to gain the training and knowledge needed to excel in pharmacometrics. More experienced pharmacometricians will want to keep the flagship text on hand to address questions and further develop their skills.
