The Regulation of Meat Supply in Sixteenth-Century Mexico City
An important aspect of Spanish colonial administration was the regulation of matters pertaining to meat, grain, and many other commodities. This study is a case history of the slaughterhouse business in sixteenth-century Mexico City. Investigation reveals that the Spaniards made considerable progress in coping with problems of sanitation, supply, and fair regulation in food production. Spanish authorities enacted many ordinances, in characteristically minute detail, relative to the slaughter and sale of meat. This basic industry received careful and intelligent attention. The cabildo had immediate jurisdiction over the meat business. The notary of the cabildo was required to make regular reports to the viceroy of conditions in meat markets; and the town councilmen could take no action contrary to the orders of the viceroy.1 It is to be noted that in the regulation of this business consideration was given to the preservation of the supply, the sanitary handling of the product, and its fair distribution. Every precaution was taken to insure cleanliness, fair weight, and equitable price. No person was permitted to slaughter livestock and sell the meat without a license granted by the viceroy or royal audiencia. The highest official and the oidores were charged by a royal cedula not to grant licenses to slaughter beasts unless it was for the convenience of the government and the benefit of the public.2 They were empowered to revoke licenses when the occasion required it. Often the breeders of herds would slaughter old or worthless animals contrary to the ordinances. In such cases the licenses were revoked; an individual offender lost all animals he had slaughtered, was fined two hundred pesos, and was exiled for two years from the community in which he lived.3
