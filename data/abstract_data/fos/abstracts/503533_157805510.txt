Evolution and Stasis: The Institutional Economics of David Hamilton
The demise of institutional economics has been proclaimed on more than a few occasions. For the most part the eulogies are delivered by traditional economists. The history related is one of an intellectual comet that burned brightly, but briefly, and then died a natural death. Institutional economics, it is claimed, had its fifteen minutes of fame during the 1920s and possibly into the 1930s, but thereafter lost standing and voice. Even some economists sympathetic to institutionalism depict a similar version of events. Malcolm Rutherford, for example, has written of the acceptance and influence achieved by institutional economics in the interwar period of the twentieth century. He has maintained that its decline began in the 1930s and that by the late 1940s institutional economics was "dying, if not already dead." Among the reasons cited by Rutherford for this turn of events are the splintering of the movement, the incorporation by the mainstream of many institutionalist issues, the loss of some of the connections of institutionalism to other disciplines, a misreading by institutionalists of the causes and depth of the depression, and the impact of Keynesian economics (Rutherford 2000, 298-300). While this reading of history may have some validity, I believe that it is lacking in certain important respects. The strength of institutional economics lies in the explanatory power of its ideas, not the force of its numbers. If its membership and influence dwindled in the post World War II period, the reason does not seem to me to lie in an attrition of ideas. Nor should the cause be sought in ideological differences among institutional economists.
