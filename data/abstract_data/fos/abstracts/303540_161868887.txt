Creating East and West is an intellectual history and for the most part its author is careful not to make unwarranted claims about any relationship between humanist rhetoric and the reality of Muslim and Christian relations, and this is as it should be. In several instances, she does cross the line and tries to draw conclusions on the openness and tolerance of Ottoman society, and on the intellectual environment and the cultural diversity of Mehmet II’s court. These asides are not well developed nor particularly compelling, but neither are they central to the book’s argument. In addition to this small quibble, there are also several surprising omissions in the bibliography, particularly Paolo Preto’s flawed, but important Venezia e i turchi, as well as a number of essential recent works by Ottomanists, including Halil Inalcik, Suraiya Faroqhi, and Kate Fleet. These observations should not detract from my overall admiration for this book, however. It is carefully researched, and develops a nuanced and subtle argument that portrays the complexity and variability of the West’s intellectual response to the Ottoman challenge. It also underscores the importance of this period for the evolution of concepts such as East and West, Europe and Asia, and suggests how these Renaissance views influenced early modern attitudes, and indeed may still may inform the modern discourse on Islam and the West. ERIC DURSTELER Brigham Young University
