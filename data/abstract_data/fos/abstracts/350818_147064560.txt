Breaking Racial Barriers: Part III: Scholarships for Negro Students in Predominantly White Colleges and Universities
A LTHOUGH most institutions of higher education in the United States profess to pay no attention to race, they make racial distinctions in the matter of awarding scholarships to students. Many white colleges' which enroll Negro students indicate that while they have no formal arrangements for awarding scholarships to Negroes, almost all Negroes receive aid, and the scholarship committee tends informally to reserve funds for them. In consequence, many white colleges operate on a double standard, officially recognizing no racial differences but unofficially scrutinizing applications for signs that the applicants are Negroes and need special help. It seems impossible to encourage Negro students to seek an education if they cannot be identified as Negroes. As the result of failure to include information concerning racial origin on application forms, they may lose scholarship advantage which might otherwise help to diminish the inequality between the races. Denying that any difference exists, on the one hand, and acknowledging that it is real, on the other, causes a dichotomy in college administration and student personnel services that will be difficult to overcome and will work to the disadvantage of the Negro. In spite of this general denial in college life that the Negro, as a member of a different race, should receive different treatment from that given the white student, numerous institutions seek ways around the double standard and encourage special scholarship help for qualified Negro students. Of the I,300 white colleges in the United States which responded to an inquiry made in I964 by the Institute of Higher Education, Teachers College, Columbia University, 144 stated that they did give scholarships specifically to Negro students.
