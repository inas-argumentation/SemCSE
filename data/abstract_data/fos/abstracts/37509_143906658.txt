I describe the unit, focusing primarily on its planning and implementation and also offer a series of general reflections about the nature of teaching and schooling, especially at the middle school level. The town hall in this small exurban community is normally not a very busy place, except on those days when residents exercise their two most basic forms of political participation: voting and paying local taxes. Otherwise, the town clerk and her staff go about their business in a quiet manner with few interruptions of their regular routines. At least twice a year, however, things change, dramatically so, when the seventh-grade students of the local public middle school take over the hall’s main meeting room and transform it into a museum. That is the culminating activity in an extended, interdisciplinary unit on early American history, which students and teachers alike refer to simply as “Expo.” The simplicity of the unit’s name is deceptive, however, for it masks the rich and varied educational experiences involved. Although Expo culminates with fifty or so students working together to plan and install a museum, it begins about three months earlier with a much more traditional assignment: the time-honored history term paper. The stages through which those papers are researched and written and then recast and re-presented as museum exhibits offer a model of exemplary middle school instruction, one in which students actively engage in the complex cognitive processes of constructing meaning for themselves, rather than merely memorizing—and soon forgetting— selected sets of factual information.
