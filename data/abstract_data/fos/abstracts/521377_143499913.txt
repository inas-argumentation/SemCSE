Effect of Stimulus-Defined and Response-Defined Motives Upon Pursuit Rotor Performance
The relationship between motivational disposition and motivation induced in a given situation has long been recognized to be extremely complex. The variable of anxiety, for example, is typically studied in terms of responses given by a person on a questionnaire. Although care is currently taken to distinguish long-term from immediate response tendencies, the traditional approach to the study of anxiety is nevertheless still pursued and involves a response-definition of motivation. Thus, anxiety can be measured as a general trait (Taylor, 1953) or as a set of characteristic responses to a specific situation like test taking (Mandler & Sarason, 1952). Moreover, facilitating anxiety can be distinguished from debilitating anxiety in test-taking situations (Alpert & Haber, 1960). Most of the recent research emphasis has been on distinguishing state and trait anxiety. However, even in these studies, which focus on the state anxiety as highly specific to the immediate research setting, the anxiety is still largely response defined (e.g., Stoudenmire, 1972).
