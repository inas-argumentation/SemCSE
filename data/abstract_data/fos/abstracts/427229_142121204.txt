College Student Anti-Semitism and Anti-Israeli Sentiment
Antisemitism has been investigated since 1933 (Katz & Braly). College students have relatively consistently stereotyped Jewish people through 1969. Currently in 2009, 32 countries have reported antisemitic acts. Rather than openly expressing hostility toward Jewish people, it is more politically acceptable to express such anger to Jews by being anti-Israeli. Antisemitism and anti-Israeli attitudes in college students were significantly correlated both in a large university on the east coast with ethnically diverse students and in a smaller university in the southwest with mostly Caucasian students. ********** College students' negative stereotypes of Jewish people historically have been examined over thirty-six years from 1933-1969. A foundation study by Katz and Braly (1933) demonstrated that Jews are perceived by Princeton University college students as shrewd mercenary and grasping. In 1951 Gilbert found that Princeton students viewed Jews as shrewd and mercenary and in 1969 Karlins, Coffman, and Walters demonstrated that Princeton students saw Jews as shrewd and materialistic. Dunbar (1995) demonstrated that antisemitism existed in a group of UCLA white students but not in Asian students.=///// In 2007 there were documented antisemitic acts in 17 countries (Simon & Schaler, 2007). In 2009 there are such acts in 32 countries (Simon & Schaler, 2009). In 2009 the London conference on antisemitism reported that antisemitism is at record levels since World War Two. In January 2009, two hundred and fifty antisemitic acts occurred in the world (Simon & Schaler, 2009). However, antisemitism is not always on the surface. Overt racism and negative prejudice against minority groups is often below the surface, hidden from societal punishment (McConahay, 1986). Except for overt extremist hate groups, most people do not publically advocate the harming of Jews. Another way to express one's hatred of Jews, a way less evident, might be dislike of Israel. Not to say that 100% of anti-Israeli sentiment is motivated by a hatred of Jews, but it is to state that some anti-Israeli attitudes might be seen as motivated by hostility toward Jews. Cohen, Jussim, Harber, and Bhasin (2009) found that pro Israeli sentiment and anti-Semitic attitudes were negatively correlated in a group of ethnically diverse Rutgers University social psychology students. The current study uses a midsized university population of general psychology students who are almost all Caucasian in an attempt to extend the generality of Cohen, et al.'s findings and determine the reliability of their findings. Method Participants There were sixty-eight undergraduate students drawn from the student body of a southwestern university of 6,000 students. The age of the participants ranged from 17 to 42. There were 19% males (n=13) and 81% females (n=55) in the study. The ethnicity of the sample consisted of 1% Asian (n=l), 8% Black (n=5), 2% Hispanic (n=l), 5% Native-American (n=4), and 84% White (n=57). There were 77% freshman (n=52), 10% sophomore (n=7), 1% junior (n=1), 11% senior (n=8). Compensation to these participants was provided in the form of extra credit to be applied to the final course grade in General (Introduction to) Psychology, the course they were enrolled in. Measures The revised Levinson and Sanford (1944) scale (Jones-Wiley, Rotori, Lee, & Ho, 2007) was used to assess the participant's level of prejudice toward Jewish people by self-report. The revised L-S uses a 5-point Likert scale where the total of responses are added yielding a score that when high indicates a smaller level of antisemitism and when low indicates a higher level of antisemitism. The attitudes toward Israel scale (Cohen, et al., 2009) consists of 11 questions each rated 1 to 5. Higher scores indicate a like of Israel.
