The Last Years of Innocent Gentillet: `Princeps Adversariorum Machiavelli'
C CHOLARS directly or indirectly interested in the history of Machiavelli's reputation tend now to regard as exaggerated the importance attributed; in the nineteenth century, to Gentillet's Discours... Contre Nicolas Machiavel Florentin. Nobody, for instance, would be prepared today to share Edward Meyer's view that Gentillet was, 'beyond a doubt, the source of all Elizabethan misunderstanding' of Machiavelli.1 'The grotesque Machiavel-figure of Elizabethan-Jacobean drama' -to quote from one of the most recent works on the subject-'is firmly within the acceptance/rejection theme of reaction to Machiavelli, and not an external phenomenon generated by Gentillet's Contre-Machiavel. Gentillet, despite Simon Patericke's translation, was never of any importance in England. There was never a time when Machiavelli's writings in Italian, Latin, French, or English, in print or in manuscript, were not more readily accessible in England than those of his detractors.'2 It is certainly true that 'both in England and in Europe Gentillet continued a tradition; he did not start one.'3 And it is quite possible that nineteenthcentury scholars overstated the role played by Gentillet in the Machiavelli controversy from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century. Meyer certainly did so. But it is too easily forgotten that Gentillet's importance was very well established before the nineteenth century, before Meyer's book, and even before Mohl's Geschichte und Literatur der Staatswissenschaften,4 which was probably Meyer's main source of inspiration.
