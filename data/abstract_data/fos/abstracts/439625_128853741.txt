Remanent magnetization and magnetic susceptibility anisotropy measurements in Lac des Mille Lacs area of northwestern Ontario: Discussion
In their recent paper on the magnetic susceptibility anisotropy of some metavolcanic rocks in northwestern Ontario, Nwachukwu and Currie concluded that the prolateness factor (P) of the susceptibility ellipsoid increases with increasing amount of paleostrain, and that P "can be used qualitatively to determine areal variations in the magnitude of deformation ..." (1975, p. 743, 1.7). A similar conclusion, based on reciprocal values of P, had been reached by Khan (1962) thirteen years ago. He suggested that "deformations intense enough to produce large (susceptibility) anisotropies tend to produce (magnetically) linear rather than planar structures" (1962, p. 2879, 1.50). But this generalization is at variance with the results of conventional strain analysis. A reliable parameter of prolateness for any magnitude ellipsoid (Nye 1957, p. 47) should be independent of the amount of anisotropy. I will attempt to show that the above factor P is greatly affected by large amounts of susceptibility anisotropy, and that P should not be used as a measure of prolateness if these amounts are unknown. Furthermore, the label P has been attached to different parameters in the literature on magnetic susceptibility anisotropy, which was not recognized by Nwachukwu and Currie (1975). Only if a suite of deformed rocks has constant proleteness of the susceptibility ellipsoid, may the factor P be used as a measure of strain magnitude. The shape of any triaxial ellipsoid with principal semi-axes K, > K2 > K3 can be described by combination of two independent parameters : (1) the amount of anisotropy (departure from sphericity), and (2) a prolateness ratio.
