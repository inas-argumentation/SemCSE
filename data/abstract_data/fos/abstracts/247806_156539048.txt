Financial Innovation as Facilitator of Merger Activity
In The Theory of Business Enterprise in 1904, Thorstein Veblen developed an explanation of the relationship among the rise of a national market, the phenomenon of destructive price competition, and the merger movement, which produced the modern corporate structure of American enterprise. Veblen described the evolution of financial practices through stock-financed mergers as the solution to a business crisis caused by the technological advances of the late nineteenth century. He argued that a change in the method of capitalizing business assets, coupled with the adoption of innovative financial instruments, allowed the rapid reorganization of industry. Capitalization in excess of tangible assets-made possible through the application of trust certificates, preferred stock, debenture bonds, and/or collateral trust bonds-was crucial to the mass creation of large horizontal mergers. 1 While several elements of his argument, most notably those dealing with the business environment and the revolutions in technology, communication, and transportation, have been generally accepted by later generations of writers, his unifying hypothesis has not enjoyed similar success. The purpose of this paper is to outline this argument, making only cursory reference to those elements that have been accepted, while providing evidence of the changing methods of capitalization during the merger movement of 1895-1904. The evidence presented in support of Veblen's hypothesis is based on the analysis of 517 mergers occurring between 1870 and 1904 and the detailed plans for 339 of these.
