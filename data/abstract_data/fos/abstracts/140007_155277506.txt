Impeachment and the Dynamics of Public Opinion: A Comment on "Guilty, Yes; Impeachment, No"
It can safely be said that Watergate will preoccupy political scientists for some time to come. While its permanent political effects, if any, remain to be seen, it is possible to monitor Watergate's short-term political impact and to speculate about its likely influence in the near future. In "Guilty, Yes; Impeachment, No," Professor McGeever has presented a detailed description of Watergate's effect on public opinion. Accordingly, it seems worthwhile to examine his data for evidence of possible trends in public sentiment on impeachment and for clues to the probable electoral repercussions of Watergate. McGeever's sample consists of two polar groups on the question of Nixon's guilt and removal from office-those who think him guilty and want him removed, and those who think him innocent-and a third, rather ambivalent group-those who think him guilty but oppose his removal from office. Are the members of the "Guilty-Yes/Remove-No" group incorrigible cynics willing to sacrifice political justice for political stability? There is no doubt that McGeever's data fit that interpretation.
