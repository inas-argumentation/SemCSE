In section two of a 2002 amendment on the Undergraduate Science, Mathematics, Engineering, and Technology Education Improvement Act, congress stated "A workforce that is highly trained in science ... is crucial to generating the innovation that drives economic growth ..." (Committee on Science, 2002). Given the growth in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) careers and the exodus of aging and highly skilled workers from these fields as projected by the United States Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), fulfilling this order is likely to be challenging. In 2005, The BLS projected that between 2004 and 2014, employers in the United States would be hiring 2.5 million STEM workers (BLS, 2005). Despite this looming deficit, some STEM fields in the United States are actually graduating fewer bachelor's degree students than 20 years ago (NSF, 2006). In 2004, 64,675 students earned engineering bachelor degrees compared to 76,153 in 1984. Bachelor's degrees in Physical sciences totaled 14,240 in 2004, while they reached 15,831 in 1984. This emphasizes the importance of increasing student interest in science in order to ensure we will be capable of filling the projected openings and maintaining competitive in the global market. Theoretical Framework: Developing student interest in science is made difficult largely due to misconceptions that K-12 students have about science and scientists. Research on students' perceptions of scientists has demonstrated that students do not have a clear perception of what science has to offer them or what scientists actually do. In 1957, Mead and Matraux, analyzed student essays that detailed their ideas about scientists. They found that students perceived scientists as old, white males working in a laboratory performing dangerous experiments. This has become the classic stereotype of scientists. Variations of this study have been conducted several times since and still the stereotype persists (Bassala, G., 1976; Beardsley, D., and O'Dowd, D., 1961; Chambers, D., 1983; Etzioni, A., and Nunn, C., 1974; Hills, E, and Shallis, M., 1975; Rodriguez, M., 1975). Much of the work has centered on students' perceptions of scientists and frequently this is measured with the Draw-a-Scientist-Test (DAST) developed by Chambers (1983). Students are asked to draw a picture of a scientist and these pictures are then compared to a list of stereotypical images developed by Mead and Metraux (1957). A checklist was developed from this list (DAST-C) which enables researchers to quantify image elements and analyze data from the students' drawings using statistical analysis (Finson, Beaver & Cramond, 1995). Finson, Pedersen, and Thomas (2006) used the DAST-C to investigate the relationship between teaching styles and middle school students' perceptions of scientists. No relationship was detected in the analysis, which seems to suggest that the measured perceptions are deeply engrained and are not influenced greatly by teaching methods. This study seems to suggest that regardless of how constructivist the classroom experience is, students do not have accurate perceptions of scientists or science. The DAST or variations of it have been used to demonstrate student perceptions many times over (Huber & Burton, 1995; Mason, Khale & Gardner, 1991; Ward, 1986). The cumulative results of these studies demonstrate that student images of scientists are generally negative (Finson, 2002; Schibeci, 1986). In attempt to take a more in-depth look at student views of scientists, Palmer (1997), interviewed students to find out whether students had other ideas about scientists, apart from the stereotyped images depicted in the DAST. One of the conclusions that came from his interviews is that students do not view science as having personal relevance to them. These misconceptions can be damaging to the science community in that it may lead individuals to enter science under false pretenses only to leave later on. …
