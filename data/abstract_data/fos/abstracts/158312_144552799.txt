The writer has found numerous studies in which matching of the groups has proceeded on the basis of antecedent variables such MA, IQ, CA, and length of institutionalization, and in which the effects of such matching are then ignored in the subsequent statistical analyses, with the result that interpretations of the studies’ findings were seriously beclouded. In many such studies randomized blocking, analyses of covariance (Ancova), or correlational and discriminant analyses would have been preferable. Perhaps the reason that analysis of covariance, for one, is so seldom employed is that there are so few examples in mental retardation literature to serve as guides to its employment, though many statistics textbooks explain and illustrate the technique (e.9. Cochran & Cox, 1957; Edwards, 1960; Lindquist, 1953; McNemar, 1962; Walker & Lev, 1953; and Winer, 1962). In the present paper the author proposes to explore instances and situations when matching may be either suitable or unsuitable, or where other techniques are more effective. He will compare matching with alternate solutions, e.g., with those obtained from analyses of variance and covariance in a number of simple experimental paradigms and then consider the problems of. “equating” groups in more complex designs.
