Republicanism in Political Rhetoric Before 1776
Few terms in the rhetoric of American politics are as uncontroversial as republic. It appears on title pages of countless history books, but finds its most frequent usefulness in oratory which may best be described as the opposite of critical analysis. In such a context republic is unfailingly used to evoke emotional attachment to the less appealing abstractions "government" and "state." This role for the term republic in political "positive thinking" grew out of the revolutionary experience of the founding generation. It was, indeed, one of the radical achievements of the American Revolution to have developed an "ideological attachment to republicanism."1 Only in 1776 did republic, republican, and republicanism change from defamatory cliches used to stigmatize critics of the existing order to terms with affirmative connotations, stimulating a feeling of identification with the existing political system.
