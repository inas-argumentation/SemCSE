The intervention in the democratic republic of Congo
The crisis in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), which was known as Zaire prior to 1997, has been a long-running saga. In fact the outbreaks of violence in eastern DRC during April-May 2003 that prompted a UN Security Council Resolution and the creation of a European Union (EU) multinational intervention force are but the latest in a decade-long string of violence, human rights abuses and humanitarian suffering. The international community's response to the wave of violence that erupted in 2003 is illustrative of a continuing reluctance on the part of the UN and EU to respond effectively in the early stages of humanitarian emergencies that involve civil conflict. However, as 'late' as they were, the military actions that the EU eventually undertook resulted in some important tactical successes that may serve as useful guidelines for future humanitarian interventions. In order to understand the UN and EU actions and their strategic implications, a brief review of the 2003 crisis is required. In some respects, the crisis began when the Cold War ended. Until then the United States (US) and the rest of the Western bloc provided political and financial support for the regime of Zairian President Mobutu Sese Seko. Although his regime was widely acknowledged as grossly corrupt and repressive, Mobutu was able to parlay his staunch anti-communism into Western support as long as there were Soviet and Cuban inspired/supported insurgency movements elsewhere in Africa. As the Cold War wound down, Western financial support dwindled and the incompetence of the Mobutu regime became impossible to ignore. Abundant in natural resources, the DRC (Zaire) economy nevertheless endured an extended downturn due to the corruption and ineffectiveness of the regime and the increasing disorder caused by tensions between the government and separatist areas and among various ethnic groups. Due to economic mismanagement, the country's GDP was about the same in 1997 as it was in 1958.
