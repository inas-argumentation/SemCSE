These footnotes (along with Kendal’s marginal glosses) also provide a wealth of supplementary information (far more than Nicoll’s edition), elucidating Chapman’s meaning, pointing out a reliance on or deviation from Spondanus’s 1583 edition of Homer, and detailing additions. For Chapman, “Engaging with the Odyssey is not meant to be an easy task” (34), but modern readers will be grateful for the degree of additional assistance proffered by Kendal’s new edition, which preserves but also usefully augments Chapman, just as the translator himself had sought to represent and build on Homer. Kendal states that Chapman sought “to bring Homer’s translucence within the reader’s grasp” (29) and this edition (recently joined by an edition of Chapman’s Iliad, ed. Robert Miola) does something similar for Chapman’s work, endowing a multifaceted, challenging, and important early modern poem with a new level of accessibility.
