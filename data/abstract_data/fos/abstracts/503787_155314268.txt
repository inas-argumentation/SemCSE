The United Nations, International Conflict, and American Security
When the United Nations came into being in 1945 "determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, which twice in our lifetime has brought untold sorrow to mankind," few people in this country doubted that the fledgling organization would play a major role in creating an international environment of peace and security. It would not, of course, provide fully for the common defense of the United States. It would, instead, make that task vastly less difficult and burdensome by establishing principles and machinery that would resolve dangerous conflict situations by peaceful means or suppress aggression by collective enforcement action and in view of the veto always subject to United States consent. For the past forty-one years this expectation has been tested against the hard facts of international politics. The outcome of the process has been a wide-ranging reassessment of the role of the organization, culminating this year in a major crisis in U.S.-UN relations. The United Nations may emerge from its present ordeal a leaner, stronger, and more efficient organization; alternatively, its effectiveness may be irreversibly impaired. The question is, of course, whether this process will extinguish the dream of preserving international peace and security through a world organization of governments.
