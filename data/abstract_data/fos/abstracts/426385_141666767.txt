Work Sampling Study of an Engineering Professor during a Regular Contract Period.
Work sampling is a technique that has been employed in industry and fields such as healthcare for some time. It is a powerful technique, and an alternative to conventional stop watch time studies, used by industrial engineers to focus upon random work sampling observations. This study applies work sampling to the duties performed by an individual faculty member, self-monitored during the regular academic year within an engineering school at a primarily liberal arts university in north Texas. This methodology could be applied by faculty throughout academia where it could be used as a tool by administration to better understand the work load content and tasks which occupy their time. It also may be used by individual faculty members to realign their own work tasks based upon the data obtained. 1 Introduction Typically, four different techniques for developing labor or production standards are used. These include historical experience, time studies, predetermined time standards, and work sampling. Work sampling was developed in England by L. Tippet in the 1930s [1, pg. 403]. Work sampling has traditionally been applied to industry. It involves observing a portion of the work activity at random intervals. Work sampling in many cases only requires a glimpse of the activity. A regular stop watch time study requires continuous observations by the time study analyst. The three questions that have to be answered in work sampling are how much statistical confidence is desired in the results, how many observations are necessary and at what times should the observations be made [2, pg. 195]. The industrial applications are used for determining ratio delay (idle time versus running time of a machine), performance index of a worker and the determination of the standard time of work [2, pg. 194-195], It can be used also to determine how employees are allocating their time among various duties, which may lead to staffing changes, reassignment of duties. Work sampling is used in many other different fields ranging from healthcare to logging operations. Miyata et al. noted that random sampling has a disadvantage compared to continuous observation in regular time studies when logging equipment is widely scattered or where visibility is poor since the random observations may occur at such short intervals that the observer cannot adequately observe all the equipment, but is cheaper when visibility is good [3, pg. 6]. It should be noted that in this research paper the work sampling is applied by a faculty member on himself at an engineering school of his own work schedule. It was decided that the faculty member would act as the analyst because it would be virtually impossible and cost prohibitive to have somebody follow him around performing the study for an entire semester at random intervals. It should be noted that work sampling studies that rely on self-reported logs are generally considered least reliable, since workers may not record activities in a timely fashion, and may not be totally frank concerning what activities were being done at the specified sampling times (Lurie, Rank, Parenti, et al, 1989) [4, pg. 2], The author of this paper decided that there are no "good" or "bad" activities and he decided to record the results as timely and objective as possible. The analyst decided at the beginning of the study to be completely impartial on his own results. A self observation study was discussed by Benjamin W. Niebel [5, pg. 545]. This research paper discusses how the current study in this research paper was performed differently and will be used differently than the study discussed by Niebel. It would of course be better to use an independent analyst, but this is impractical and financially not possible in this long case study.
