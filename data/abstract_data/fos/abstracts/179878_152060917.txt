For example: (a) does microcredit reduce poverty rates permanently?; (b) is microcredit capable of fixing more fundamental problems associated with poverty such as poor medical treatment, inadequate education system, corrupt governments?; and (c) is the group lending method the most effective way to make micro loans (a topic discussed in Chapter 9)? Microcredit is the focus of Chapters 6, 8, and 9. Because of microcredit's recent rise in popularity there are many organizations (The World Bank, for one) espousing microcredit loans. Indeed, these loans are a purposeful and inventive tool. However, by itself microcredit is incapable of significantly reducing poverty rates permanently. Microcredit should be one of many tools implemented to make credit effectively available to poor households. Unfortunately, microcredit has worked less successfully in developed countries such as the United States and those in Europe. Some believe this is because the group lending technique works well in small communities typically found in developing countries but is less effective in larger metropolitan areas frequently found in the United States and Europe. Anyone interested in credit markets, poverty, and inequality will find this book interesting. But, as is typically the case, not all chapters in this book are of equal quality. For example, Chapter 5 is a specialized study that found when Italy's court systems are inefficient there is a reduction in the amount of credit available to poor people. While this chapter is interesting by itself, its narrow scope makes one doubt the importance of its conclusions and the value of its inclusion in this book. One last complaint is that too few chapters provide convincing solutions to the problems they investigate. It would have raised the book's value if all authors made clear recommendations concerning how to solve the problems they investigate. Regardless, it is pleasing to know these topics are being discussed openly among a variety of academics and professionals.
