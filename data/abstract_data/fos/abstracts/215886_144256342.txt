The Insertion of Rural Areas into Global Markets: A Comparison of Garment Production in Yucatán and La Laguna, Mexico
This article examines the recent trends in extending the global garment production process to the rural areas of the Laguna region and the Yucatain Peninsula in Mexico. As these remote regions are incorporated into global markets, several similar patterns and rationale are found behind the policies followed by state governments to attract these types of enterprises. The implications for regional economic development are also discussed. Since the early 198os, when the Mexican government abandoned its importsubstitution industrial policies and retreated from participation in many economic activities, a sharp shift in policy orientation has taken place. Under the new economic model (NEM), the Mexican government began to see the maquila as an integrated part of export-led development.1 Initially, maquiladoras could only be located in the border towns. In recent years, rapid technological change, the elimination of tariffs under North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), and changes in Mexican legislation, have combined to create new opportunities for export-processing plants located away from the border. These regions depended on a single agricultural commodity for most of their history, but they have undergone dramatic changes over the past decades, at least partly due to the adoption of neo-liberal policies in Mexico. Most recently, the rural areas of both regions have been incorporated into the industrialisation process, as large numbers of rural (garment) factories were established throughout both regions. This article investigates the local effects in the Laguna region and the Henequen Zone of the globalisation process in one of the most globalised industries: the garment industry.2 These regions are two of the most notable examples of recently developed, dynamic garment clusters, exhibiting a marked boom in export production since NAFTA came into effect. The garment cluster of the Laguna region in north central Mexico and the one in the Henequen Zone in southeast Mexico are taken as points of entry for the investigation of the impact of industrial development in the rural areas. The boom in garment production has had a significant impact on both regions in a variety of ways. Both have been faced with serious problems in the rural areas due to a change in patterns of agricultural production over the past few decades. The garment industry has been one of the main vehicles for the creation of much needed employment opportunities in the rural areas of both regions. However, this article will show there is reason to believe rural garment factories may in the long run not live up to the high expectations created by the state governments. The next section outlines the economic structure of both regions and the background to the proliferation of garment plants. The third section discusses the recent economic transformations, which in both cases entailed a switch from ailing agriculture concentrated on a limited number of crops to industrial (garment) production for export. The next section addresses the current crisis affecting the rural garment factories and in the final section; the pros and cons of this particular industrialisation strategy with regard to the development of the rural areas in both regions.
