American Foreign Policy and the Limits of Military Might: A Review Essay
This timely RAND Corporation study focuses on threats of military action and on the coercive functions of military operations in war.1 Its publication coincides with increasing acceptance in the policy community of the normalization of force as an instrument of U.S. foreign policy and the revival of scholarly interest in coercive diplomacy. The coercive use of military power-actual or threatened-is as old as war itself, for most wars and war threats have developed within ongoing conflicts where the adversaries were unable to resolve their disputes through bargaining and nonviolent inducements, including economic coercion. The initiation of war has often been seen as a means of getting the adversary to change its behavior. Rarely is total destruction of the enemy the purpose at war's outset, although once underway, overwhelming and unconditional victory can become the objective. These interactions between the political and military dimensions of conflict, what Daniel Byman and Matthew Waxman call "the dynamics of coercion," were integral to the power plays among the ancient Greek city states and were at the core of Thucydides' analysis in The Peloponnesian Wars. Such dynamics were also captured in Carl von Clausewitz's famous aphorism about war being "a true political instrument, a continuation of political intercourse, carried on with other means."
