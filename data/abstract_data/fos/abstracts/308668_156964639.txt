Collective Ownership, Property Rights, and Control of the Corporation
The evolution of large corporations has been accompanied by changes in the institutions that distribute decision-making authority within them. In particular, property rights, which less than a hundred years ago were predominant, have been attenuated to an exceedingly secondary position. At the turn of the century, Max Weber was convinced that control of enterprises would necessarily be based on private property: "Centralized control over the process as a whole is functionally essential to efficiency, and thus control cannot exist without what we consider property rights" (paraphrased by Parsons, in Weber [1947, p. 45]. But in 1932, Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means pointed out that ownership and control were becoming separated, and by about 1960 the conventional (although not unquestioned-see Zeitlin [1974]) wisdom held that ownership was virtually irrelevant to control: "So far as economic efficiency is concerned, it will already be clear that no general statement about ... ownership can ever be justified.... Efficiency has little to do with ownership because in the modern corporation ownership has little to do with control" [Crossland 1963, p. 325].
