Personal Safety Practices, Beliefs and Attitudes of Academic Faculty on a Small University Campus: Comparison of Males and Females (Part 1)
The current study reports on a study examining the safety concerns of male and female faculty members on a small university campus. A 160-item questionnaire was distributed to 100 faculty members (58 males and 42 females; response rate was approximately 30%), which asked individuals questions pertaining to socio-demographic information, daily routines and campus activities, awareness of campus services related to safety issues, personal safety precautions taken while on campus, attitudes and beliefs about safety on campus, and finally reported cases of victimization on campus. The findings indicated that not only did women feel more threatened on campus than men did, they also had experienced more cases of harassment and violence. Women also took more personal safety precautions and felt that the university should improve various safety features on campus. Clearly, the incidence of harassment and victimization is as prominent if not more so on a small university campus as in larger universities and workplaces. ********** Research has shown high rates of sexual harassment in work and educational institutions (Gutek, 1985; Fitzgerald, Shullman, Bailey, 1988; Richman, Flaherty, Rospenda, & Christensen, 1992; Koss, Goodman, Browne, Fitzgerald, Keita, & Russo, 1994). Although both men and women may experience sexual harassment, findings indicate that women are more likely to be sexually harassed (Gruber, 1997; Gutek, 1985). In addition, women are more likely to experience negative consequences of sexual harassment in the workplace, such as quitting or losing a job (Dansky & Kilpatrick, 1997). As well, there is evidence that sexual harassment has both mental health consequences and public health consequences (Fitzgerald, Drasgow, Hulin, Gelfand, & Magley, 1997; Richman, Rospenda, Nawyn, & Flaherty, 1999), including increased incidence of psychosomatic illness, reduced concentration, and negative mood (Barling, Dekker, Loughlin, Kelloway, Fullager, & Johnson, 1996). Unfortunately, the university environment is not immune to either sexual harassment or more generalized violence (Blackston & Fulton, 1975; Cammaert, 1985; Fitzgerald, et al., 1988). Richman et al. (1999) found that among faculty members, females were subjected to higher rates of both harassment and abuse. They also reported linkages of harassment and abuse with depression, anxiety, hostility, frequency of drinking, prescription drug use and cigarette use. Bagilhole and Woodward (1995) reported that just over 25% of women academics indicated that they had experienced sexual harassment (as defined by the participant), with another 40% describing incidents that would be classified as such using Gruber (1992) typology of sexual harassment. McKinney (1990) found that approximately 20% of female faculty and 9% of male faculty have been harassed by another faculty member. Interestingly, McKinney (1990) found that women report more sexual harassment by colleagues, while male faculty report more harassment from students. Perhaps due to the higher incidence of sexual harassment, more women are also more concerned for their safety on university campus than men (Klodawsky & Lundy, 1994). The structure of academic institutions, specifically in the division of power and authority between males and females, may increase the chances of women being isolated and marginalized, particularly as women still find themselves in the situation of being under-represented on university campuses (Kanter, 1977). Others have argued that the university environment may be particularly vulnerable to high rates of harassment due to the relative autonomy of academics, the lack of accountability and the fact that women are still a minority on university campuses (Dzeich & Weiner, 1984).
