Claire Richter Sherman built on nineteenth-century scholarship (1982) and added the necessary dose of feminist art criticism. Three recent studies picked up that thread, each with a different approach. The authors in Therese Martin’s two-volume collection of essays argue that queens made art through patronage and commissioning the works (2012). Mariah Proctor-Tiffany looked at a single Valois queen, Clémence of Hungary, asserting that she literally moved a variety of objects across Europe (2013, 2015). Cynthia Brown analyzed queenship and image making through her study of the prodigious library of Anne of Brittany (2010). Keane’s book is important because it focuses on a range of objects owned by and then donated by an individual queen to reveal both public power and intimate relationships at court. For queenship scholars, Keane’s book is essential reading for both its methodology and its conclusions about the meanings of patronage, politics, and cultural power at court. And it opens fruitful avenues for research into the history of emotions. It will speak to a much wider audience of historians, art historians, and their students, both graduate and advanced undergraduate, who will appreciate her close textual analysis of the testament and her insightful reading of the objects.
