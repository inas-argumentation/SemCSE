In the Florentine Histories (1520–25), he criticized the upper-middle class and vilified the working classes; he said Florence’s military weakness was due to the emasculation of the feudal aristocracy by the upper-middle and middle classes; he called Venice “a republic that, through its institutions and its power, must be celebrated above every other principate in Italy” (book 1, chapter 28). Such a “conservative turn,” to adopt McCormick’s phrase, is confirmed by his “Memorandum to Cardinal Giulio” and “Draft of a law for the reform of the Florentine constitution” (both from 1522), each more conservative than The Prince, the Discourses, or the Discourse on Florentine Affairs, as well as by his respectable final comedy Clizia (1524–25), in contrast to the amoral, antisocial, and egocentric earlier comedy, Mandragola (1515–17). McCormick’s defense, in his view, of the unwaveringly democratic and radical Machiavelli is that he did not mean what he said when he reviled the working classes responsible for the radical Ciompi Revolt (1378), when he praised Venice, when he criticized the Florentine middle classes, or when he advocated a mixed constitution for Florence favoring the aristocracy and upper-middle class. This was, according to McCormick, simply “rhetoric.” Actions “trump” words: the Ciompi did not, in the end, attempt to eliminate but only to lessen the political power of the upper and middle classes; they terrorized but did not murder their opponents. Their “crimes,” “evil,” “arson,” “riots,” “inflamed evil spirits,” threats to “slaughter” their opponents’ “children and raze their houses to the ground” were excused by the “trying times” and the “egregious oppression” they had suffered. On the basis of such a defense, what jury would fail to find the Ciompi guilty? And what jury would fail to take the evidence of what Machiavelli in fact wrote in preference to what his advocate alleges he meant?
