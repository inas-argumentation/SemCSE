Nixon's Visit to China and Increased Favorability toward Communism
During President Nixon's week-long 1972 visit to the People's Republic of China, the television media displayed many of the positive aspects of Communist Chinese society to the American people. According to cognitive dissonance theory, exposure to such televised coverage should have resulted in a reduction of the negative stereotype of "Red China" widely held before the trip This study examined the hypothesis that high levels of exposure to the televised coverage of Nixon's visit increased favorability toward communism. The subjects were 114 male and 108 female undergraduates enrolled in introductory sociology classes at a midwestern stare university during 1972. Since the extent of televised coverage of the president's visit and the level of the audience's attraction to the coverage were essentially "unplanned events," data on attitude change were obtained after the events assumed to generate dissonance had occurred, using procedures similar to those of Cozby (1974) and Bishop (1975). Questionnaires measuring amount of exposure to televised coverage of the visit, level of increased favorability toward communism in China and communism in general, and political orientation were administered during the week following Nixon's visit. Responses to the questions on televised exposure and attitude change were uichotomized. The results supported the hypothesis. Level of exposure to televised coverage was positively related to increased favorability toward communism in China (xP = 20.17, p < .001) and communism in general ( X I a = 15.13, 9 < .01). Further analyses indicated that the relationship between level of televised exposure and increased favorability toward communism existed for both political leftists and rightists. The analyses were inspired by the contemporary growth of political, economic, and cultural contact with socialist societies. However, the results should be interpreted with caution since there is no way to determine whether the reported attirudinal changes were persistent or ternporary or whether attitude changes occurred in the non-college population of the U.S.
