The Electoral Connection Meets the Past: Lessons from Congressional History, 1789-1899
As with the puzzle that all dynamic, complex political organizations present, the modern U.S. Congress has fascinated scholars with the number and variety of its pieces: the decline of involuntary congressional turnover; the increase in members who make Congress their career; the weakening of institutional leadership; the decrease in party voting and other manifestations of party discipline; and the growth in individual legislator's staff, perquisites, and other resources. To solve this puzzle would require making sense of a variety of trends in virtually every dimension of legislative life including members' accountability to the electorate, legislative voting patterns, internal organizational structure, and distribution of institutional and political, power. David Mayhew's remarkable 1974 book, Congress: The Electoral Connection, not only took up the task, but gained wide and lasting acceptance as one of the most important theories of the contemporary Congress. According to Mayhew, the key to the congressional puzzle lies in understanding members' goals of careers in Congress and by extension their goals of reelection.
