New Approach to Fluorescence Spectroscopy of Individual Molecules on Surfaces
In recent years, the detection and spectroscopy of single molecules by laser induced fluorescence under ambient conditions has seen tremendous development [1]. Single molecule detection (SMD) has found many applications, both in the study of fundamental questions on a single molecule level and in analytical applications like fluoroimmunoassays, electrophoresis, and cytometry [2]. The central difficulty in detecting single molecules is not so much the ability to collect and to detect enough of their fluorescence but to be able to efficiently discriminate any source of background signal. Different approaches for improving the signal-to-background ratio have been demonstrated, e.g., using appropriate spectral filters, minimization of the detection volume [3], or employing near-infrared fluorophores [4]. Most experimental work on SMD has been done in the green to red range of wavelengths. However, the majority of naturally occurring fluorophores has excitation maxima in the blue and UV region of the spectrum: The strong dependence of the scattering intensity on wavelength makes SMD of these fluorophores nearly impossible. This is especially regrettable if one considers the potential application of SMD in biological applications, such as studying single protein molecules without attaching fluorophores to them. For example, the three amino acids tryptophan, tyrosin, and phenylalanin show a native fluorescence, with absorption maxima in the UV region. Taking also into account the relatively small absorption cross section and fluorescence quantum yield of these molecules, SMD of single proteins by their native fluorescence would appear to be extremely problematic. One solution may be a two-photon excitation of the fluorescence [5]. However, due to the low cross section of a two-photon absorption process, it often follows that to achieve reasonably high excitation rates for SMD, the intensity of the exciting light has to be so high as to invoke nonlinear scattering processes leading to a very broad white light background signal, again deteriorating the signal-to-background ratio [6]. In the present Letter, we propose a new method for fluorescence detection of single molecules immobilized on a surface. The core idea is to use a thin layer of an optically nonlinear material as the solid support on which the molecules are immobilized. The principal detection scheme is depicted in Fig. 1. By focusing a laser beam, with a wavelength 2 times larger than the excitation wavelength of the fluorophores, into a diffraction limited spot onto the surface, one generates within the thin layer of the optically nonlinear material a small region of frequency doubling of the exciting light. This frequency doubled light is then used for a one-photon fluorescence excitation of the immobilized molecule. In the present Letter, we will mainly be concerned with the question whether such a fluorescence excitation is sufficiently efficient, in particular, when compared with a two-photon excitation scheme.
