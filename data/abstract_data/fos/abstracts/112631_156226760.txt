Domestic Monetary Policy: If Not Monetarism, What?
During the fall of 1919-in the midst of a significant run from the dollar-the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, under its recently appointed chairman, Paul Volcker, announced a shift of operational guidelines and objectives for the Federal Reserve's monetary policy. This shift was toward "practical" monetarism-that is, the Federal Reserve henceforth was to use the quantity and rate change of some monetary aggregate as its proximate target for policy. The Federal Reserve would no longer be directly concerned with interest rates, money market conditions, or even the current performance of the economy. One implicit implication of monetarism, whether "practical" or "strict," is that the Federal Reserve's function as a "lender of last resort," that is, a rescuer of institutions and as a stabilizer of financial markets through the refinancing of banks and other money market entities and as a supplier of reserves, is not necessary. Monetarism must hold either that financial instability of the kind that forces lender-of-last-resort interventions will not occur (either in the transition to monetarist rules from other less precisely defined policy regimes or once monetarism is established), or that if financial instability does occur, no great harm will be done if it is allowed to run its course. The rule is that the central bank does not take special action in a financial crisis, but rather it sticks to its program for the monetary aggregates.
