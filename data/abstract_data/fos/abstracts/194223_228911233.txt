Can Empathy Be Learned? An Exploratory Analysis at Two Schools of Pharmacy
Objective. To determine whether empathy would increase among first-year student pharmacists completing longitudinal professionalism courses at two schools of pharmacy. Secondary objectives included assessing for potential moderators and whether students’ conceptualization of empathy changed with time. Methods. Surveys to assess empathy (Jefferson Scale of Empathy for Health Professional Students) and other variables were administered at baseline and at the end of the two courses. Baseline and follow-up scores were compared to detect changes over time. Multivariable analysis was used to identify predictors of empathy scores. Factor analysis was performed to ascertain changes in the dimensionality of empathy. Results. Demographics and baseline empathy scores differed between the two schools. Predictors of empathy at baseline included age, female gender, prior healthcare experience, and altruism score. A small increase in empathy was observed at one school but not in the combined cohort. Empathy was more likely to increase among female students, those with less healthcare experience, and those who did not work during the school year. Factor analyses suggested that conceptual clarity of empathy improved over time and became more consistent with existing models. Conclusion. Although an increase in empathy was not observed in the overall cohort, we identified subgroups that may benefit from empathy-related interventions. Factor analyses suggested that students’ understanding of empathy improved, representing a potential alternative assessment for affective domains. Given differences in demographics, instructional methodologies, and changes in empathy at each school, this study reinforces the importance of replication and multicenter studies to understand the generalizability of educational research.
