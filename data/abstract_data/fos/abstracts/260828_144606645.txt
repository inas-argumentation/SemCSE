An interesting if problematic issue is the author’s concern with time and memory, where the Oriente’s sacred spaces are seen to communicate, ‘present, express, and embody a special appreciation of Africa-based orientation and its accompanying understandings of past time’ (pp. 50–1). Another crucial domain is that of ancestors and the spirits of the deceased, and of the naturalness of possession as a form of revelation. So, Dodson asks in the final chapter of Part I, what do sacred spaces do? They set community boundaries, she argues, and they serve to ‘ re-member ’ (p. 63) participants of a tradition, to define the aesthetic foundations of these traditions, and to inspire creative and meaningful acts. Sacred spaces are also sites of spirit manifestation and materialisation, and here the author could have given us more on the kind of ‘work ’ involved in achieving these presences. Rather, she posits sacred spaces as ‘visual representations of a common, collective body of knowledge ’ (p. 62). Part II deals more specifically with religious practice – namely, palo monte/palo mayombe, vodú and espiritismo. The historical formation of each of these is examined, particularly the role of palenques (zones of liberation) and cofradı́as (brotherhoods) in sedimenting Haitian and Congolese cosmic heritages, as well as the influence of American and European spiritualist movements on the development of espiritismo, whose characteristically ‘oriental ’ version, espiritismo de cordón, Dodson expands on. While the significance of altars, water and spirit representations are examined in relation to this latter practice, Dodson mainly does justice to her concern with sacred spaces in her analysis of palo monte/palo mayombe, whose practitioners work the spirits of the dead in often grotesque-looking ngangas (recipients), and also in her analysis of models of the cosmos, of nature and micro-universes, all of which could point to a different conceptualisation of ‘space ’. Not much has been written on Haitian communities and their descendents in Cuba – Dodson ties in the chapter on vodú quite nicely with some valuable ethnographic and historical data. Finally, in Part III she grapples with the project’s wildcard : muertéra bembé de sao. At the end of the book one is left with the impression of having been introduced to some vital religious spaces and wonderful people, but all of this without having been told of the relationship between these spaces, nor about how these practices are rendered meaningful and consequent in contemporary Cuban society.
