From Republican virtue to technology of political power: Three episodes of Czech nonpolitical politics
One of the defining properties of totalitarianism is the encroaching expansion of the political realm to suppress, destroy, and abolish the realm of civil society, composed of voluntary autonomous associations, private exchanges, and institutions. In civil society, autonomous individuals and communities may coexist in peace, protected by the rule of law.1 Communist totalitarianism did not tolerate the existence of any independent organizations between the state and the family. To achieve total control, the communist state had to politicize and ideologize all aspects of life. The Marxist-Leninist ideology that was used initially to mobilize popular support for the ruling dictatorship devolved since the 1950s into a meaningless ritual in which few believed, but all acted out of fear and hypocrisy. Communist totalitarianism created a total separation between what politicians did and how they described and justified their actions. East European dissidents reacted to their totalitarian environment by attempting not just to resurrect civil society from its ashes, but also to expand it to abolish the political realm.
