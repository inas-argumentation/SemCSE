The Education of Future Scientists: Is Technical Expertise Stifling Scientific Creativity?
A T THE time the first sputnik made its appearance, the American educational system, at both the elementary and secondary levels, was concerned to a substantial degree with preparing young people for the twin rites of Life Adjustment and The Vote. At the collegiate level, it was business as usual in the sciences: facts, equations, graphs were thrown at students, very much as schoolboys throw snowballs at middle-aged gentlemen in top hats. Sputnik was a shock. We had been informed repeatedly that we led the world in the field of science (what we should have been told is that we led the world in scientific applications), and here were the Russians with a big Scientific First (translate, technological first). In the months that followed, as our rockets blew up on their launching pads or failed to go off at all, we were in turn overcome by consternation, chagrin, depression and, finally, anger. Subsequent events have not done much to change our mood. During the last six years, the following pattern of thought has emerged as a consequence of this trauma to national self-esteem: We must have more persons study more science. Laboratories should be bigger and better; more money should be spent on them. Science must be made more glamorous, more interesting, more vital to the student. Gifted students should be identified at an early age and motivated, by whatever means are necessary, to make a career of scientific pursuits.
