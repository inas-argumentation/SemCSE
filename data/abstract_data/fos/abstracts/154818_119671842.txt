One of the outstanding contributions of Garcia Pinto's editorial control lies in the questions she consistently poses to each writer. Even as the women interviewed reveal their uniqueness vis Ã vis each other, the similarity of the questions posed and their respective, unique answers also enable the reader to identify similar, if not identical, attitudes among the writers. The cultural information thus gained would be unavailable in less open critical texts or texts which are skewed to find only certain types of information. For example, if we agree that, as Jean Franco states in Plotting Women, Frida Kahlo "accepts, even if only indirectly, a complementarity in which women are closer to nature but men are heroes of culture," then Garcia Pinto's searching questions reveal a newly emerging attitude among later artists. As the twentieth century ends, women artists in Latin America are creating an alternative cultural text whose definition depends not on biological gender but on artistic focus, a way of producing a text, as Marta Traba explains on page 191. As do so many others, Traba invokes Poniatowska's phrase "literature of the oppressed" within the alluded to context to communicate insights which may lead the reader/listener to speculate on the role of the Latin American artist as a leader in cultural interpretation and critique, a role which may in fact lead to a revision of the culture/nature, male/female polarities discussed in regard to Kahlo. Plotting Women and Women Writers of Latin America chronicle the shifting of these and other cultural categories, a shifting which suggests fertile ground for cultural analysis.
