The need for Health Technology Assessments of PET
In a very interesting editorial on the difficulties of Health Technology Assessments of new imaging techniques [1], Højgaard raises two crucial issues that are probably in the minds of many people: “Is it appropriate to require documentation which shows better survival due to the use of PET, when such data have never been required prior to the introduction of other diagnostic modalities?” and secondly, “Is it appropriate to demand randomised studies for documentation purposes: should the assessment of a diagnostic tool be linked to patient outcome in the first place?”. Both questions should, in our opinion, be answered with a straightforward “yes”. In fact, the editorial itself contains plenty of support for such positive answers. In the Introduction to the Editorial, the author states “High costs, increasing demand for healthcare, increasing medical abilities and limited budgets have necessitated prioritisation”. It is because of these developments that evidence of diagnostic performance expressed in terms of sensitivity and specificity can no longer be considered sufficient to justify the use of PET in general health care, especially if one aims to incorporate PET in potentially complex diagnostic algorithms. Accuracy studies are not only highly susceptible to bias, but also fail to cope with the full complexity of how an imaging technique may contribute to the diagnostic and therapeutic process. For the same reason, new therapeutic agents are developed through a series of specifically designed studies, and acceptance and reimbursement can often only be achieved when at least two randomised studies show their benefit. This is in sharp contrast with the evaluation of diagnostic tests, where Højgaard correctly notes that randomised controlled trials are rare. PET has been under investigation for two decades, but until recently, clinical application was limited and its introduction is indeed still a matter of debate in many countries. Policy makers call for an HTA assessment of PET simply because sufficient evidence of effectiveness has not yet been produced. Demonstration of “impact on survival”, as Højgaard mentions, is not required for diagnostic technologies, but HTAs have long related efficacy to other patient outcomes, costs and organisational matters [2, 3]. Costs have so far only been assessed by means of mathematical models using parameters from the accuracy studies, which in turn are not easily translated to routine clinical practice. Because of prevailing uncertainties among diagnosticians and clinicians, PET is now usually added “on top” of conventional strategies, which in itself increases the costs of patient management without evidence-based justification. One of the most serious problems in performing randomised controlled trials with new technologies is the short time window in which investigators consider it ethical to randomise patients. In the Netherlands we have performed two randomised studies with PET in lung cancer and we have not encountered the difficulties suggested by Højgaard. When these studies were done, PET was not yet commonly available for routine diagnostic investigations and it was perhaps more ethical to use it within, rather than outside, the context of a randomised study because of this shortage of availability. In the first study, accrual of 188 patients from nine centers took only 1 year [4]. The main endpoint was the reduction in futile thoracotomies and its findings were highly relevant. The results of this first study led to clinical guidelines for patients with potentially operable and resectable non-small cell lung cancer, which in fact even boosted the introduction of PET facilities. In the second study, which assessed the issue of substitution, 465 lung cancer patients were included over 18 months and the analyses are currently being finalised. Since we started designing PET studies in 1997, the technique of PET imaging has not changed greatly. In conclusion, properly designed studies assessing the effectiveness of diagnostic imaging techniques in impacting on patient management in routine clinical practice will provide the evidence base for a meaningful judgment regarding the adoption and use of emerging techniques. This will help to satisfy Højgaard’s desire for HTA assessments to be as reliable as they are for treatment.
