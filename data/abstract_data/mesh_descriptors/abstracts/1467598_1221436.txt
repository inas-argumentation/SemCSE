Regarding the first comment raised by Jeganathan et al ., we agree that fundoscopy may be imprecise in detecting early signs of diabetic retinopathy. However, fundoscopy is the commonest way of diagnosing retinopathy in clinical practice. We believe that the possible imprecisions of fundoscopy examination could have been partly balanced by the fact that a single ophthalmologist diagnosed retinopathy in our patients. Moreover, and more importantly, we believe that if subtle retinopathy changes were not detected in patients with non-proliferative retinopathy, this would have weakened rather than strengthened our findings. Conversely, a possible misclassification by fundoscopy in those with proliferative and/or laser-treated retinopathy—who showed the higher relative risk of developing future CVD events—would be excluded as this more advanced form of retinopathy was also confirmed by fundus fluorescein angiography. Regarding the second comment raised by Jeganathan et al ., we would remind readers that we documented 47 non-fatal ischaemic strokes (25 in men) during follow-up. Thus, the number of events was too low to detect a significant association between retinopathy and ischaemic stroke. The association of retinopathy with non-fatal coronary heart disease (CHD) was significant and stronger than that with stroke. However, we documented 231 non-fatal CHD events during follow-up. For that reason, we decided to combine all CVD outcomes (i.e. non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal ischaemic stroke, coronary revascularization procedures or cardiovascular death). Based on previously published information, we think people with diabetic retinopathy have excess risks of systemic vascular complications, including stroke, coronary heart disease, heart failure and nephropathy. Regarding the third comment raised by Jeganathan et al ., we have calculated the composite score for sensitivity and specificity (area under the curve; AUC), as determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis for prediction of incident CVD (included as a composite outcome) by diabetic retinopathy. Overall, AUC was 0.76, suggesting a fairly good model for prediction of CVD using retinopathy. Perhaps our discussion of the patho-physiological mechanisms linking retinopathy and CVD was too succinct. However, we agree with Jeganathan et al . that diabetic retinopathy and CVD share a broad number of pathophysiological backgrounds, such as hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, subclinical inflammation, increased oxidative stress, elevated advanced glycated end-products, neovascularization and a hypercoagulable state. Moreover, we believe that diabetic retinopathy may reflect widespread microcirculatory disease not only in the eye but also vital organs elsewhere in the body.
