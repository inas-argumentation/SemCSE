A celebration of protein–protein interactions
This issue is a celebration of protein–protein interactions, which have become one of the most important aspects of proteomics. Two-dimensional gels can help us understand what proteins are present in a sample and their level of expression, as can the digestion of proteome to peptides and their analysis by mass spectrometry. However, studies of protein–protein interactions are required to discover how proteins form multisubunit complexes. This is crucial for defining the functional units of the cell and how these function as molecular machines. What is there to celebrate in the area of protein–protein interactions? The year of 2009 marks the 20th anniversary of the yeast two-hybrid technique. Described by Stanley Fields and Ok-kyu Song in 1989 (Nature 1989, 340, 245–246), this technique and variants thereof have proved to be powerful and robust means to test if any two proteins interact. It has made possible the generation of large-scale protein interaction networks, and heralded the era of the interactome. In this issue, Stanley Fields gives a fascinating and personal perspective on the yeast-two technique. He also shares a unique piece of protein–protein interaction history with us – one of the original sketches that map out the method. We are approaching a similar anniversary with the technique of blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE). Described by Hermann Sch. agger and Gebhard von Jagow in 1991 (Anal. Biochem. 1991, 199, 223–231), it has been widely embraced as a means of separating protein complexes. It provides data that are complementary to the yeast two-hybrid technique and allows mass spectrometric analysis of complexed proteins. In this issue, Hermann Sch. agger reviews recent progress in this method and related techniques. A particular focus is how transient interactions, some of which can be detected with yeast two-hybrid approaches, can be studied with BN-PAGE when proteins are first crosslinked in vivo. No celebration of protein–protein interactions can be complete without reviewing aspects of protein structure or of interaction networks. These are at opposite poles of the world of interactions; one considers the minutiae, and the other the whole-cell view. Both are areas in which dramatic advances have recently been made. In this issue, Joel Mackay and colleagues review how NMR methods, especially residual dipolar couplings and the use of paramagnetic effects, can provide insight into the structure of protein–protein complexes. From a whole cell perspective, and with a focus on the dynamics of phosphorylation and their role in networks, Chris Soon Heng Tan and Rune Linding review how experimental data and computer prediction can be combined to understand kinase–substrate relationships and thus construct accurate networks of signal transduction. The study of protein–protein interactions, particularly on a global scale, has predominantly focused to date on proteins from Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Two-hybrid techniques and those that affinity–purify protein complexes have been applied on a global scale. Impressively, these studies have been done multiple times, and hence one can consider that there is high ‘‘coverage’’ of many interactions in the S. cerevisiae interactome (by analogy with ‘‘coverage’’ in genome sequencing). This has provided detailed insights into the number and type of complexes in the eukaryotic cell and how different proteins participate in different numbers and types of interactions.
