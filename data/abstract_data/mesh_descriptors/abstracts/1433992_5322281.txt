Epstein-Barr virus load in whole blood correlates with HIV surrogate markers and lymphoma: a French national cross-sectional study.
To the Editor: The incidence of AIDS-related lymphoma (ARL) has fallen since the advent of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART), but lymphomas remain a major cause of death among HIVinfected patients. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is detected in 40%–90% of cases of ARL, probably owing to a lack of EBV control. Systemic EBV load is helpful for screening and treatment monitoring of posttransplant lymphoproliferative disease but does not seem to correlate with the risk of developing EBV-associated disease in HIV-infected patients. The few available studies involving significant numbers of patients have shown no correlation between the EBV DNA level and surrogate markers of HIV disease. In plasma samples, 1 team detected EBV DNA solely from EBV-positive ARL patients, suggesting that EBV plasma load could be a valuable surrogate marker of EBV disease in AIDS patients with ARL. The aim of this cross-sectional study was to determine EBV DNA load both in whole blood and plasma from a large cohort of HIV-1–infected adults, most of whom were receiving HAART, and to ascertain whether EBV DNA load correlates with the CD4 cell count, the plasma HIV RNA level, and EBV disease. EBV DNA was measured with a real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction assay. The study was initiated by the EBV Working Group of Agence Nationale de Recherche sur le SIDA et les Hépatites [ANRS (the French national research agency on AIDS and viral hepatitis)], which standardizes EBV DNA quantification and organizes national quality controls. The participating laboratories used their regular assays (inhouse methods, or LightCyclerdEBV Quantification Kit; Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France) validated by the working group. Between January and May 2006, each of the 12 participating centers enrolled 50 patients. A total of 582 paired whole blood and plasma samples were collected from 369 patients with CD4 >200 cells per cubic millimeter (group 1) and from 213 patients with CD4 <200 cells per cubic millimeter (group 2). Data were compared between the groups by using analysis of variance, the Mann–Whitney U test, Spearman correlation coefficient, the x test, and Fisher exact test as appropriate. The results are shown in Table 1. EBV DNA was detected in whole blood from 380 patients (65.5%), significantly more frequently in group 2 than in group 1 (72.8% vs 61.2%, P = 0.03). The median whole blood EBV load in positive samples was significantly higher in group 2 (3.69 log10 copies/mL vs 3.41 log10 copies/mL, P < 0.001), as was the proportion of patients with EBV loads above 4 log10 copies per milliliter (32% vs 17%, P < 0.01). EBV DNA was detected in plasma samples from only 28 patients (4.8%) who were equally distributed between the 2 groups. The median plasma EBV load in positive samples was 2.57 log10 copies per milliliter, with no difference between the 2 groups. All patients with positive plasma samples had whole blood EBV loads above 2.9 log10 copies per milliliter. The whole blood EBV DNA load correlated positively with the plasma HIV RNA load (P < 0.001) and negatively with the CD4 cell count (P < 0.001). The plasma EBV DNA load correlated positively with the plasma HIV RNA load (P < 0.001) and tended to correlate negatively with the CD4 cell count (P = 0.077). Three of the 582 patients had NHL at the time of EBV quantification. Their whole blood EBV DNA loads were higher than those of patients with no history of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) (median 4.84 log10 copies/mL, range: 2.88–5.46 vs 2.93 log10 copies/mL, range: 0–6.48; P = 0.05). EBV DNA was detected in the plasma of 1 of these 3 patients. This large national cross-sectional survey confirms the frequent detection of EBV DNA in the blood of HIV-infected patients and shows, for the first time, a correlation of EBV DNA load in whole blood with surrogate markers of HIV disease. Previous studies may have missed this correlation because of a lack of statistical power. The significance of EBV load in whole blood is poorly understood, and so are the mechanisms underlying the more frequent EBV reactivation in HIV-infected patients, especially those with preserved immune function who have strong T-cell responses to EBV lytic antigens. Recently, in HIV-infected patients with high EBV DNA loads, Stevens et al observed elevated anti-VCA p18 immunoglobulin G levels in peripheral blood but neither cell-free EBV DNA nor EBV lytic cycle transcripts. This suggests an increased EBV lytic replication restricted to mucosal lymphoid sites, possibly linked to EBV infection of epithelial cells or antigen cross-linking of B-cell receptors on EBV-infected cells. The hypothesis that an antigen can drive a latently infected B cell to EBV reactivation is supported by a report from Chene et al, who showed that Plasmodium falciparum CIDR1alpha protein triggered memory B cells and increased the number of cells switching to the EBV lytic cycle. This hypothesis needs to be investigated in the context of HIV infection. EBV preferentially infects B lymphocytes in immunocompetent individuals and transplant recipients, but HIV may alter the lymphocyte tropism of EBV as recently observed with the frequent detection of EBV in CD4-positive and CD8-positive T cells in HIV-infected adolescents receiving HAART. Therefore, the high EBV DNA load in whole blood is more difficult to interpret in HIV-infected patients than in transplant patients and requires further investigation. Immune deficiency, chronic immune activation, and unknown individual factors may influence EBV load as recently suggested. We found that EBV was rarely detectable in plasma and only in patients with high whole blood EBV loads.
