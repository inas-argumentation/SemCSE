Is High-sensitivity C-reactive Protein Associated With Subclinical Peripheral Atherosclerosis?
B o et al in this issue of Angiology report that after adjustment for covariates, highsensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels were not independently associated with lower ankle-brachial index (ABI) and greater carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) values in 320 individuals without prior cardiovascular disease (CVD) attending a lipid clinic. The ABI and cIMT were used as indices of subclinical atherosclerosis. Among asymptomatic, moderate-to-high CV risk individuals, hsCRP levels were associated with peripheral arterial disease (PAD) severity, but these associations were not independent of traditional CV risk factors. These results contribute to the current controversy regarding the predictive value of hsCRP. This topic is considered in this editorial. C-reactive protein was first described about 78 years ago when sera of patients with acute infection precipitated with C polysaccharide. C-reactive protein is synthesized by the liver under the control of cytokines. Via its effects on opsonization and phagocytosis and activation of complement, neutrophils, and monocyte-macrophage, it plays a role in the recognition of microbial organisms, as an immunomodulator in host defense and in the recognition of necrotic tissue. Many diseases/conditions can raise CRP values. For example, obesity, cancer, connective tissue diseases, myocardial infarction, infection, inflammatory bowel disease, infections, rheumatoid arthritis, pregnancy, birth control pills, intrauterine devices (local inflammation), and hyperlipidemia. However, these situations may be associated with very high CRP levels; hsCRP assays deal with much lower levels. Drugs, like nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and statins, can reduce circulating hsCRP levels. Hemolyzed, lipemic, or turbid sera may influence results. Furthermore, procedural differences may alter results: overnight refrigeration may produce a false-positive result but same day testing does not require refrigeration. Age, sex, and race alter the reference range for CRP. These factors will influence the interpretation of CRP values. Epidemiological studies can dilute the technical and clinical factors altering CRP results but these concerns may also increase with population diversity and number of participating laboratories. C-reactive protein production is a nonspecific response to disease and may therefore not be used as a diagnostic test on its own. More specifically to PAD, the prospective case-control Physicians’ Health Study involved 144 apparently healthy men who subsequently developed symptomatic PAD and 144 age-matched controls who remained free of vascular disease during a follow-up period of 60 months. Median CRP levels at baseline were significantly higher among those who developed PAD than among those who did not (1.34 vs. 0.99 mg/L, respectively; P 1⁄4 .04).
