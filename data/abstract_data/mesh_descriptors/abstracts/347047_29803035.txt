However, we question the conclusion of the author that randomised studies are not valid and they should be replaced by studies of individual patients. Firstly, the importance of chaotic dynamics and related nonlinear phenomena in medical sciences is not well established (Ruelle 1994). Secondly, Brincat (1994) correctly pointed out that chaos is not mindless disorder; it is a subtle form of order, and that approximate results of treatment can be predicted. Chaotic dynamics are characterised most of the time by what is called a strange attractor. This roughly means that during the chaotic evolution, the variables characterising the state of the system remain in a restricted range of values. This leads to the possibility of characterising the system evolution in terms of probabilities (Firth 1991). For some highly chaotic systems in physics, such as a gas of molecules, probabilities and statistics are the only means of characterisation. This suggests that, when dealing with chaos, statistical methods would be more liable to provide realistic results than studies of individuals. When studying an outcome subject to chaotic dynamics, we believe that randomised controlled trials would still be valid. Outcome variables of individuals would be dispersed in the strange attractor areas and statistical differences between groups might still be found. Moreover, if there is no relation between intervention and outcome, spurious associations would not emerge, the variables being dispersed with no systematic difference between groups. In physics, where it had the largest impact, the discovery of chaos did not question the validity of all previous theoretical and experimental methods. In our opinion, chaos theory is not questioning the validity of randomised controlled trials in medicine either.
