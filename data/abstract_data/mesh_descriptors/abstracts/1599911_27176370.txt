Calcium supplementation during pregnancy: a systematic review of randomised controlled trials
An inverse relation between calcium intake and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy was formally described first in 1980 (Belizin & Villar), and it was suggested that this association might be causal. The hypothesis was based on the observation that Mayan Indians in Guatemala, who traditionally soak their corn in lime before cooking, had a high calcium intake and a low incidence of preeclampsia and eclampsia. Reports from other parts of the world have confirmed an inverse association between calcium intake and blood pressure during pregnancy (Hamlin 1952, 1962; Belizan et al. 1983a). The importance of this observation is that it suggests an increase in calcium intake during pregnancy may reduce the incidence of preeclampsia. Low calcium intake may cause high blood pressure by stimulating either parathyroid hormone or renin release, thereby increasing intracellular calcium in vascular smooth muscle (Belizan et al. 1988), and leading to vasoconstriction. A possible mode of action for calcium supplementation is that it reduces parathyroid hormone release and intracellular calcium, and so reduces smooth muscle reactivity. By a similar mechanism, calcium supplementation also could reduce uterine smooth muscle contractility and prevent preterm labour and delivery (Villar BE Repke 1990). Calcium supplementation is attractive as a potential intervention to reduce the risk of a woman developing preeclampsia. It is relatively cheap, readily available, likely to be safe for both the woman or her baby, and could be truly nutritional if adequate amounts were introduced into the diet by either additives or dietary advice. Potential disadvantages are that the form of supplementation currently being evaluated involves each woman taking three or four large tablets a day, and there is a theoretical risk of urinary calculi. Randomised trials are the best method for minimising systematic error (bias) when assessing the effects of care (Chalmers 1989), but randomised trials, particularly if small, are prone to random errors (the play of chance). The likelihood of being misled by random error is minimised by using data from all relevant randomised trials in systematic reviews (overviews). We report here a systematic review of the evidence from randomised trials of calcium supplementation during pregnancy.
