LTP: variation between inbred mouse strains
Hippocampal LTP, a synaptic mechanism implicated in memory, has been the target of intensive molecular genetic analysis for the past decade. Several studies show that LTP is altered when particular single genes are knocked out or overexpressed in null mutant or transgenic mice. What was not suspected, however, is that natural variation in LTP might exist. The authors show, by using the hippocampal slice preparation, that C57BL/6J (B6) mice exhibit robust LTP but CBA/J (CBA), DBA/2J (DBA) and 129/SVEms/J (129) strains suffer from differing degrees and sorts of abnormalities in LTP. For example, theta burst stimulation induced strong LTP in B6 but much weaker LTP in 129. Furthermore, LTP in DBA and CBA strains, although initially less impaired, decayed significantly faster than in B6. Another LTP induction protocol gave a different pattern of results. A temporally compressed tetanic stimulation induced robust LTP in both B6 and 129 strains but resulted in an almost completely absent potentiation in DBA and CBA. That is, ‘hippocampal neurons of some mouse strains may be more optimally tuned to particular temporal patterns of synaptic activity’ as the authors explain.In contrast to these differences in LTP, LTD, an activity dependent reduction of synaptic transmission, appeared unaltered in the four mouse strains. Hippocampal neurons from these strains also exhibited similar membrane excitability (measured as spike firing efficacy using whole-cell patch-clamp recording), membrane input resistance, or synaptically evoked glutamatergic postsynaptic currents. Thus, the strain differences in LTP are probably a result of altered molecular mechanisms associated with synaptic strengthening, one of which could involve the cAMP-PKA (protein kinase A) pathway (as shown by the authors), and not as a result of modified biophysical properties of the neuronal membrane.What was not suspected, however, is that natural variation in LTP might exist.These results have several interesting implications. First, they suggest that variation in synaptic strengthening either might have existed in the wild population of mice from which the inbred strains were derived or, alternatively, might have developed as a result of the occurrence of random mutations during inbreeding. Both of these possibilities imply an absence of strong selection pressure on LTP. Second, because differences in behavior among inbred strains have been well documented, scientists might now be able to investigate whether they correlate with alterations in synaptic plasticity using quantitative genetic techniques.
