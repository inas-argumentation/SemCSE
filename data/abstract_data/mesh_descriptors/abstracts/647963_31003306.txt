Medical management of sinusitis in pediatric patients.
U he diagnosis of sinusitis has changed during our 25 years of clinical practice. During our pediatric residencies, a diagnosis of acute or chronic sinusitis was not common. Twenty years later as an allergistimmunologist and pediatric infectious disease specialist, we were seeing numerous patients with sinusitis, especially of a chronic nature. Is the increased diagnosis of sinusitis a result of experience, increased prevalence, medical postgraduate educational experience, or epidemiologic and societal factors? Our subspecialty experience is important in the diagnosis and treatment of sinusitis. There is no doubt that patients with chronic respiratory symptoms unresponsive to routine therapy are referred to physicians with our specialty training for evaluation. The proportion of patients with chronic sinusitis seen in subspecialty respiratory disease clinics is probably higher than that in the general medical population, thereby contributing to the emphasis on this diagnosis. This concentration of cases has provided us the opportunity to examine and treat many children with sinusitis and has given us the perspective on diagnosis and treatment that is discussed in this article. A review of MEDLINE from 1975 to 1995 revealed 161 citations of 240,554 under the title sinusitis (major medical subject heading) for 1975, and in 1995 alone, 175 listings (of 381,865).
