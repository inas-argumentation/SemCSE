Alice in wonderland: the psychiatric perspective.
Dear Editor: Any student of the mind who reads Lewis Carroll’s ‘Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland’1 cannot fail to be alarmed by the range of psychopathology on display. The novel’s tone may be light and playful, and the author’s intent to entertain, but there can be no doubt that the novel also succeeds as a vivid demonstration of human psychopathology albeit mostly in animals. The Cheshire Cat is an excellent example. Not only does the Cheshire Cat display a constant, mask-like grin, but he has a limited range of affect, lack of emotional responsiveness and notable disregard for social mores. These features point strongly towards a prolonged state of elation, possibly combined with dysphoric features. At the other end of the spectrum, the Mock Turtle, who sighs and cries and ruminates on the past, appears to be clinically depressed. Several other characters present similar cause for concern. Early in the story, Alice encounters Mouse, when they are both swimming in a pool of Alice’s tears. When Alice speaks to Mouse, he reacts with disproportionate shock, generalized trembling and a clearly hypersensitive startle-response; this is highly suggestive of post-traumatic stress disorder. The impulse-control dysfunction and difficulty with inter-personal relations that Mouse demonstrates later are consistent with this tentative diagnosis. In addition, as the story progresses, it becomes clear that the anxious, obsessional White Rabbit suffers from a similar neurotic or stress-related disorder. It is similarly difficult to vouch for the mental health of the Duchess: not only does she put excessive amounts of pepper in the soup, but her speech pattern clearly demonstrates clang-associations, commonly associated with elation. The Queen herself is so fond of beheading people that one wonders about the possibility of dissocial personality disorder; the fact that she does not follow through on these threats simply suggests complicating features of borderline personality disorder. And even Young Crab is not immune to the psychopathology: she dramatically disobeys her mother in full view of Mouse, Alice and the Dodo, raising the possibility of unsocialized conduct disorder, hyperkinetic disorder or, perhaps, the first case of oppositional defiant disorder ever described in a juvenile crustacean. It is Alice, however, who provides the greatest cause for concern. She experiences a worrying array of visual distortions and disturbances, as she reports her overall size to be increasing and decreasing on several occasions and, at one point, reports that her neck is lengthening so that her head hovers above the top of the trees. Alice also exhibits worrying disturbances of core self-identity, leading to a fluid, inconsistent sense of self and a constant feeling of uncertainty about who she really is. What is most disturbing, however, is the fact that these experiences tend to occur following the ingestion of various substances. On one particularly regrettable occasion, Alice encounters a talking caterpillar relaxing atop a large mushroom. This remarkable caterpillar appears to be seriously dependent on tobacco or whatever it is he smokes in his hookah. Not only that, but the caterpillar promptly invites Alice to ingest sections of this apparently magic mushroom, with the result that Alice experiences the rather psychedelic sensation of growing and shrinking in size once again. With all of this psychopathology on show, it is little wonder that ‘Alice in Wonderland’ continues to excite the imaginations of adults and children the world over. In addition, given the level of psychopathology apparent in even the most minor of characters, it comes as no surprise that the Mad Hatter and March Hare, happily engaged in their absurdist tea party, ultimately appear the most stable of them all.
