Cellular Blue Nevus in Association with Phototherapy
Phototherapy (PUVA and UVB) is a well-known important treatment modality in the long-term management of moderate to severe psoriasis. Short-term side effects are commonly known. Long-term and dose-dependent side effects include an increased risk of developing accelerated skin ageing, actinic keratosis, atypical cutaneous pigmentation, PUVA lentigo and ophthalmologic abnormalities. PUVA might also increase the risk of melanoma and of nonmelanoma skin cancer. A 41-year-old Caucasian woman with skin type II had been suffering from psoriasis since her teens. From 1996 to 1998, the patient was treated in our outpatient department with UVB approximately every 6 months. UVB irradiations were performed with Waldmann UV 800 1K with a cumulative UVB dose of 52.9 J/cm2. Other occasional treatment comprised the topical administration of glucocorticosteroids and Daivonex®. She never received methotrexate or other cytotoxic agents, isotretinoin, arsenic or X-ray and PUVA therapy. The patient had neither been to sun-rich countries nor been sunburnt. In January 1998, she began UVB therapy with the starting dose of 0.03 J/cm2. The patient’s psoriasis responded well to the treatment. After 16 treatments (1.62 J/cm2) the patient noticed a black-brown nodulus on her right shoulder. The lesion was approximately 4 ! 4 mm in size and showed irregular pigmentation. A diagnosis of melanoma was suspected and a wide excision was performed. The biopsy specimen was reviewed by a panel of dermatopathologists, who concluded that this was a cellular blue nevus. Primary agents to treat psoriasis include UVB, PUVA, topical glucocorticosteroids, dithranol, tar and vitamin D analogues. PUVA therapy increases the risk to develop squamous-cell carcinoma and basal-cell carcinoma. A very recent report indicates the possibility that there is an increased risk of melanoma in patients with psoriasis treated with PUVA [1]. A multicenter study conducted in the USA in 1998 showed a noticeably increased incidence of melanoma after long-term PUVA treatment. During a prospective followup of 1,380 patients enrolled in the PUVA study, 11 melanomas were diagnosed [2]. In 1999, Lindelof [3] analyzed the same cohort of 1,380 patients with psoriasis and he concluded that about 15 years after the first PUVA treatment, the risk of melanoma was increased approximately 5-fold in patients treated with high doses. It has been suggested that UVB phototherapy may be less carcinogenic than PUVA. Maintenance therapy for psoriasis with UVB radiation results in a greater cumulative UVB radiation dose and thereby increases the risk of skin cancer. In 1991, Pathak [4] using a hairless pigmented mouse strain (Skhhr2) showed that single application of DMBA as an initiator and subsequent repeated exposure to either UVB or UVA or a combined exposure of UVA and UVB resulted in the formation of blue-nevuslike lesions. He concluded that some initiation event is essential to transform melanocytes to blue-nevus-like lesions before UV radiation can act as a promoter and accelerate the development of malignant melanoma. In 1998, Atillasoy et al. [5] demonstrated in an animal experimental system that chronic UVB irradiation with or without an initiating carcinogen can induce human melanocytic lesions, including melanoma. Within patients developing skin cancers, analysis of the pattern of DNA damage suggests that both UVB and PUVA are implicated on the basis of the frequency of p53 psoralen type mutations (51TpG sites, which are the primary targets for PUVA mutagenesis) and UVspecific mutations (C – T or CC – TT) [6]. The blue nevus represents a localized proliferation of dermal melanocytes with onset at birth or in childhood [7]. Blue nevi are thought to arise because of arrested migration of melanocytes migrating towards the dermoepidermal junction. Lesions similar to the cellular blue nevus have been produced experimentally by painting the dorsal pigmented spots on hamsters with carcinogenic hydrocarbons [7, 8]. Although ordinary blue nevi remain benign, there is a low incidence of malignant transformation among cellular blue nevi [9]. Prior to excision, clinical examination of our patient definitely revealed no pigmented lesion or nevus at this site. The patient had neither used a photosensitizer or tar preparations nor arsenic, methotrexate, retinoids, cyclosporine, radiotherapy or PUVA. Thus, it seems possible that treatment with UVB may have played an etiological role for the development of cellular blue nevus. However, it is not possible to prove a causal connection between UVB therapy and the development of this tumor.
