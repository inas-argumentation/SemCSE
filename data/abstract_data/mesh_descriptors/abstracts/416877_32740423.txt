Small size and disease severity in living donation: A difficult match
The amount of liver mass transplanted has been shown to be an important variable following transplantation. Early experimental studies showed that the graft size to recipient ratio was critical, in that small grafts grew to meet the demands of a larger host, but grafts that were too small resulted in decreased survival. The clinical term small-for-size syndrome (SFSS) was coined to describe how some recipients of small grafts demonstrated significant functional impairment, with prolonged cholestasis, ascites, coagulopathy, encephalopathy, and histologic changes consistent with ischemic injury, often resulting in failure and retransplantation. Emond et al. first described this phenomenon in adultto-pediatric living donor liver transplantation (LDLT), in which a child would generally receive the left lateral segment of an adult liver, which was usually a sufficient amount of liver for an infant but often was too small for larger children. As the transplant community broadened LDLT to adult recipients, the importance of graft size became more important, so right lobe grafts were often the graft of choice to provide more liver mass. Liver grafts with a graft weight/standard liver volume of less than 40% or a graft weight to recipient weight ratio (GWRWR) of less than 0.8% were found to have poor graft survival. The initial experience in adult LDLT also showed us that patients who had fulminant hepatic failure had poorer results than those transplanted for other reasons, and patients with very decompensated liver disease may have decreased survival or have more difficulty recovering, with longer length of stays. Some believed that it was the LDLT learning curve, and others believed that half a liver was not enough to support a very ill patient. In general, most programs in North America stopped performing adult LDLT in the critically ill because of these results and because in the United States we have the ability to provide deceased donors to these patients on account of our allocation system and the national system of organ donation. Unfortunately, the availability of deceased donors is limited in many other parts of the world, and LDLT may be the only option for the critically ill patient with a high Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score. As reported in this issue of Liver Transplantation, Yi et al. and a group from the Seoul National University College of Medicine in Korea investigated outcomes in adult LDLT recipients with high and low MELD scores with respect to the size of the graft received. All 167 patients had chronic hepatitis B with calculated MELD scores ranging from 7 to 48. Looking at the various combinations of high/low MELD scores and small-forsize/normal-size grafts, they concluded that there was no significant difference in 1-year survival between high and low MELD scores, no matter what the size of the graft was. For centers with limited access to deceased donors, this is terrific news, essentially increasing the number of potential living donor recipients at an experienced living donor center. Ah, but the devil is in the details. Let us dissect this a little bit more, as we can learn a lot from this center’s experience. First, a relatively small number of patients received small-for-size grafts. In this series, as in most, smallfor-size grafts were defined as those with a GWRWR of 0.8%, with 10.8% (n 18) of the recipients receiving a graft this small.
