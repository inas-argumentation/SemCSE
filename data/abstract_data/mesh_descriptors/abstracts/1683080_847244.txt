Hepatoma in hepatitis B: Infectious disease or liver disease?
C hronic hepatitis B (HBV) infection is still a very common infection worldwide. Although most with chronic infection are asymptomatic and may never even know that they were infected, some develop complications of cirrhosis, liver failure, and liver cancer. The link between hepatitis B infection and hepatoma was convincingly shown by Palmer Beasley’s report of a prospective study of 22,707 Taiwanese men in 1981. Since then, hepatoma has been linked to many other causes of chronic liver disease (CLD) in the setting of established cirrhosis. Hepatitis B is unique in that hepatoma can occur even in those without cirrhosis. A reexamination of a cohort of 3,653 Taiwanese adult men from 1991 to 1992 using modern sensitive methods to quantify HBV DNA suggested that the HBV viral load had a major impact on the risk of hepatoma. This finding has led many to conclude that everyone with chronic HBV infection should be treated with a goal complete viral suppression. Essentially, chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is viewed as an infectious disease that needs treatment. Treatment of CHB infection is now quite simple. Oral nucleoside/nucleotide analogs can effectively block HBV viral replication. However, treatment is not curative. Furthermore, HBV viral suppression reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk of hepatoma. Current treatment guidelines do not advocate treatment for all infected individuals. Rather, clinical parameters are assessed to determine those who might be at risk and who might therefore benefit from treatment. Essentially, guidelines acknowledge that CHB is a liver disease where treatment is warranted for those at risk. Identifying which patients are at risk has been tricky. The natural history of hepatitis B is convoluted with phases of liver injury and phases where liver injury seems to abate. The inactive carrier state seems to be low risk for hepatoma where the HBV-DNA levels are low. However, the immune tolerant state found in most young individuals with extremely high HBV-DNA levels is also a low risk for hepatoma. It has been recognized that male sex, older age, higher HBV-DNA level, and presence of cirrhosis are the biggest risks for hepatoma in those with chronic HBV infection. Of these variables, the assessment of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis has historically been problematic because of the need for liver biopsy. Liver biopsy is impractical for large cohort studies. Without liver biopsy, cirrhosis was diagnosed based on imaging and clinical characteristics—methods that are very insensitive for cirrhosis in its earlier stages. Currently, liver biopsy is rarely done for chronic HBV infection for fibrosis assessment because of the availability of noninvasive methods, such as vibrationcontrolled transient elastography, which estimates liver stiffness. Liver stiffness measurements (LSMs) have considerable advantage over liver biopsy not only because it is noninvasive, but also the dynamic range is much greater in those with cirrhosis. For liver biopsy, the worst score is cirrhosis. For LSMs, cirrhosis is diagnosed at 13-14 kilopascals (kPa), but the dynamic range for cirrhosis is 13-75 kPa. This large range in LSMs likely corresponds with the large range in cirrhosis: from early cirrhosis, where the liver still looks normal on imaging despite cirrhotic nodules on biopsy, to late-stage cirrhosis, where dense cirrhotic nodules with large areas of extinction and parenchymal collapse result in a small, dense, nodular liver where the diagnosis of cirrhosis is so obvious clinically that a liver biopsy is not needed. There is likely a big difference in hepatoma risk between those with cirrhosis and liver stiffness 14 kPa, compared to those with liver stiffness of 20 or 40 kPa. HBV-DNA levels as a risk predictor of hepatoma is also problematic. For those on antiviral therapy, HBV DNA should be very low, if not undetectable.
