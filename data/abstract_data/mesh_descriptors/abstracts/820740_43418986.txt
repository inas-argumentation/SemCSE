Communicating with cancer patients.
Having been a cancer patient for the past two and a half years, I very much welcome the recent articles by Dr Peter Maguire and Ms Ann Faulkner (8 October, p 907; 15 October, p 972). I think, however, that their model does not fully take into account the vulnerability of this group of patients and the realities of doctor-patient interaction. I support the idea that patients should determine the pace at which distressing information is divulged, but not to this extent. The model dialogues require an unreal degree of persistence on the part of both doctor and patient. There is a danger that they will end prematurely and in confusion. I would like to think that if I were the model patient I would break off the dialogue to tell the doctor what I thought of the patronising and humiliating way in which I was being forced to elicit information. In fact, of course, I would stop talking. I would go away frustrated and the doctor would be satisfied that he had answered my questions. A "hierarchy of euphemisms for the term cancer" will mislead patients, even if this is unintentional. In my case, despite a PhD and long experience of communicating with doctors, the suggestion that a cyst be removed "because it will continue to grow and cause you trouble" never led me to suspect malignancy until half an hour after I had left the consultation room. Only then did I begin to wonder at the haste with which surgery was being arranged and worry that I had not questioned the doctor further. I was bewildered, unwilling to be "difficult" and ask for a new appointment. Surely that nice doctor would have told me the truth? I agonised for a month until the night before the operation, when I had the opportunity to initiate the next step up the hierarchy and found my suspicions to be true. I was more relieved to be rid of uncertainty than upset at the possible diagnosis. I still do not trust the doctor who misled me. Every cancer patient I know can tell similar stories. When nervous and anxious we do not ask the right question at the right time. We need our doctors to take the initiative routinely, not just in extreme cases of collusion or denial. The model, to be effective, also requires a doctor to be skilled in assessing verbal and non-verbal clues and sufficiently patient and empathetic to elicit them. It also presupposes a system of patient management that allows patients to feel relaxed and confident enough to express their needs. With few exceptions, neither has been my experience.
