Current practice in amyloid detection and typing among renal pathologists
Renal pathologists are in a uniquely advantageous position with regard to the diagnosis of amyloidosis. Typically, renal amyloidosis leads to kidney function impairment, especially proteinurianephrotic syndrome, which then prompts a kidney biopsy. In turn, renal biopsies are routinely subjected to an extensive workup, involving special stains, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy, which facilitate the detection of amyloid deposits. As a consequence, renal pathologists are more frequently involved in the detection of renal and nonrenal amyloidoses than general surgical pathologists. While a routine kidney biopsy work-up can lead to the early detection and diagnosis of amyloid (and other diseases associated with an underlying plasma cell dyscrasia), experience is required in the interpretation of these results. Therefore, pathologists, including renal pathologists, should focus primarily on the detection of amyloid and not its typing. In this regard, a simple, pathologist-friendly amyloid screening test would be highly desirable. While amyloid typing can be successful in many instances, it is best delegated to specialized laboratories. Introduction: Renal pathologists are in a uniquely advantageous position with regard to the diagnosis of amyloidosis. Typically, renal involvement by amyloid leads to kidney function impairment, especially proteinuria-nephrotic syndrome, which then prompts a kidney biopsy [1]. In turn, renal biopsies are routinely subjected to an extensive workup involving special stains, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy, all of which facilitate the detection of amyloid deposits. Moreover, renal involvement is almost always associated with a systemic amyloidosis and, hence, is clinically significant [1]. The aim of this publication is to review current practice in amyloid detection and typing among renal pathologists and to compare this with methods prevalent among general surgical pathologists and amyloid centers, as well as to consider future directions and to identify areas for possible improvement. Methods: In preparation for a consensus discussion on amyloid detection and typing, assessment of its current status among renal pathologists was performed using a survey questionnaire. Additional discussions were also held with, and among, members of the Renal Pathology Society. Results and discussion: A total of 39 responses were received from the US/Canada (19), European Union (10), Turkey (3), Saudi Arabia (1), Russia (1), South East Asia (2), and Brazil (3). One-third of the respondents evaluated between 6 and 10 cases of amyloid per year, while 22 respondents evaluated 411 amyloid cases per year, and only 4 respondents evaluated less than 5 cases per year. For a generic diagnosis of amyloid, all respondents used Congo red stain under polarized light, but 41% used, in addition, Congo red stain þ immunofluorescence or þ immunoperoxidase (20%) or thioflavin (20%). Respondents stated that Congo red stain examined under immunofluorescence light or thioflavin stain are more sensitive for the detection of amyloid. Moreover, several respondents reported difficulties in interpreting Congo red stain. Among renal pathologists, variability in Congo red stain was reported by 66% of respondents and was most frequently noted in AL. By comparison, 75% of respondents from amyloid centers reported similar observations. Although most respondents preferred to use thicker paraffin sections (8–10 mm) for detection of amyloid in Congo red stained slides, at least one respondent reported satisfactory results with the sections routinely used in renal pathology (2–3 mm); however, examination of at least two slides stained with Congo red is recommended in such cases. Although, in other organs, amyloid is infrequently included in the differential diagnoses, renal amyloidosis should be included among the four main differential diagnoses in patients presenting with proteinuria/nephrotic syndrome. Although renal pathologists are more prone than general pathologists to perform Congo red stain, there is still a need for a better, pathologist-friendly amyloid stain and for increased awareness of amyloidoses among both renal and general pathologists [1]. Electron microscopy was used routinely by 72% of respondents, mostly as part of a routine native kidney biopsy workup. Congo red negative deposits with amyloid-like fibrils, detectable only by electron microscopy, were reported by 39% of respondents. At present, the clinical significance of these findings is not clear. It is felt that the interpretation of small amyloid-like deposits is inherently difficult when based on electron microscopy alone. It is particularly difficult in the kidney, since renal glomeruli not uncommonly show vaguely fibrillary areas, in particular associated with scarring. Thus, for clinical purposes, the diagnosis of renal amyloid based solely on electron microscopy should be discouraged. However, electron microscopy is critical for the detection and characterization of various other organized deposits other than amyloid.
