Aging and Nonsteroidal Antiinflammatory Drug-Induced Renal Dysfunction
OLD AGE HAS BEEN DEFINED as a risk factor for nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drug (NSAID)-induced acute nephrotoxicity. I The increased use of NSAID by elderly patients and the possibility of NSAID-induced renal toxicity present a conflicting clinical situation, wherein the patients who may benefit the most from the drug may be at high risk for toxicity. The purpose of this commentary is to explore the relationship between patient age and the risk of developing NSAID-induced renal toxicity. The development of acute renal toxicity associated with NSAID is more often reported in elderly patients. This is not surprising since NSAID are widely prescribed in elderly patients for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, and other musculoskeletal diseases. At least 30 percent ofNSAID prescriptions are written for patients 65 years or older.' Suprofenassociated acute flank pain and anuria is the exception. This condition is more common in young than elderly patients." The adverse renal effects of NSAID can present in a variety of ways. Renal toxicity can manifest as an allergic reaction (interstitial nephritis), acute ischemic renal insufficiency due to inhibition of renal prostaglandins, or analgesic nephropathy," We will focus on the acute renal effects of NSAID. Patients with decreased effective or actual circulating blood volume are at the greatest risk of acute ischemic renal insufficiency when given NSAID. These clinical conditions result in vasodilatory renal prostaglandins counteracting tbe vasoconstricting effects of angiotensin II and catecholamines. Inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis results in unopposed renal vasoconstriction and decrements in renal function.
