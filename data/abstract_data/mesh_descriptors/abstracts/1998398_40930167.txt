Vascular risk factors and erectile dysfunction: ‘sexing‐up’ the importance of lifestyle changes
Penile erection is a vascular event under neural control and is determined by the balance between arterial inflow and venous outflow. Abnormalities of the arterial endothelium will therefore affect erectile function and it is the recognition of the endothelial dysfunction common denominator that links erectile dysfunction (ED) to coronary artery disease (CAD) that has stimulated basic and clinical research in this area (1,2). The greater weight of endothelium relative to the small penile arteries (1–2 mm) has been one explanation for the evidence that ED often precedes a cardiac event by 2–3 years and why ED is common in men with CAD. In addition, the role of inflammatory markers reflecting low-grade systemic inflammation has provided further insights into the EDCAD link (3). So far, much of the research has focused on the mechanisms and clinical relevance of the ED presentation especially in the absence of cardiac symptoms. However, evidence is now accumulating that not only links lifestyle to developing ED (and CAD) but that also suggests that by lifestyle modification alone ED (and CAD) may improve. In the second Princeton Consensus on Sexual Dysfunction and Cardiac Risk, the role of lifestyle factors and their modification by intervention leading to clinical benefit was emphasised (4). Obesity and physical inactivity significantly increase the risk of developing ED. In the Health Professionals Follow-up study obesity nearly doubled the risk of developing ED and in the Global Study of Attitudes and Behaviour 31.8% of men who had less than average levels of physical activity had ED compared with 13.9% who exercised more than average (5,6). The question that needed answering was whether lifestyle intervention would lead to a decrease in ED. In a study of 110 obese men aged 35–55 years with a BMI of ‡ 30 kg/m, Esposito et al. (7) assessed the degree to which weight loss combined with increased physical activity could affect erectile function. None were diabetic, hypertensive or hyperlipidaemic. In a single blind fashion half were randomised to active intervention and half given general information about healthy eating and exercise. The intervention group were given detailed advice about how to lose 10% or more weight, attended monthly group sessions, were set targets, taught how to reduce calories and allocated food diaries. As well as detailed advice about food types they also had personal activity training including advice on walking, and games such as soccer and swimming. After 1 year of monthly meetings with their nutritionalists and exercise trainers they met twice monthly for a further year. When assessed at 2 years the BMI had decreased from 36.9 to 31.2 kg/m in the intervention group and 36.4 to 35.7 kg/m in the control group (p < 0.001). Of interest, the level of the inflammatory markers, interleukin 6 (p 1⁄4 0.03) and C-reactive protein (CRP), also decreased (p 1⁄4 0.02). Physical activity increased more in the intervention group from 48 to 195 minutes per week compared with 51 to 84 minutes per week in the controls (p < 0.001). ED improved significantly more in the intervention group with their International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) score increasing from a mean of 13.9 to 17 (p < 0.001) and in 17 men the score was over 22. The mean score in the control group was stable (13.5 to 13.6) although three achieved a score over 22. The authors showed, using multivariate analysis, that improvement in BMI and physical activity as well as CRP were independently and significantly associated with improved IIEF score. They concluded that onethird of obese men with ED can improve their erectile function as a result of intensive lifestyle changes. In the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, ED was associated with the level of physical activity as well as BMI (5). When the men were categorised according to their level of physical fitness the least active (higher levels of sedentary behaviour) were independently linked to ED. All types of exercise reduced the incidence of ED. Running at least 2.5 h a week was associated with a 30% relative risk reduction for ED when compared with no regular activity and 1.5 h running or 3 h rigorous outdoor work reduced the relative risk by 20%. Therefore with obesity increasing the incidence of ED by 30% and weight loss combined with increased physical activity reducing ED by 30% the importance of lifestyle intervention is clear. Sex sells, so perhaps our focus on CAD risk reduction could be ‘sexed-up’ by promoting the sexual benefits.
