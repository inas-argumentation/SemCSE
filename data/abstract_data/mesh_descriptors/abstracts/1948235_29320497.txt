"Proteinuria" itself cannot be taken as a definite marker of improved renal function.
Dear Sir, In a recent report by Esnault et al. [1], furosemide uptitration with half-dose ramipril (5 mg/day) and valsartan (80 mg/day) was found to better decrease proteinuria than a combination of half-dose ramipril (5 mg/day) and valsartan (80 mg/day), and combined full dose of ramipril (10 mg/day) and valsartan (160 mg/day). They concluded that the antiproteinuric effect of diuretics was partly due to a decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate and noted that more symptomatic hypotensive episodes occurred. The observation is certainly of interest, but I would like to raise a few issues regarding the role of proteinuria in renal diseases. Firstly, in the ONTARGET Study [2], the combination of telmisartan and ramipril indeed reduced proteinuria, in patientswith high risk of vascular events andwith diabetes, to a greater extent thanmonotherapy. Nevertheless, combination therapy worsened major renal outcomes, such as the need of any dialysis, doubling of serum creatinine and death at long-term follow-up. Secondly, in a recent letter, the author postulated that blockade of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) in combination with diuretics may increase urine excretion and subsequently increase fluid intake via drinking, which has been suggested as the mechanism for reduced microalbuminuria in diabetic patients [3]. Thirdly, what is the benefit if we reduce proteinuria at the expense of haemodynamic stability? In conclusion, an add-on diuretic with RAAS blockers was demonstrated to decrease the extent of proteinuria in a short-term trial [1]. However, as the ONTARGET Study suggested, proteinuria itself cannot be taken as a definite marker of improved renal function [2]. Instead, it would be of interest to study whether add-on diuretics with RAAS blockers can improve major renal outcomes and to determine which specific proteinuric renal diseases benefit most from a combination therapy.
