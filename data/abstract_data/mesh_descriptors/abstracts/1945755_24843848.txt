cDNA Arrays: The Ups and Downs
Changes in gene expression levels during pathological situations alter normal cellular behaviour and may lead to various abnormalities, such as cardiac hypertrophy. Identification of abnormally expressed genes during (patho)physiological conditions may increase our knowledge of affected pathways, provide markers for prognosis and early diagnosis and reveal new drug target sites. In molecular biology, RNA expression levels are usually studied using techniques such as Northern blotting, RNase protection assays and quantitative reverse transcription (RT)-PCR. However, using these approaches, only a single gene of interest can be studied in one experiment. Since various genes may be differentially expressed during certain conditions, there is an increasing need for large-scale gene expression analysis. The recent introduction of DNA microarray technology is considered an important development in the field of, among others, gene expression profiling. Sakata et al. in this issue of Cardiovascular Drugs and Therapy [1] used this technique to study alterations in gene expression during acute myocardial infarction in mice. cDNA microarrays consist of a miniaturised spotted pattern of thousands of DNA sequences (each corresponding to a specific gene) attached to a nylon membrane, which are used for gene expression profiling. For this, messenger RNA (mRNA) is extracted from both healthy and diseased cellular or tissue sources, converted into the more stable cDNA (copy DNA) and radioactively labelled. The labelled “healthy” and “diseased” cDNA pools are subsequently hybridised to two separate sets of nylon arrays whereby each cDNA will hybridise to its complementary sequence on the membrane (Fig. 1). After washing, the radioactive signal at each spot on the array represents a quantitative measurement of the expression level of the particular gene and can be compared between healthy and diseased arrays. This technique allows the simultaneous analysis of mRNA levels of thousands of genes in a single hybridisation experiment. For correct interpretation of the results obtained from cDNA microarrays, it is important to take into account methodological considerations such as the type of DNA arrays used, microarray design, hybridisation, signal sensitivity and data analysis (reviewed by van Hal et al. [2]). Thousands of cDNA sequences are spotted on the arrays; obviously, only DNA sequences that are available can be spotted. The Humane Genome Project has and will continue to provide an increasing number of gene specific sequences. However, this is not the case for most other species such as rat, rabbit and pig. Since many experimental protocols can only be performed using whole intact organs or in situ experiments, many experiments will continue to be performed in non-human material, with only a limited amount of DNA sequences available for microarray analysis. Another important issue is the nature of the DNA spotted on the arrays either randomly selected cDNA clones or non-randomly selected known genes can be used or a combination of both. As discussed by Abdellatif, the random approach is biased toward detection of mRNAs of higher abundance, whereas the selected use of genes such as those isolated from specific tissues may also give biased results [3]. In any case, without full knowledge of the proteins encoded by each DNA sequence, the results remain difficult to interpret. In addition, since large parts of DNA sequences are highly conserved throughout gene families, one cannot be sure that observed changes reflect expression differences of one specific gene or a whole family of similar genes (isoforms). This problem can be overcome by spotting only isoform-specific parts of DNA sequences which is unfortunately a laborious task and not often performed. DNA array sensitivity may constitute another potential drawback. In principle, only genes that are relatively abundantly present in the tissue studied will be visible on the array. Therefore, it will be impossible to study low-abundant genes such as those encoding certain ion channel proteins or signalling molecules. Furthermore, in order to find a significant difference in mRNA expression, an up or downregulation of at least twoto threefold is required. An increase in mRNA expression of only 40% may already have profound biological consequences but it will not be detected on microarrays.
