Thirteen intracranial nails: no neurological and neuropsychological disabilities
Dear Editor, Penetrating injuries constitute a small percentage of all head injuries. They can be divided in missile and nonmissile injuries. We present an unusual case of nonmissile injury—a patient with 13 intracerebral nails—and focus on imaging techniques and outcome. A 55-year-old male patient with a history of depression presented to the emergency room with a Glascow Coma Scale (GCS) of 12 (E3M6V3) and two nails penetrating the forehead. The left temple was swollen. No further deficits were observed. A head CT (angiogram) showed 13 intracranial nails, located bi-temporally and around the circle of Willis (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7). We only observed some traumatic subarachnoid blood along the right convexity. The patient was transferred to the operating room. On both sides, we were not able to locate all nailheads after removal of the boneflap. To determine the exact location of these missing nails, we used ultrasound guidance (Fig. 8). All nails could be removed. A postoperative CT scan demonstrated some subarachnoid blood in the basal cisterns and right temporal contusion where a folded nail was extracted. The postoperative course was uncomplicated, and the patient made a complete neurological recovery. The patient had amnesia for the event, but no other cognitive disturbances could be detected by neuropsychological assessment. The patient went home 6 weeks later; the patient reported feeling well, with no suicidal ideation. This was confirmed by psychiatric assessment. The incidence of nail-gun injuries tripled between 1991 and 2005 [2]. A small percentage of these injuries are intracranial. Although mortality rates are high and range from 8 to 40 % [1, 4, 10], our case shows that intracerebral nail-gun injuries can have good neurological and neuropsychological outcomes. Missing these injuries is possible. Entrance wounds can be small without nails visible on physical examination [5] and minimal neurological deficits [8, 10]. Therefore, non-contrast CT scanning is the key imaging modality to visualize foreign objects and their course, hemorrhage, and bone and parenchymal injury [6, 8]. For vascular damage, cerebral angiography is the ‘gold standard,’ although CT angiography is increasingly being used and more readily available. However, as in our case, scatter artifacts may compromise imaging of key structures.
