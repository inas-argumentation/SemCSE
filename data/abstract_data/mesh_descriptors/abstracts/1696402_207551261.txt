What patients newly diagnosed with clinically localized prostate cancer need to see: an editorial dialogue.
Iwas recently approachedby amanwhowas diagnosedwith clinically localized prostate cancer (PCa). Not surprisingly, he was confused by his treatment options and unsure about which one he should pick. He asked my advice on how I would approach the problem. I told him I would getmultiple opinions with the goal of constructing what David Eddy described long ago as a ‘‘balance sheet’’ [1], a table of expected benefits and harms associatedwith each treatment option. I also pointed out that he needed amodern version of this tool, with probabilities tailored to himself. The newer incarnation goes by the label of a comparative effectiveness table [2]. The man welcomed this approach and set about constructing this table. He got a second opinion in the form of a quite detailed and lengthy letter from a highly respected and talented academic urologist. The man, however, was having trouble extracting information from the letter to place in his table and askedme for help. In going through the man’s letter, the best I could do is shown in Table 1. Although this second opinion letter is probably one of the most thorough the man could have expected to receive, many cells of the table are essentially left blank, which confuses ‘‘unknown’’ with 0. Additionally, terms such as low, rather than actual numbers, confuse interpretation. We have a long way to go to fill the uncertainty gap. Much more data need to be collected, and many statistical models remain to be built and tested. The most visionary of the disease management teams will work together, across specialties, to build a comprehensive comparative effectiveness table system that is shared by all patients that a disease management team sees.
