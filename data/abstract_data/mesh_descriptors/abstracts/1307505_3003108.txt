Natural History of Gastric Cancer After Helicobacter pylori Eradication in Japan: After Endoscopic Resection, After Treatment of the General Population, and Naturally
There are a number of important unanswered questions regarding the effects of Helicobacter pylori eradication and gastric cancer. One important question is, “Does H. pylori eradication reduce the incidence of subsequent gastric cancers after endoscopic mucosal resection of early gastric cancer?” In this issue of Helicobacter , the investigators of a large study in Japan provide the rationale for their study design and plans for analyses [1]. We review their assumptions regarding the anticipated cancer incidence and what changes they expect to occur as well as the assumptions underlying the design of community-based studies. The magnitude of the expected outcome of H. pylori eradication on the incidence of gastric cancer is dependent on the population studied. In the general population, the incidence of gastric cancer increases with age. This “moving target” makes it difficult to know whether apparently successful therapy reduced or reversed the natural tendency of the incidence to increase. One would theoretically like to be able to calculate the outcome compared to the cancer incidence at the time of randomization but because the age-specific increase in incidence is exponential, one generally does not have that information. However, if one knows the age-specific gastric cancer incidence for the general population from which the sample was drawn, one can reasonably assume that the course of the control group is similar to that of the general population and one can use that available age and incidence data to estimate the cancer incidence of the patients at time zero. An example of this approach is illustrated using data from a recent Chinese study [2] and we thank Dr Benjamin C.W. Wong for providing specific data regarding cancer incidence and ages from that population. In that study, the overall incidence of gastric cancer was 147/100,000 per year. The incidence per 100,000 of gastric cancer after a follow-up of 7.5 years among those whose H. pylori infection was untreated was 180.4 (placebo group) compared to 114.2 for those who received triple therapy. The average age of the participants at the end of the 7.5-year trial was approximately 50 years. Figure 1 shows a plot of those data superimposed on the gastric cancer incidence for ages 40–65 years for Japanese men in 1970. We chose those particular data because comparable data from China were unavailable, both populations were high-risk populations, and the incidence of gastric cancer at the end of 7.5 years of follow-up in the Chinese control group was approximately the same as in Japanese men of the same age. Using the Japanese age-specific incidence data, we were able to estimate the population risk at the time of randomization as approximately 83/100,000 (Fig. 1). Using that incidence as the starting point, we then plotted the outcome of H. pylori eradication on cancer incidence in the treatment group. As shown in Fig. 1, the cancer incidence increased in both groups but the placebo group increased at a higher rate.
