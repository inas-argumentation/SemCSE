Stevens–Johnson/toxic epidermal necrolysis overlap syndrome following lenalidomide treatment for multiple myeloma relapse after allogeneic transplantation
Dear Editor, A 61-year-old patient with IgA kappa multiple myeloma presented in our emergency department with fever and a generalized, rapidly evolving maculopapular rash involving the entire integument with target lesions, bullae (with positive Nikolski sign), skin sloughing, oral mucous membrane erosions, and crusting (Fig. 1a–d), including the soles (Fig. 1e) and palms (Fig. 1f). The patient also showed involvement of the perianal and genital mucous membranes (not shown). Herpes simplex virus (HSV) could not be isolated from the oral lesions, and other infectious causes were excluded. Histology demonstrated keratinocyte necrosis, epidermal necrolysis, subepidermal detachment, and a moderately dense mononuclear cell infiltrate of the papillary dermis (Fig. 2). The patient had received lenalidomide (Revlimid) due to a relapsed myeloma after an allogeneic peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (allogeneic PBSCT) and had responded well. However, with the start of the second lenalidomide cycle, the severe cutaneous reaction occurred. Based on typical skin lesions, the extent of skin sloughing (>10% but <30% of body surface), skin histology, and drug history, the diagnosis of Stevens–Johnson/toxic epidermal necrolysis overlap syndrome (SJS/TEN) triggered by lenalidomide was made. Discontinuation of lenalidomide, systemic corticosteroids, and intensive supportive care measures led to clinical recovery, and the patient was discharged from our medical center after 3 weeks of treatment. In June 2007, the patient had been diagnosed with Durie and Salmon stage IIIB and ISS III multiple myeloma, showing adverse risk features at initial diagnosis, such as extramedullary lesions in the lumbar spine, hypercalcemia, renal failure (creatinine, 4.1 mg/dl), and anemia (7.0 mg/dl) [1]. The initial bone marrow infiltration rate was 90%, IgA was 14.36 g/l, and serum kappa light chains were 11,200 mg/l. Fluorescence in situ hybridization confirmed high-risk features with deletion of chromosome 13 [del13q14], 17p [del(17p)], and t(4;14). Therapy was initiated with high-dose dexamethasone, plasmapheresis, and irradiation of the lumbar spine, leading to prompt myeloma response and pain relief. Thereafter, the patient was enrolled in the German Study Group Multiple Myeloma (DSMM XI) trial and received three induction cycles of bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, and dexamethasone followed by stem cell mobilization, autologous PBSCT performed in complete remission, and matchedunrelated allogeneic PBSCT. The patient developed a mild cutaneous chronic GvHD, which was well controlled with cyclosporine A and prednisone. Ten months after the allogeneic PBSCT, the immunosuppression was terminated, and the patient remained in very good partial remission. However, 1 year after the allogeneic PBSCT, rapidly evolving first disease relapse occurred, again presenting with renal failure and paraplegia due to extramedullary paraspinal myeloma lesions. The patient immediately received bortezomib, hemodialysis, local resection of the spinal myeloma lesions, and irradiation. Thereafter, the patient was consolidated with 12 cycles of bortezomib, dexamethasone, and cyclophosphamide [2] followed by donor lymphocyte infusions without recurrence of cutaneous GvHD. R.
