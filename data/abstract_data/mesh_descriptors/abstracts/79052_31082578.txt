Confrontations with Difficult Patients: The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly.
Physicians are taught to remain empathic toward, negotiate with, and educate patients. Despite this training, some patients continue to pose management challenges for physicians. As a consequence, fatigue and negative emotions (such as anger, frustration, helplessness, exasperation, resentment, contempt, or even hatred) can develop. At times, doubts about one’s professional identity or personal self-worth can also surface. Tolerance, nonjudgmental listening, use of humor, thoughtful planning for patient encounters, and extra time spent onmanaging difficult patients are often recommended techniques to use in these trying circumstances. Confrontation can also serve as an effective style of communication to provide much needed care for difficult patients. We present the case of a young woman who was suspected of inoculating her intravenous lines with drainage from infected wounds (resulting in recurrent bacteremia). Although the primary team had their suspicions as to the etiology of her recurrent infections, they were uncertain about whether and how to confront her. They posed several questions about the confrontation of the patient, including the following: Who should confront her?Whatwould preparation for a confrontation entail? What communication techniques should be used? When should she be confronted? What content should be contained in the confrontation?Where should the confrontation take place?What were the risks and benefits of the confrontation? We discuss the case and provide guidelines on how confrontations can be therapeutic, improve care, and enhance the doctor-patient relationship.
