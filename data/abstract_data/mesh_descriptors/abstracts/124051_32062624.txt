Folate, antifolates, and folate analogs in Pediatric Oncology
In the late 1940's antifolates entered the arena of anti-cancer therapy when aminopterin (AMT) was used successfully to induce remissions in children with acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL) [1]. Further investigations of other antifolates led to preclinical trials using methotrexate (MTX). In comparison studies, MTX was found to have a better therapeutic index with a higher tumor to normal tissue toxicity ratio, and at more predictable dose levels [2, 3]. MTX subsequently emerged as the dominant antifolate used clinically. It is an essential drug in the chemotherapy regimens used to treat various malignancies including leukemias, lymphomas, osteosarcoma, breast cancer, choriocarcinoma, and head and neck cancers. Some cancers however are inherently resistant to MTX or through the course of therapy acquire resistance to MTX via selection. This ultimately limits its efficacy. New antifolate compounds are being developed as well as being tested for unique and/or more potent mechanisms of action to improve therapies and circumvent MTX resistance. The importance of folate metabolism and folate mediated reactions is further demonstrated by the spectrum of nonmalignant disorders, such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and other inflammatory disorders such as asttuna and graft versus host disease that are amenable to folate analog therapy. Antifolates have also found uses as antimicrobial agents (trimethoprim-sulfa and trimetrexate) in bacterial and parasitic infections, and in opportunistic infections encountered in patients with HIV infection [4]. Lessons learned from using folate analogs to treat patients with these disorders have provided information to help develop new strategies for treatment of patients with cancer.
