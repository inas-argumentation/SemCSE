All that is obvious is not clear: What is the origin of throbbing pain in migraine?
The notion that throbbing pain in the head necessarily implies a change in vessel caliber, active congestion [14], is not new. The International Classification of Headache Disorders includes throbbing amongst the diagnostic criteria for migraine, although it is not a compulsory feature [17]. Triptans, serotonin 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonists [13], were developed based on the hypothesis that a dilatory (and pathophysiological) effect of serotonin on extracranial vessels could be reversed by a suitable agonist [20]. The hypothesis was built on the accepted wisdom that ergot alkaloids, such as ergotamine and dihydroergotamine, tetracylic ergoline derivatives [6], were effective as vasoconstrictors of the dilated vessels in migraine [30]. Taken together these findings all offered a neat way to think about migraine pain and its treatment [32]. Unfortunately, once a concept is enshrined in medical lore exculpatory evidence seems to be needed well in excess of any criminal proceeding: vessels are guilty and only when they are proven innocent we can re-think migraine pathophysiology. On this difficult base, and building upon earlier work of Ahn [1], Mo and colleagues in this issue of Pain [23] now question the dogma. The authors report on a patient with throbbing head pain, whose arterial pulse and EEG were simultaneously recorded. The throbbing rate so reported was 48 bpm, while the heart rate was 68 bpm. Clearly, the throbbing rate did not correspond to heart rate. On the other hand, the authors found 8-12 Hz alpha power increased with intensity of the throbbing. Moreover, modulations of alpha power coincided with the timing of the throbbing as reported by the patient, and this the degree of the relationship, or coherence, was proportional to the intensity of the throbbing. In previous work the same group explored the relationship between reported throbbing pain, 62 bpm, and arterial pulse rate, 80 bpm, in twenty patients. Two patients had rates that fell in and out of sync [1]. Again the percept of throbbing was not simply related to heart rate. How does this refreshingly heretical view stand when compared to other data? Do vessels really dilate in migraine? The last word on this question has no doubt yet to be written. Clearly not all patients with migraine complain of throbbing pain, and occlusion of flow does not control all migraine pain [10]. In fact, some studies have found no change in vessels using MRA methods [28], while others have slightly different findings, although modest in nature [4]. In any event, what is not explained at all by the vasodilation concept is why a potent vasodilator like calcitonin gene-related peptide can trigger migraine in migraineurs, while in controls only a modest 1 out of 10 discomfort, unaffected by sumatriptan, was reported [5]. Moreover, there was no relationship in temporal terms between the vessel changes induced by sumatriptan and headache resolution [22]. It is, therefore, plausible that vessels do not dilate to cause the pain in any simple one-to-one sense.
