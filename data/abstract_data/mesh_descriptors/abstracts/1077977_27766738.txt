Re: lack of benefit from paracetamol (acetaminophen) for palliative cancer patients.
To the Editor: The well-conducted study of Israel et al., ‘‘Lack of benefit from paracetamol (acetaminophen) for palliative cancer patients requiring high-dose strong opioids: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover trial,’’ raises perhaps more questions than it answers. Although adequately powered, there remains a 20% chance that the study did not detect a clinical meaningful (as defined by the authors) difference between paracetamol and placebo, when added to high-dose opioids. As the authors state, there also is the question whether patients on lower doses of opioids do benefit from paracetamol. Finally, assuming that the findings of the study are accurate, it is possible that subgroups of patients might benefit from paracetamol, despite the average subject in this study not benefiting. For example, bone pain is sometimes refractory to opioids, and the addition of paracetamol might improve the pain. As patients on increasing doses of opioids were ineligible for the study, could paracetamol benefit patients with unstable pain? Unfortunately, the way the study was reported ina general practice bulletin board in Australia has resulted in general practitioners concluding that paracetamol does not work for cancer pain. So should practice change as a result of this study? Individualized medication effectiveness tests, which are n1⁄4 1 placebo studies of individual patients, have been advocated for chronic pain and other conditions. Such an approach might involve an unacceptable burden to palliative care patients, although more scientifically rigorous than patient impression. Unfortunately, like many situations in medicine, the individual patient may have to decide whether the burden, and potential side effects (such as liver toxicity), of taking paracetamol is worth any improvement in pain or lessening of opioid side effects that they perceive, even if this decision is based on only partial information. It certainly cannot be stated emphatically, as a result this study, that paracetamol has no role in the control of cancer pain, even in patients on high-dose opioids. However, the study does give one pause in advocating the addition of paracetamol to strong opioids, as a matter of policy, irrespective of the clinical situation, if that is, indeed, what palliative care professionals have been doing.
