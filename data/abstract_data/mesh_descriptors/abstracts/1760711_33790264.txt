The Sustainability of Recovery-Oriented Evidence-Based Practices
Bond et al. (2014) illuminate the challenges of sustaining EBP, which is timely given national efforts to expand EBP throughout mental health systems (Karlin and Cross 2014). The authors primarily examine the sustainability of EBP with respect to leadership, funding, and infrastructure (such as training, supervision, and fidelity monitoring), but do not examine the topic through the lens of recovery. For the sake of those readers who are unfamiliar with the recovery literature, I feel compelled to write a brief correspondence to locate Bond and colleagues’ article vis-à-vis their larger body of work. By doing so, I hope to underscore the importance of promoting the sustainability of EBP while simultaneously contending with the broader challenge of implementing, disseminating, and sustaining recovery-oriented services. What the authors do not mention explicitly, but suggest in their other writings, is that EBP should be situated within the recovery paradigm, which is an ascending precedent for mental health care. Recovery has been generally understood as both an outcome of symptom remission, and a process of pursuing broader life goals despite the effects and side effects of mental illness (Davidson and Roe 2007). It is ‘‘a journey of healing and transformation enabling a person with a mental health problem to live a meaningful life in a community of his or her choice while striving to achieve his or her potential’’ (SAMHSA 2005). More than a decade has passed since the New Freedom Commission on Mental Health recast the ethos of mental health care, calling for a nation-wide effort to replace the traditional medical model with the recovery paradigm (Hogan 2003). Still organizations are figuring out how to realize this vision (See Slade 2009a, b). Over the years, EBP have not always been viewed as consonant with recovery, as some have argued that EBP only serve the medical model’s goal of achieving immediate symptom remission (Anthony et al. 2003). The medical model has been a pervasive and seemingly incorrigible force underlying an entire managed care system that evolved to finance the treatment of ‘billable’ DSM diagnoses using the most cost-effective interventions. As such, EBP have been packaged into manualized protocols to maximize their reproducibility and communicability, making them more likely to proliferate and endure across institutional environments (see Hasselbladh and Kallinikos 2000). Additionally, funding based on fidelity measures prescribe consistent implementation and dissemination of EBP. But scholars question whether EBP within a medical model can successfully address long-term recovery goals, such as self-actualization and community integration (see Hoge et al. 1998; Bond et al. 2004; Drake 2005). Recent studies have shown that clinical measures for many EBP do not assess key aspects of recovery as defined by the consumer (Andresen et al. 2010). This is in large part because recovery entails multiple dimensions (Onken et al. 2007; Whitley and Drake 2010), including intrapersonal improvements (e.g. a stronger sense of personal responsibility for managing one’s own illness) as well as functional improvements (e.g. returning to work, attending church services, participating in recreational activities with friends, voting and volunteering) (Noordsy et al. 2002). EBP in and of themselves are insufficient in accomplishing these goals, and should instead be regarded as some of the technologies available to support the person’s pursuit of recovery (See: Davidson et al. 2009; Mueser 2012).
