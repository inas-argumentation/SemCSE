MAGNESIUM, CALCIUM, AND PARATHYROID HORMONE INTERACTIONS
The first published observations concerning magnesium (Mg) deficiency in man were those of Hirschfelder; in a relatively small number of hypomagnesemic patients with renal insufficiency or hypoparathyroidism, he noted twitching and convulsions, some of which improved with magnesium.' Flink and his associates advanced knowledge of human Mg depletion with their studies in patients with acute alcoholism superimposed on chronic alcoholism and in others who had been maintained on prolonged intravenous fluids without Mg.' Flink et al. described personality changes, tremors, athetoid movements, and convulsions and made certain observations relative to Mg and potassium levels. Nevertheless, the symptomatology and biochemical abnormalities ascribable to deficiency of Mg were not adequately defined and were a matter of some disagreement despite various individual case reports. This lack of clarity was attributable to two major facts: firstly, despite various attempts, experimental symptomatic human deficiency had not been induced; and secondly, the accounts of symptomatic depletion in patients always occurred in the setting of predisposing diseases and clinical situations that were usually complex (TABLE 1). In such circumstances, associated complex and uncontrollable variables-such as multiple dietary inadequacies, metabolic abnormalities, manifestations of basic disease, severe infection, and treatments given in close proximity to the Mg supplementation-made it difficult and potentially misleading to ascribe certain clinical manifestations specifically to Mg deficiency. Furthermore, at that time, the clinical literature was in disagreement concerning the relation of Mg deficiency to serum calcium (Ca) levels and to the occurrence of latent or overt tetany-defined here as a positive Trousseau sign or spontaneous carpopedal spasm. Some investigators expressed the opinion that tetany was not a manifestation of Mg deficiency, per se, and that a coexisting hypocalcemia was not related to the Mg depletion. Others suggested that there was a relationship, since hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, and neurological symptoms improved in relation to Mg therapy but not to Ca. Still another type of clinical picture was reported in which hyperimtability, tetany, and other neuromuscular abnormalities occurred in the setting of hypomagnesemia but where the serum Ca was normal; this syndrome was claimed to respond to Mg but not to Ca salts.
