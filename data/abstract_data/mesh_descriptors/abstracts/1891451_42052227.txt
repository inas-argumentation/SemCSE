Surrogate Markers in Oncology Practice and Anticancer Drug Development: Lessons Learned from Antiretroviral Therapy?
The lives of patients with advanced cancer and AIDS are threatened by shortened survival and emergence of drug resistance or refractoriness to current therapies. In light of this, it is imperative for clinicians taking care of these patients to develop alternative or substitute means, true clinical ‘‘surrogate markers,’’ with which to predict and gauge the effectiveness and biologic activity of new therapies as they enter the clinic rather than simply observing patients for toxicity and survival to determine outcome. The identification of reliable surrogate markers in the clinic will greatly facilitate the development of new anticancer agents and shorten the timeline of drug development if clinically validated and proven to be predictive of outcome. In this regard, the article by Petrella and colleagues is prescient. [1] Infectious disease clinicians, immunologists, and virologists were quick to ascertain the linkage of level of immune function, as assessed by CD4 lymphocyte count, as a true surrogate of clinical outcome in patients with underlying HIV infection and that improvement in CD4 count correlated with better survival. [2,3] With the advent of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technology and the capacity to assess viral replication (HIV-1 RNA copies per mL of plasma), it is now possible to monitor patients receiving active antiretroviral therapy to achieve more effective suppression of HIV replication (i.e., undetectable viral load) and sustained elevations in CD4 lymphocyte counts. Partial immune restoration is seen after suppression of HIV-1 replication, and this improvement in immune phenotype and function appears to be important in achieving both reduction in short-term opportunistic infectious complications and prolongation of overall survival. [4] These outcomes correspond with the short-term therapeutic goal of diminishing viral load [targeting at least a 1.0 log10 decrease at two to eight weeks and no detectable virus (< 50 copies/mL) at four to six months of therapy] and the long-term goal of sustaining CD4 lymphocyte counts.
