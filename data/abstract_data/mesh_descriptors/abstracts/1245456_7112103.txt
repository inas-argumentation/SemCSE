No association of polymorphisms in the cell polarity gene SCRIB with breast cancer risk in German population
We read with great interest the recent paper by Dunnebier et al. [1], which investigated whether genetic variability in the Drosophila Scribble (SCRIB) tumor suppressor gene impacts breast carcinogenesis and tumor pathology. The study analyzed five tag single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in SCRIB (rs6558394, rs7844493, rs11786637, rs7822430, and rs4875054), and the results suggest that genetic variability in the SCRIB polarity gene does not contribute to breast cancer (BC) development. Nevertheless, several issues seem worth commenting. Firstly, in the study, associations between genetic variables in the SCRIB and BC risk were analyzed by conditional logistic regression adjusted for six potential epidemiological BC risk factors (menopausal status, family history of breast or ovarian cancer, use of oral contraceptives, use of hormone therapy, body mass index, and smoking). Alcohol consumption is a well-established risk factor for BC [2]. A recent meta-analysis including 98 studies showed a 10% increase in BC risk per 10 g of alcohol consumed per day [3]. Unfortunately, alcohol consumption was not included in the potential epidemiological BC risk factors in the study, which may be distorting the associations between genetic variables in the SCRIB and BC risk. Secondly, among the recruited individuals, DNA samples were available for 1,021 (89%) cases and 1,015 (88%) controls, and the response rate to information on potential epidemiological BC risk factors was 88% for cases and 67% for controls. In epidemiological studies, nonresponse may introduce bias and limit generalizability [4]. Thus, nonresponse bias may be distorting the current results. It may be helpful for exploring the associations between genetic variables in the SCRIB and BC risk to analyze the reasons for nonresponse. Thirdly, they concluded that genetic variability in the SCRIB polarity gene does not contribute to BC development. But their results are insufficient to support the conclusion. Their results only suggest that genetic variability in the SCRIB polarity gene does not contribute to BC development in German population. To reach a definitive conclusion, further gene–gene and gene–environment interactions studies based on larger sample size are still needed. We believe that our remarks will contribute to more accurate elaboration of the results presented by Dunnebier et al. [1].
