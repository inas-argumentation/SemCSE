Problem solved: erectile dysfunction (ED) = early death (ED)
The Second Princeton Consensus on sexual dysfunction and cardiac risk concluded: ‘The recognition of ED (erectile dysfunction) as a warning sign of silent vascular disease has led to the concept that a man with ED and no cardiac symptoms is a cardiac (or vascular) patient until proved otherwise’ (1). Several studies have documented the presence of coronary artery disease (CAD), which is asymptomatic in men presenting with ED (2–5). The artery size hypothesis compares the smaller penile arteries (1–2 mm) with the larger coronary arteries (3–4 mm) where the same degree of endothelial dysfunction will be symptomatic (ED) in the smaller vessels, but subclinical in the larger vessels (CAD) (6). Exercise electrocardiography does not detect non-obstructive coronary lesions that can be identified on computer tomography coronary angiography (7). Lipid rich non-obstructive coronary plaque is recognised to be vulnerable to rupture, precipitating an acute coronary syndrome or sudden death. Montorsi and colleagues evaluating 300 patients with acute chest pain and documented angiographic CAD identified the development of ED on average 3 years before the acute event in 49%, of which 67% had no cardiac symptoms (8). The link between ED and CAD presenting acutely can be explained by the rupture of the clinically silent coronary plaque. With a time window between ED and a CAD presentation averaging 3 years (range 2–5 years), the prevention of CAD by the early detection of ED has been advocated by many authors, leading to the Evidence-Based Guidance and Consensus published in this edition of the IJCP (9–11). The International Society of Sexual Medicine Cardiovascular Committee concluded ‘ED is a marker for silent CAD that needs to be excluded’ (12). However, it is not just cardiovascular disease (CAD) events that we are detecting, it is also increased mortality. In a prospective populationbased study of 1709 men followed for 15 years, the association between ED and all-cause as well as CVD mortality was evaluated (13). Of 403 men who survived, data were complete in 371 men. After adjustment for age, body mass index, alcohol consumption, physical activity, smoking, hypertension and diabetes, ED was significantly associated with hazard ratios (HR) of 1.26 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.01–1.57] for all-cause mortality and 1.43 (95% CI 1.00–2.05) for CVD mortality. This study extends the finding that ED is a powerful predictor of CVD mortality and morbidity in diabetic patients with silent CAD, in whom importantly, treatment with statins reduced the risk of cardiac events in diabetic men with ED (14), to the general population of men with ED. In the ONTARGET ⁄ TRANSCEND trial, ED was evaluated as a predictor of events in men with cardiovascular disease (15). In this study of 1549 men, ED predicted all-cause death (HR 1.84; 95% CI 1.21–1.81, p = 0.005), cardiovascular death (HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.13–3.29, p = 0.016), myocardial infarction (HR 2.02, 95% CI 1.13–3.58, p = 0.017) and hospitalisation for heart failure (HR 1.2, 95% CI 0.64–2.26, p = 0.563). The results remained after adjustment for possible confounders. Therefore, identifying ED as an early warning of endothelial dysfunction and atheroma beyond the penile arteries offers the opportunity for aggressive vascular risk reduction to reduce further cardiovascular events (10). This is particularly important in view of the sevenfold increased risk of a cardiovascular event in men under 40 years of age with ED and no cardiac history, compared with similar aged men without ED (16). Death is a sharp endpoint – time to blunt it and use ED as a means of reducing short and long-term risk. When the penis heads in the wrong direction, the heart need not follow.
