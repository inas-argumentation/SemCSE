Transanal endoscopic microsurgery following neoadjuvant chemoradiation therapy in rectal cancer: a word of caution about patient selection?
DISEASES OF THE COLON & RECTUM VOLUME 56: 1 (2013) The treatment of rectal cancer has improved dramatically in recent years. The adoption of the principles of sharp mesorectal excision, the introduction of new imaging studies that facilitate greater accuracy in preoperative staging, and the use of neoadjuvant radiation and chemotherapy have contributed to reducing the high rates of local recurrence historically associated with rectal cancer treatment. Advances in perioperative care and new adjuvant systemic therapies have also contributed to improved survival. All of these advances have crystallized into a treatment plan that often includes neoadjuvant chemoradiation therapy (CRT), surgery, and postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. With this long and intensive treatment, patients with rectal cancer are now as likely to survive the disease as patients with similarly staged colon cancer. However, these excellent oncological outcomes are achieved at the expense of significant morbidity and impaired quality of life, secondary to the different components of multimodality therapy. Many of the current research efforts in rectal cancer are aimed at identifying subgroups of patients who could be overtreated by this multimodality therapy, and who might therefore be spared the toxicity and expense of some components of this treatment without compromising risk of tumor recurrence or survival. The PROSPECT trial is investigating the possibility of selectively eliminating the use of neoadjuvant radiation in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer of the mid and upper rectum. Although radiation adds significant toxicity to rectal cancer treatment, most of the long-term sequelae of multimodality therapy are attributable to removal of the rectum. In recent years, surgeons have tried to reduce the side effects of these multimodality therapies by replacing the total mesorectal excision with either a local excision of the tumor, or even by avoiding surgery altogether in patients who respond to CRT. The group from the University of Sao Paolo, led by Professor Habr-Gama, has pioneered the nonoperative management (NOM) of the small but significant group of patients with rectal cancer who develop a sustained clinical complete response (cCR) to CRT. According to their protocol, patients with distal rectal cancer are treated with 5-fluorouracil-based CRT to a total radiation dose of 5040 cGy delivered over 6 weeks. Patients with an incomplete clinical response after a subsequent 8-week hiatus are offered total mesorectal excision (TME). Those with a clinical cCR are followed closely in a NOM protocol. Finally, partial responders with a remaining “small nodule or lesion” undergo full excisional biopsy by local excision or transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM). If the biopsy is positive, radical surgery is recommended; if negative, these patients are observed in the NOM protocol. In the article accompanying this editorial, Perez et al publish the results of a subset of patients treated according to their protocol who were offered TEM as an alternative to TME, because they had small residual tumors or, in some cases, refused a major operation and underwent TEM as palliative treatment. The authors report a local recurrence rate of 15% after 15 months of followup. Considering that the median time to local recurrence in patients with rectal cancer treated by CRT and TME is 3 years, and that almost one third of recurrences are diagnosed after 5 years, the recurrence rate is likely to rise with longer follow-up. In view of these dismal results, the authors raise a word of caution about the use of TEM in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer and a partial clinical response to CRT. However, these results should not be a surprise, considering the criteria used to select patients for TEM. Although preoperative imaging is known to overstage some patients with rectal cancer, and probably understage others, current guidelines recommend baseline clinical staging to make treatment decisions and compare outcomes. The variable degree of tumor regression in response to the neoadjuvant therapy, combined with the fibrosis and edema induced by the radiation, reduces the accuracy of these imaging studies in assessing the depth of tumor penetration into the bowel wall or identifying metastasis to the mesorectal lymph nodes after CRT.
