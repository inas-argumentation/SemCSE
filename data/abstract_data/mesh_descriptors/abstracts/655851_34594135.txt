Efficacy of systemic cefquinome and erythromycin against digital dermatitis in cattle
DIGITAL dermatitis causes significant lameness in cattle throughout the world. However, there are relatively few peerreviewed articles that provide experimental data concerning its treatment. Topical treatment, mainly with antibiotic preparations, is the most commonly reported treatment method (Laven and Logue 2006). The use of systemic antibiotics is less commonly reported, and there are contradictory reports of the effectiveness of this treatment method (Blowey and Sharp 1988, Read and Walker 1998, Rutter and others 2001, Laven and Logue 2006). Nevertheless cefquinome is authorised in the EU for the treatment of digital dermatitis by intramuscular injection. This short communication describes an investigation of the effectiveness of two antibiotics, cefquinome and erythromycin, against digital dermatitis when given systemically compared with topical erythromycin applied via a footbath. Cattle with digital dermatitis were selected from a 650-cow herd in southern England. They were included in the study provided they were pregnant heifers or dry cows within two weeks of drying off, they had digital dermatitis that had not been treated in the past 14 days, and they had no significant infectious foot disease other than digital dermatitis. In total, 53 heifers and 28 dry cows were selected for treatment and the severity of digital dermatitis was scored (Table 1). Cows were then randomly allocated to one of four treatments using a predetermined random allocation table. Treatment 1 comprised a footbath containing 35 g erythromycin (Erythrocin soluble; Ceva Animal Health) per 100 litres. Cattle were walked through the footbath with prewashed feet on two occasions 24 hours apart. Each cow spent a minimum of 30 seconds in the antibiotic footbath. Treatment 2 comprised 1 mg/kg cefquinome (Cephaguard; Intervet Animal Health) administered daily by intramuscular injection for three days. Treatment 3 comprised 1 mg/kg cefquinome administered daily by intramuscular injection for five days. Treatment 4 comprised 10 mg/kg erythromycin (Erythrocin injection; Ceva Animal Health) administered by single intramuscular injection. Treatment 1 was used as the positive control, as it had been shown to be effective in reducing lesion severity in previous studies at ADAS Bridgets Research Centre, Winchester, Hampshire (Laven and Proven 2000, Laven and Hunt 2002). The product is authorised for use in poultry in the UK, but as it has maximum residue limits for meat and milk it can be used under the cascade provided minimum seven-day milk and 28-day meat withdrawal periods are observed. Treatments 2 and 3 were designed to assess the efficacy of the only injectable antibiotic specifically authorised for the treatment of digital dermatitis. Treatment 4 was designed to directly compare the efficacy of systemic erythromycin with that of topical erythromycin. The dose used was the same as that previously used in France to treat digital dermatitis (P. McGuire, personal communication). As erythrocin injection was not available in the UK, the French product was used. Twenty-one cows with 33 lesions were assigned to treatment 1, 20 cows (30 lesions) were assigned to each of treatments 2 and 3, and 20 cows (32 lesions) to treatment 4. The hindfeet of all 81 cows were examined using a modified Olympus borescope at the beginning of treatment (day 0) and then four, seven, 21 and 42 days later. For the examination, the borescope was placed behind each hindfoot in turn, focusing on the primary site of digital dermatitis (that is, between the bulbs of the heels). The borescope magnified and lit up the site, so that the skin of the foot could be examined without lifting the foot up. The size of the lesion was measured using a triangular plate attached to the borescope. All examinations were undertaken by the same trained technician. The lesions observed on these occasions were scored using the scheme outlined in Table 1 (Laven and Hunt 2002). All procedures were undertaken in accordance with the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986. The effect of treatment at each examination point was analysed using Kruskal-Wallis analysis of variance. When a cow had more than one lesion of digital dermatitis, both were included in the analysis. For each timepoint, the MannWhitney U test was used to identify homogenous subsets within the treatment groups. All statistical analyses were undertaken using Statistica software. Table 2 summarises the effect of treatment and time on lesion score, with significance set at P<0·05. There was a gradual reduction in lesion severity throughout the study; severity scores on day 42 were lower than the scores on day 0 for all treatment groups. As digital dermatitis is a cyclical disease that waxes and wanes (Dopfer and Willemen 1998), it is unclear whether this reduction in lesion score would have occurred without treatment. However, the positive control used in this study (the erythromycin footbath) has been shown to be an effective treatment for digital dermatitis on the study farm and on other farms (Laven and Proven 2000), thus the significant differences observed on day 21 and day 42 strongly suggest that the different treatments had different effects on digital dermatitis. Cattle treated with the five-day course of cefquinome had significantly lower lesion scores on day 21 and day 42 than cattle treated for three days with cefquinome and those given a single dose of erythromycin.
