What can we do for patients undergoing saphenous vein graft interventions?
In this issue of the Journal, Waksman et al. examine the characteristics and outcomes of 192 patients who presented with acute myocardial infarction (MI) and underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of a saphenous vein graft (SVG) at a large tertiary referral center with significant experience in treating such lesions [1]. Compared to 4,001 MI patients undergoing PCI of a native coronary artery during the same time period, MI patients undergoing PCI of a SVG were older, had more comorbidities and significantly higher 30-day and 1-year mortality, even after adjusting for comorbidities. The high risk of patients undergoing SVG interventions has been well documented in multiple studies [2]. As we cannot modify a patient’s age or his past medical history, how can interventionalists help improve these patients’ outcomes? First, by routinely using an embolic protection device (EPD) when intervening in SVGs. Although pivotal EPD trials in SVGs excluded MI patients [3], the EPD benefit may actually be greater in acute MI than in stable patients. However, EPDs remain underutilized: An EPD was used in only 34.6% of patients in the present series, although the low utilization rate was in part due to inclusion of patients treated before 2003, when the first EPD was approved for clinical use in the US. Contemporary EPD utilization is low, both in clinical trials [4] and in daily clinical practice, as reflected in NCDR (23–24%) [5,6]. A recent survey of interventionalists suggested that ‘‘anatomic difficulties’’ is the main reason for not using EPDs, followed by device complexity [7], but lack of reimbursement and limited familiarity with the devices are also important factors. Until novel and simpler EPDs and/or micromesh-covered stents [8,9] become available in the future, continuous training and education on the importance and technical aspects of EPD use can help. Moreover, using undersized stents [10] or intragraft vasodilators [11] may be useful. Interestingly, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors continue to be commonly used in SVG PCI, despite the lack of data that they provide benefit in this setting [12]. Second, by using drug-eluting stents. Although controversy remains on the relative merits and risks of drug-eluting stents in SVGs [13,14], the recently presented ISAR-CABG trial (presented at the American College of Cardiology Annual Scientific Sessions in New Orleans, Lousiana in April 2011) randomized 610 patients undergoing SVG PCI to bare metal versus drug-eluting stents and demonstrated significant reduction in the incidence of target lesion and target vessel revascularization with drug-eluting stents without differences in the incidence of death, MI, and stent thrombosis compared to bare metal stents. Third, by intervening on a native coronary artery instead of a SVG, if feasible, to improve both acute (lower risk of distal embolization) and long-term (as stented native coronary arteries have lower risk for instent restenosis than SVGs) results. Among prior coronary bypass patients, PCI of a bypass graft was independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality in NCDR [15]. Although PCI of native coronary arteries can be very challenging, especially in the presence of chronic total occlusions, such interventions can be successfully performed in experienced centers [16]. Fourth, by optimizing medical therapy and encouraging lifestyle modifications post PCI.
