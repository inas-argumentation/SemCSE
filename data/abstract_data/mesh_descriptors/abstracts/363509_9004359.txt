Is aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐platelet ratio index a reliable tool in human immunodeficiency virus patients in Africa?
To the Editor: Vinikoor et al. [1] recently reported that liver fibrosis was associated with increased mortality among human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected individuals in Zambia. The authors used previously published cut-offs of aspartate aminotransferase (AST)-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) to define significant liver fibrosis. However, APRI has never been validated in HIV-infected patients in Africa, and its use can lead to flawed conclusions. Aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index by definition increases when platelets decrease, and might be a useful indicator of liver fibrosis after exclusion of other causes of thrombocytopenia, in particular malaria and schistosomiasis in sub-Saharan Africa. In HIV patients, cytopenia is also a common manifestation of the HIV infection itself, and HIV-associated thrombocytopenia (HAT) has been reported in as many as 5–30% of HIV patients in Western countries [2]. The situation in Africa is less well described, but in our own cohort in rural Tanzania, 16.6% had thrombocytopenia at the time they initiated antiretroviral treatment (ART) [3]. Human immunodeficiency virus infects megakaryocytes and causes decreased platelet production. Furthermore, peripheral destruction is accelerated because of immune complexes and cross-reacting antibodies. HAT is more common in patients with advanced HIV; hence, it is not surprising that thrombocytopenia predicts mortality in various studies [3, 4]. Interestingly, ART reverses HAT, which supports the notion that HAT is a phenomenon associated with HIV disease progression [2]. In the study by Vinikoor et al., we suspect that the excess mortality in patients with elevated APRI score was caused mainly by advanced acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) and not liver disease. The Kaplan–Meier plots show that most deaths occurred within the first 3 months after starting ART. As liver fibrosis is not a rapidly progressive disease, this indicates that the patients died of AIDS rather than cirrhosis. Of note, a recent study found that APRI was a poor indicator of significant liver fibrosis in HIV/hepatitis C virus (HCV)-coinfected patients with CD4 counts <250 cells/mm3. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.64, which is only slightly better than chance [5]. In the study by Vinikoor et al., as in the majority of HIV treatment programs in Africa, most patients had advanced HIV at presentation, suggesting that APRI is not the right indicator of liver fibrosis in this setting. Clearly, there is a need for simple markers of liver fibrosis in HIV-infected individuals in resource-limited settings; however, such markers should be validated before they can be recommended for clinical use. An imprecise tool might create more problems than it solves.
