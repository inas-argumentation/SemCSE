EFFECT OF AGING ON CARDIAC AND ELECTROENCEPHALOGRAPHIC AROUSAL IN SLEEP APNEA/HYPOPNEA SYNDROME
As shown in Figure 1, based on the putative peripheral amplification of the pulse pressure, the aortic pressure was estimated from cuff brachial blood pressure, which was divided into forward (Afor) and backward reflection (Aref) components using the augmentation index. The putative systolic aortic blood pressure in the absence of wave reflections (SP*) was also obtained. The aortic augmentation index (AAI) was determined by the carotid augmentation index (CAI), using a regression equation between them (AAI = 0.91 * CAI + 27.6).’ Brachial systolic and pulse pressures increased significantly with age ( I = .24, P < .05; Y = .27, P < .05), whereas diastolic and mean pressures did not change. When agedependent peripheral amplification was taken into consideration, increases in estimated aortic systolic and pulse pressures were more marked (Table 1). As shown in Table 1, both Aref and Afar were positively correlated with age. However, the slope of the regression line for Aref was steeper than for Afor (.43 vs. .17, P < .01). SP* was not correlated with age. In addition, body height was inversely correlated with age. A significant correlation between body height and Aref ( I = .44, P < .00005) is consistent with earlier wave reflections in case of shorter length of the arterial system. Left ventricular mass index (LVMI; adjusting for body surface area) tended to increase with aging, and it was correlated significantly with estimated aortic systolic and pulse pressures. There was no association between diastolic or mean blood pressure and LVMI. LVMI showed a significant positive correlation with Arch but not with Afo, or SP*. These results suggest that the increase in the reflection component of the aortic pressure was a predominant determinant of age-associated systolic blood pressure increases, contributing to an age-associated increase in left ventricular mass. Reduced aortic distensibility may affect age-associated blood pressure changes indirectly by altering pulse wave velocity, which results in earlier wave reflections. The ageassociated earlier wave reflections are also attributable, in part, to the tendency toward shorter stature with aging. In conclusion, evaluation of aortic wave reflections can provide clinically important information concerning redundant left ventricular afterload even in normotensive elderly patients. Prospective studies will be required to ascertain the benefit of intervention to increased wave reflections associated with the normal aging process.
