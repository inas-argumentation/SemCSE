Resting Heart Rate and Blood Pressure as Indices of Cardiovascular and Mortality Risk: IS LOWER INVARIABLY BETTER?
HR, BP, Cardiovascular, and Mortality Risk 353 I general, life expectancy is inversely related to the resting heart rate (RHR). For example, a pet mouse with an RHR of 600 beats per minute (beats/min, bpm) generally lives 1 to 2 yr, whereas a whale with an RHR of 20 bpm lives, on average, 30 yr. Interestingly, a tiger or giraffe in captivity, which have RHRs similar to that of humans (ie, 60-90 bpm), have an average life span of about 15 to 20 yr. 1 The fact that humans live longer than predicted from their RHR is presumably related to their metabolism, membrane composition, and access to medical care. 2 This commentary summarizes recent studies that have examined the relation between RHR and blood pressure (BP) as indices of cardiovascular and mortality risk, with specifi c reference to patients with and without cardiovascular disease (CVD) as well as potential confounders (eg, aerobic capacity, coronary risk factors, infl ammatory markers), pathophysiologic mechanisms, and clinical implications.
