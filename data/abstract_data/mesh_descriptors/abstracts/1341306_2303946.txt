What links cod liver oil, spinach, and flow cytometry? Flow cytometric detection and quantitation of folic acid receptors in tumor cells
COMMENTARY Folic acid (water-soluble vitamin B9) found in brewer’s yeast, spinach, and cod liver oil is essential for de novo synthesis of nucleoside thymidine and thus essential for syntheses of nucleic acids and proteins and prevention of fetal malformations such as spina bifida. Antifolates such as methotrexate reversibly inhibit dihydrofolate reductase needed for folate synthesis and thus inhibit nucleic acid synthesis and proliferation. The a-folate receptor needed for folate transport has restricted expression in normal tissues with significant overexpression in several types of tumors. Thus, the differential expression of this receptor in normal versus malignant cells allows for treatment of tumors while sparing the normal tissues (1). Folic acid is essential for prevention of anemia in pregnancy, and its extraction from yeast and spinach provided a means for correcting this nutrient deficiency. Folic acid was synthesized in 1946, and the use of antifolates in therapy started with the Indian medicinal chemist, Yellapragada Subarao (http://ysubbarow.com). Having been denied a faculty position at the Harvard Medical School, Subarao moved to Lederle labs in Pearl River where along with his colleagues he made some major discoveries, such as the synthesis of antifolates (amethoptrin or methotrexate), the first tetracycline (Aureomycin), and several other important chemotherapeutic agents, in medicinal chemistry. Dr. Sidney Farber at the Children’s Hospital in Boston was instrumental in getting Dr. Subarao and his team to synthesize the antifoliates for clinical trials in leukemia patients (2). In 1953, methotrexate was approved by the FDA for cancer chemotherapy and continues to be one of our best and oldest chemotherapeutic antimetabolite for cancer treatment. Low-dose methotrexate is used to treat a wide variety of noncancerous diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, psoriasis, asthma, and inflammatory bowel disease. The anecdotal relation between antifolate therapy and flow cytometry goes a long way back. In the 1960s, Dr. Emil Frei and his colleagues at the Sidney Farber Cancer Institute in Boston were using selective rescue with folinic acid (citrovorum factor) for patients on high-dose methotrexate protocols. One of the important steps in this protocol called for monitoring of proliferation in bone marrow cells for which we were using tritiated thymidine autoradiography. However, it took more than a day to get these results to the clinicians, and Dr. Frei asked if a quicker method could be found to monitor bone marrow recovery in rescued patients. We were lucky to hit upon the propidium iodide/hypotonic citrate method for rapid monitoring of cell cycle analysis (3) and used it for monitoring of bone marrow and tumor cell proliferation by laser flow cytometry of patients on high-dose methotrexate/ rescue protocols (4,5). In recent issues of Cytometry, several excellent articles on the innovative use of flow and laser scanning cytometry for the study of cellular markers in tumor cells have been published. Megyeri et al. (6) developed a quantitative method for measuring and comparing ratio of two different enzymes involved in the activation of fluoropyrimidine antitumor drugs.
