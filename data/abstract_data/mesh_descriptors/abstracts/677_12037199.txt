Furthermore, the preferred orientation, which runs from vertical (0° angle) through horizontal (90° angle) back to the vertical (0° which is equivalent to 180°), progresses in an orderly way as one proceeds circumferentially around the pinwheel center. A schematic pinwheel, whose borders are indicated by the dotted square, embedded in a 4 ! 4 pinwheel array is represented in figure 1 a, with orientations of 0°, 22.5°, 45°, 67.5°, 90°, 112.5° and 135°, and 157.5° labeling the spokes of the pinwheel where the cells are tuned to those preferred orientations. The lines in this figure represent iso-orientation contours, with the heavy lines designating 0°, 45°, 90° and 135°. Pinwheels come in two distinct types, clockwise (+) and counterclockwise (–). The pinwheel outlined by the dotted square in figure 1 a is a clockwise type whose preferred orientation increases as you progress clockwise around the center. Counterclockwise pinwheels increase the angle of their preferred orientation as you go counterclockwise around the center. Pinwheels tend to tile the cortex in a particular way so that clockwise and counterclockwise pinwheels are, as seen in figure 1 a, surrounded by nearest neighbors with the opposite sign. At macroand microscales, one can argue that brains are the ‘same’. Does this same ‘sameness’ extend to the mesoscale structure of brains? In other words, can we find structural principles that hold at the level of neural circuit design? Recently published work by Kaschube et al. [2010] argues that, indeed, pinwheels in the visual system do share a single design, an observation that raises the possibility of common brain designs from the microthrough mesoto macroscales. What are pinwheels, and where are they found? Neurons in the primary visual cortex are tuned to specific locations in the visual world and also to the orientation of a line or edge at that location [Hubel and Wiesel, 2005]. Thus, many visual cortical neurons are capable of responding to light at a particular location, but only those cells whose preference matches the orientation of an edge or line at that point will generate spikes. The collection of neurons that look at a particular point but have individual cells tuned to all possible orientations are arranged in a structural motif called a ‘pinwheel’ [Blasdel and Salama, 1986; Bonhoeffer and Grinvald, 1991].
