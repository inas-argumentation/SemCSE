Heart valve lesions in patients treated with appetite suppressants.
The article by Ryan et al. (l), published in this issue, extends the knowledge and the debate on the adverse heart valve effects of the two related serotoninergic diet drugs, fenfluramine and dexfenfluramine. By way of background, let me revisit some history. Fenfluramine was approved in the United States in 1973 and also in Europe and was used widely, albeit generally short-term, with no published reports of heart valve abnormalities. Dexfenfluramine was also approved in Europe in 1985, 11 years before its approval in the US. in 1996, and again there were no published reports of heart valve complications despite an estimated 40 million prescriptions in Europe alone by 1992. In 1992, Weintraub et al. (2,3) published a study describing a very significant weight loss with a combination of fenfluramine and phentermine as compared to placebo. After this publication, this combination (popularly known as fedphen) began to be prescribed heavily in this country. An estimated 18 million prescriptions of phentermine and fenfluramine were written in 1996 alone (4). Dexfenfluramine was also approved by the FDA in April 1996 for long-term use in the USA and an additional estimated 2.4 million prescriptions were written in 1996 for this agent (4). Fifteen months later, in July 1997, the Mayo Clinic reported on the Internet that 24 women receiving fen-phen or dexfenfluramine alone, for an average duration of 12 months, had developed valvular lesions with regurgitation in the left heart, with 5 patients requiring valve replacement. The replaced valves were examined grossly and histologically and were found to have features reminiscent of the abnormal valves described in the carcinoid syndrome, which is characterized by high levels of circulating serotonin. The FDA issued a public health advisory on July 8, 1997, followed by letters to US. health care practitioners and institutions requesting information about any additional similar patients (5).
