Chemical castration and survival in low-risk prostate cancer.
Chemical castration is widely used in men with prostate cancer. In bone-metastasized disease, it was shown to clearly affect prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and progression-free survival. In locally advanced and lymph node– metastasized disease, chemical castration showed benefits in men who were also treated on the primary tumor with either surgery [1] or radiotherapy [2,3]. In men without such local treatment, the advantages of this therapy seem to be limited or nonexistent [4,5]. The morbidity of long-term chemical castration is becoming clear [6], and the timing of initiation of treatment is pivotal. The need for long follow-up in studies of androgenablative therapy for men with organ-confined prostate cancer underscores the paucity of data on the topic. Reliable surrogate end points for such a trial are not available, and follow-up of >15 yr is considered to be required, often exceeding life expectancy of older men in general. Hence, retrospective analyses, second best for sure, are called to the rescue. Wong et al [7] earlier published a retrospective analysis of the use of local treatment by surgery or radiotherapy in localized prostate cancer in men >65 yr of age and concluded that local treatment improved survival, even in men aged up to 80 yr. The elaborate statistical analyses to abolish confounding factors from the analysis did not prevent an editorial in JAMA by Litwin and Miller [8] with the title, ‘‘Survival (or Selection) of the Fittest.’’ A similar propensity analysis was applied in Wong et al’s publication on the use of early chemical castration in low-grade prostate cancer in this issue of European Urology [10]. The analysis makes solid conclusions from their findings rather difficult to draw and supports their careful conclusion that it still remains possible that the observed survival benefit of observation in this cohort is partly due to confounding factors not accounted for in the propensity analysis.
