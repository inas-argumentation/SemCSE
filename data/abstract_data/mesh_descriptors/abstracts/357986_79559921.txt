Biologic mesh for ventral hernia repair: a cautionary tale.
I n the current issue of Annals of Surgery, Rosen et al1 report their clinical experience using biologic mesh for single-staged repairs of contaminated complex ventral hernias. Their study specifically sought to provide long-term data regarding the clinical effectiveness of biologic prosthetic use in this challenging patient population. Importantly, the authors detail treatment of 128 patients at a single institution over a 6/2-year period, yielding a wound complication rate of 48%, a perioperative mortality rate of 2.3%, and a 3-year hernia recurrence rate of more than 50%. The authors conclude, “While biologic mesh in these situations is safe, the long-term durability appears less favorable than previously reported.”1 This is an important publication for many reasons. First, and foremost, it provides much needed information regarding the long-term performance of biologic mesh, an increasingly popular class of prosthetic material for repairing ventral hernias. Considering the frequency of this often-vexing clinical problem, surgeons and their patients are in desperate need of a safe and effective solution. The report presents the best long-term clinical efficacy data currently available and notably comes from clinicians with considerable expertise in abdominal wall reconstruction. Second, these data add to the growing evidence, much of it summarized in recent comprehensive reviews, indicating that biologic mesh use is associated with significant wound complications and a high incidence of hernia recurrence.2–4 These reviews consistently note that existing data on the use of biologic mesh to repair ventral hernias are of poor quality, dominated by low-level evidence, and insufficient to support the clinical effectiveness of these products. The studies within this topic area are almost exclusively retrospective case reports or uncontrolled case series that are generally riddled with inconsistent clinical definitions and variably robust outcome reporting. Then, there is the issue of cost. Although few reports focus on this aspect, biologic mesh is 10to 20-fold more expensive than alternative prosthetics.4,5 Thus, when it comes to the question of whether biologic prosthetics should be used to repair complex ventral hernias, it seems as though the jury “is in”; “no,” except under selected circumstances. The continued broad use of these intriguing biomaterials is unjustifiable, given their mediocre clinical performance and high costs. Unfortunately, there are too many variables and not enough information to govern the rational and appropriate application of this relatively new technology. We simply do not have adequate information regarding how the different types of mesh (human, porcine, equine), processing methods (cross-linking, sterilization), surgical techniques (underlay, retrorectus, onlay with or without component separation) and patient comorbidities interact and should therefore impact our clinical decision making. An industry-sponsored national registry of ventral hernia repair patients that included key clinical information, such as the type of prosthetic used, surgical technique, patient factors, and outcome, would be a major step toward improved patient care.3,4 Third, the report from Rosen et al prompts the question of why clinicians and patients are trapped within such a tangled vortex of mediocre clinical performance, high costs, and inadequate outcome data when biologic mesh was introduced almost 15 years ago. Why are we only now discovering the long-term performance capabilities of these biomaterials? The answer is likely complex and controversial. And, although a thorough exploration of the question is beyond the scope of this Editorial, the answer constitutes a cautionary tale involving the diffusion of innovations and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), medical device manufacturers and surgeons among other players. The adoption of new technologies in surgery begins with innovation but is regulated by the FDA through the agency’s review process. Every medical device must first be either cleared or approved by the FDA before it can be marketed in the United States. And, herein lies a critically important distinction between clearance versus approval. In brief, the federal review process for biologic mesh is principally focused on safety, not clinical efficacy.
