Estrogen Deprivation Therapy and Colon Cancer Risk in Breast Cancer Patients
To the Editor: Anti-estrogens have been proven to be effective in the treatment of early and advanced stage estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer in both pre and postmenopausal women. Tamoxifen has especially been used as an adjuvant therapy and prophylactically to reduce the incidence of breast cancer in women at high risk (1). However, among its side effects, the one of most concern is its apparent carcinogenic effect. Some studies, though equivocal, have demonstrated a possible association between tamoxifen and colon cancer risk. With the increasing use of newer agents like aromatase inhibitors, it is important to determine if such an association between aromatase inhibitors and colon cancer exists. We report two cases of accelerated colorectal cancer that developed while patients were taking aromatase inhibitors for adjuvant breast cancer therapy. The first case is a 57-year-old woman admitted to a hospital with a 3-day history of foul-smelling, purulent vaginal discharge. A gastrograffin enema demonstrated irregular narrowing at the rectosigmoid junction with fistularization to the vagina and a subsequent colonoscopy showed an apple-core, ulcerated malignant lesion in the rectum. She was diagnosed with widely metastatic rectal cancer and was placed on chemotherapy with 5-FU and radiotherapy for her primary rectal lesion. Five years prior, she had been diagnosed with bilateral breast cancer for which she underwent bilateral mastectomy. Both breasts revealed infiltrating ductal carcinoma with involvement of one of 16 lymph nodes on the right. She received cyclophosphamide, methotrexate and fluorouracil as adjuvant chemotherapy after which she took tamoxifen for 2 years followed by exemestane, which she took till her diagnosis of rectal cancer. The second case is a 62-year-old woman, with no family history of colon cancer, found to have several ulcerated lesions in her colon during a screening colonoscopy at a local hospital. A biopsy further revealed moderately differentiated, invasive adenocarcinoma for which she underwent a segmental resection of her sigmoid colon and rectum. Three years earlier, she was diagnosed with breast cancer after discovering a mass in her left breast. A lumpectomy was subsequently done which revealed infiltrating ductal carcinoma grade III with associated ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and positive lymphnode involvement. The tumor was ER ⁄ PR positive and HER-2 ⁄ neu negative. She received adjuvant chemotherapy with Adriamycin ⁄ Cytoxan, followed by radiation, and Arimidex was prescribed. She was treated with Arimidex for a few months, but due to joint pains, she switched to tamoxifen, which she took for about a year before switching to Letrozole. She continued on Letrozole until her diagnosis of rectal cancer one year later. A year prior to her diagnosis she had a negative fecal occult blood test, which was done as part of a routine health screening. Aromatase inhibitors have proven to be more effective than tamoxifen as adjuvant treatment for breast cancer in post-menopausal women. Several studies, such as the Arimidex-Nolvadex trial 95 (ARNO 95), the Austrian Breast and Colorectal Cancer Study Group (ABCSG) trial 8 and the Arimidex, Tamoxifen, Alone or in Combination (ATAC) trial, have demonstrated a significant reduction in treatment-related adverse events and a significant increase in disease-free survival when compared with tamoxifen (2,3). As a result, aromatase inhibitors are fast gaining acceptance over tamoxifen in the adjuvant setting (4). Reports of serious adverse events, especially carcinogenic events, are therefore crucial in determining the long-term safety of the drug.
