Regenerative versus destructive cell death in developing systems and tissue homeostasis (retrospective on DOI 10.1002/bies.201200018)
At the beginning of the last century, biologists identified the presence of abundant dead cells in tissue sections of embryos. The connection between the pattern of such death processes and morphological changes occurring in the course of development was soon made. The loss of the tail of tadpoles during metamorphosis, and the separation of the digits from a hand/foot plate in tetrapods were soon selected as paradigms of the phenomenon termed “physiological” or “programmed” cell death. A morphogenetic role for this regressive process was inferred from the absence or scarcity of cell death in species with morphological features not requiring tissue regression, and by observing that increased patterns of cell death result in abnormal morphologies. The discovery that tumor tissues also exhibit a striking amount of cell death, along with the identification of evolutionarily conserved death gene cascades, were major stimuli for researchers to unravel the molecular mechanisms responsible for cell death. The family of proteases termed caspases was identified as central components of the predominant type of cell death in the embryo, which was named “apoptosis” to distinguish it from passive cell death involving disruption of cell membranes, termed “necrosis”.
