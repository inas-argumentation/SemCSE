Molecular medicine and cancer.
This themed issue on molecular medicine and cancer explores the impact of chemistry on recent developments in cancer therapy and the prospects for future step-changes, in particular new approaches to targeted/personalized therapies, chemoprevention and the emergence of drug discovery in academia. Clinical success stories are highlighted as well as selected examples of novel compounds in development. The most widely used anticancer drugs are platinum compounds, but what advantages can new generations of metal-based anticancer drugs provide? The tutorial review by Farrell (DOI: 10. 1039/C5CS00201J) describes a new generation of substitution-inert polynuclearplatinum complexes which are as potent as cisplatin, but have different target sites. These poly-cationic complexes are accumulated in cells via heparan sulfate proteoglycan binding, and then bind strongly in the minor groove of DNA causing efficient nuclear condensation. Their activity is independent of p53. Gold, in the form of Au(I) complexes, has been used for treating rheumatoid arthritis for about 90 years (chrysotherapy). Che et al. (DOI: 10.1039/C5CS00132C) review the potent anticancer activities of Au(III) compounds which have clinical potential and a novel mechanism of action. Jaouen et al. (DOI: 10.1039/C5CS00486A) review recent developments in the design of ferrocene derivatives of tamoxifen. The coupling of the redox activity of ferrocene to such an estrogen receptor-targeted anticancer drug introduces new mechanisms of action involving new signaling pathways which induce senescence or apoptosis. They point out that despite current interest in biologicals, in 2013 and 2014 over 80% of new medicines approved for sale by the FDA were small molecules. The aim of chemotherapy is not only to kill the primary tumour cells, but also to prevent their spread (metastasis). Sava et al. (DOI: 10.1039/C5CS00134J) review the Ru(III) drug NAMI-A which can inhibit tumour invasion and migration and has been on clinical trial, and emphasise the new opportunities offered by metal complexes for the treatment of tumour metastasis. Chemoprevention could be even more effective than chemotherapy, but has yet to be widely accepted. It could play an important role in combating the high cost of introducing new chemotherapies and their limited success in terms of survival rates. Wallace and Penny (DOI: 10.1039/C5CS00705D) highlight the challenges for medicinal chemistry in terms of designing effective chemopreventative agents.
