Cholestatic hepatocellular injury: what do we know and how should we proceed.
Tissue injury and inflammation, repair, and fibrosis are fundamental components of acute and chronic cholestatic liver diseases, regardless of cause. Hepatocellular injury and death, with the accompanying inflammatory response, provoke symptoms of fatigue and weakness, and account, in part, for the characteristic cholestatic biochemical dysfunction that is characteristic of these liver diseases. The ensuing responses to cell injury can culminate in liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and chronic liver failure. Indeed, in the United States the cholestatic liver diseases primary sclerosing cholangitis and primary biliary cirrhosis account for 10% of all patients undergoing liver transplantation in 2003 (http://www.ustransplant.org/ srtr.php). Optimal therapy would address the mechanisms causing cholestasis and reverse the process. Unfortunately, such therapies do not currently exist, although agonists for the nuclear receptor FXR hold such promise [1,2]. Until such specific therapies exist, alternative therapeutic strategies would include approaches to reduce hepatocellular injury. Clarifying the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying hepatocellular cytotoxicity in cholestasis would be helpful for designing and developing effective treatments for patients with cholestatic liver diseases. In this context, there is considerable experimental interest in the cellular mechanisms underlying hepatocellular injury during cholestasis. Common bile duct ligation (CBDL) with or without cholycystectomy in the mouse, or CBDL alone in the rat (which has no gall bladder) are popular models of cholestatic liver injury.
