Apoptosis in heart failure: is there light at the end of the tunnel (TUNEL)?
When the concept of apoptosis was initially introduced in the 1970s, there was very little excitement at the time for what was considered to be an interesting but insignificant mode of cell death (1). However, over the last decade, there has been an explosion of knowledge and an equally impressive number of reports that implicate apoptosis in a wide variety of physiological and pathological processes. There also has been accumulating chemical evidence suggesting that apoptosis may play an important role in heart failure. For example, there have been over 150 articles published just in the last 3 years that associate apoptosis with heart failure. These data range from ischemic heart diseases to dilated cardiomyopathy in animal and human models of heart failure (Table I). Therefore, apoptosis must be very important in heart failure. . , or is it? In this issue of Journal of Cardiac Failure, Tamura et al report the other side of the argument (2). These investigators examined the presence of apoptosis in an animal model of heart failure by using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated 2'-deoxyuridine 5'triphosphate (dUTP) nick end-labeling (commonly known as TUNEL staining), which is currently the most widely used method to detect individual cells undergoing apoptosis (3). They compared the rate of TUNEL-positive cells in the hearts of control rats, nonfailing spontaneously hypertensive heart failure (SHHF) rats, and failing SHHF rats. They found an extremely low level of TUNEL-positive cells (1 to 2 TUNEL-positive cells/106 myocyte nuclei) in the hearts of all 3 groups of rats.
