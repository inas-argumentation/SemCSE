It includes periductal stromal sarcoma and periductal stromal hyperplasia (PDSS and PDSH); both are characterized by a nodular and periductal stromal proliferation around open tubules and ⁄ or ducts devoid of a phyllodes pattern. Burga and Tavassoli state that in PDSH the stromal cells show no or minimal atypia, at most 0–2 mitotic figures per 10 HPF and the margins are usually non-infiltrative. The features of PDSS include variable stromal cellularity and atypia, stromal mitotic activity of three or more mitotic figures per 10 HPF and infiltrating margins. Burga and Tavassoli described 20 cases of PDSS, one of which metastasized and one of which recurred, and seven cases of PDSH, one of which also recurred. These lesions can also show intraepithelial proliferation similar to this case in which there was moderate, focal epithelial hyperplasia. It has been suggested that the difference between PDSS and frondless phyllodes tumours is unclear and others consider that periductal stromal tumours occupy a position intermediate between phyllodes tumours and stromal sarcomas. Although fronds may not be present in a phyllodes tumour, stromal heterogeneity and irregular dilated glands are characteristic and differ from the apparent homogeneous stromal proliferation and pericanalicular-type, non-distorted ducts of PDSS and PDSH. An additional differentiating feature, as illustrated by this case, apparently includes the lack of pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia within the lesional PDSH. In contrast, PASH-like change may be seen in more collagenous areas of phyllodes tumours and PASH has been reported in fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours arising both in the breast and in so-called anogenital mammary type glands. However, if PASH is a prominent feature in the adjacent breast tissue as in this case, its sampling on core biopsy could lead to an erroneous diagnosis and, with needle aspiration, to a diagnosis of fibroadenoma.
