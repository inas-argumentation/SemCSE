A piece of my mind. The things that matter.
ABOUT A YEAR AGO I SAT IN ON A GRADUATE COURSE ON Modern British Texts that featured three authors— Samuel Beckett, Virginia Woolf, and W. H. Auden— given by Edward Mendelson, the Lionel Trilling Professor of English at Columbia University. It was a wonderful experience for me to go back to my alma mater almost seven decades later, to revisit “humanities” that had so blown my mind then. Of course, this time I was a curiosity in the classroom, with students in their 20s and 30s, but I felt welcome, and could hold my own. I got to know Professor Mendelson well enough to tell him about myself as a geriatrician, interested in aging, and he thought it fit to give me a copy of a book he wrote a few years earlier, called The Things That Matter, reviewing “what seven classic novels have to say about the stages of life,” spanning “birth through parenthood,” and concluding with “an unknowable future.” This seemed to me a meaningful context in which to present a piece of my mind. Shortly thereafter, my own future became more knowable when I gradually took up the care of my spouse for symptoms consistent with a “geriatric syndrome,” about which I have written. This stage reflects a time of life not dreamed of in Professor Mendelson’s authors, beginning with Mary Shelley (Birth: Frankenstein) and concluding with Virginia Woolf (The Future: Between The Acts), because the events I recount occurred as a consequence of our advanced chronological ages, unimagined then, and have a distinct but not unique medical tone. In older persons, geriatric syndromes represent “transitions” from functional changes of normal aging to manifestations that evolve over time into a number of age-related chronic conditions. Older persons rarely identify the early physical expression of these transitions, are not likely to consult a health care professional for them, and if they do, there would be little remarkable for the clinician to note, at least early on. Following a rather sustained time of anguish related to family events, I became aware that my spouse was manifesting a geriatric syndrome that geriatricians designate as “frailty.” This condition is perhaps currently in the forefront of aging-related clinical and research studies, and while health professionals inherently recognize frailty in their elderly patients, it remains difficult to define as a discrete clinical entity and has no laboratory biomarker. I have had extensive literary experience with frailty, and I was aware and concerned that this condition usually progresses in time to a state of dependency. The most notable early sign in my spouse was weariness: I also use the word “languor,” for it seems to me to capture the emerging state better than “fatigue.” There followed an unsteady gait and weakness in motor functions previously carried out well; a profoundly diminished appetite, notable uniquely for lack of desire for desserts, especially chocolates, the consumption of which had always provoked mirth and anticipation; weight loss of 6 pounds; and then—of greatest concern because its intrusion could be a new entity in its own right not necessarily linked to frailty—impairment of memory affecting recent recall. A series of consultations with health professionals in geriatric medicine, geriatric psychiatry, and neurology were necessary, independently and collaboratively, to provide decisions and directions for care that would not have been appropriate or possible for me. Tests were ordered and medications prescribed. But the personal perspective this essay describes is my taking up in the home the ongoing needs of the one dear to me, providing care based on my own medical background, and experiencing my own transformative personal insights. My multifaceted roles involving essentially full-time, personal caregiving are not likely to be anticipated by most physicians when they think about their future. Certainly this is a new and unique experience for me. My late career in geriatric medicine helped me in surveillance of round-theclock health needs. I make decisions relating to safety and well-being in the home and have become newly attentive during social occasions. I try persuasion and explanation to encourage completion of tasks, seek to curb my customary impatience, and to slow my hurried pace. For all “the things that matter,” I need to be at home much more and to schedule my few remaining outside professional activities to times when someone trusted is at home for company. My days are rather uneventful, more focused on routine events, and yet somehow this adjustment is satisfying because it is less driven. As a geriatrician encountering a new patient, I reviewed the medications prescribed for my spouse, and now observe the accuracy with which they are taken day and night, and their side effects.
