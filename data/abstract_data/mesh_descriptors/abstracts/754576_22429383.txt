Molecular aspects of Helicobacter pylori cag‐pathogenicity island
Type IV secretion systems (T4SS) are supramolecular structures found in many prokaryotic species. They are used by bacteria to exchange materials with the external world (e.g. for the export or import of DNA, or for the translocation of proteins or protein ⁄DNA complexes across the cell envelope). In the case of Gram-negative bacteria, these protein complexes span the inner and outer membranes and form a needle-like structure that protrudes outside the cell. Studies of some prototypical T4SS, particularly that of Agrobacterium tumefaciens, have provided a considerable amount of information about the function and organization of the machinery, although most of the molecular aspects of the organization of T4SS, including the specific functions of components and the translocation mechanism, remain largely hypothetical or missing. At least three different types of T4SS have been identified and grouped according to their functions: (a) translocation of DNA substrates by a contact-dependent process; (b) delivery of proteins to eukaryotic target cells; and (c) DNA release or uptake from the extracellular milieu. Helicobacter pylori, the Gram-negative pathogen that affects the stomach of approximately 50% of the world’s human population, presents all three types of T4SS system, although one of them is unique: type I strains of the bacterium have a special T4SS called cytotoxin-associated gene-pathogenicity island (cagPAI). At least two different roles have been assigned to the cagPAI: it is used by the pathogen to inject a toxin into the host cell, and it is also able to induce the production of interleukin-8 in humans. These two effects are apparently the result of two independent mechanisms. The organization of the cagPAI (a genomic insert of approximately 30 genes) is more complex than that of other T4SS: one of the genes encodes an effector protein, whereas others encode proteins that have been recognized, based on amino acid sequence similarity and other experimental evidence, to correspond to the components of a T4SS pilus. The remaining genes encode proteins that are accessory to the secretion process and whose function is largely unknown or hypothetical, although they may play a role in the process of secretion of the effector and ⁄or in the induction of interleukin-8 production. The fact that the cagPAI is present only in the pathogenic strains of the bacterium, and that its presence is generally used to define the pathogenicity of the strain, illustrates the relevance of this insert. In this minireview series, the most recent findings on the function, organization and structure of the H. pylori cagPAI secretion system are considered. In the first minireview, Tegtmeyer, Wessler and Backert introduce and discuss the role of cagPAI T4SS in pathogenesis. The effects of the injection of CagA toxin on the host cell are analyzed using animal infection models, transgenic mice and in vitromodels. In the second minireview, Fischer describes the assembly of the secretion apparatus core complex and the mechanism of CagA toxin recognition and transfer. The last two reviews are more structurally-oriented: one, by Terradot and Waksman, illustrates the 3D structure at different resolutions of some of the components of the T4SS, along with our present model of the overall architecture of the secretion system core complex; the other, by Cendron and Zanotti, describes the current functional and structural knowledge about the other gene products of cagPAI (i.e. those proteins that are not structural components of the secretion complex but are essential or accessory components of it). We hope that these reviews offer a way into further research on the cagPAI because much remains to be done before we arrive at a complete understanding at the molecular level of the functioning of this complex apparatus.
