Media Research Contributes to the Battle Against Childhood Obesity
Concern about childhood obesity has mushroomed in recent years from an esoteric focus to page-one news headlines that identify the issue as one of the most serious threats to the nation’s public health. Over the past several decades, the proportion of all children classified as overweight has more than doubled. In some age groups, such as 6–11-year-olds, obesity rates have tripled since 1970. Multiple factors contribute to childhood obesity, including reduced levels of physical activity, easy availability of fast foods, and changing family dynamics that often leave young people responsible for preparing their own meals. In 2006, the Institute of Medicine (IOM) of the National Academies of Science identified one other significant contributor to the obesity epidemic: pervasive media advertising promoting nutritionally poor food products to children (IOM, 2006). The IOM’s conclusion about this issue was derived from a broad range of evidence accumulated over time documenting the nature and extent of food products marketed to children; children’s exposure to such advertising; and the impact of the marketing campaigns on young people’s food preferences, purchase requests, and diet. Most of the relevant studies came from the fields of communication and public health, the nexus that is the focus of this journal. One of the most immediate responses to the IOM’s report was an industry-wide commitment from food marketers to significantly improve the nutritional quality of any food products advertised to children (Council of Better Business Bureaus, 2007).
