A cost-effective analysis of postoperative goal-directed therapy for high-risk surgical patients: improved outcomes are not enough … you have to "show me the money".
www.ccmjournal.org 1311 The late Bennett (February 21, 2012) (1), one of the international founding fathers of critical care medicine and original investigators of postoperative goaldirected therapy (PGDT), would be proud of this publication (2, 3). Bennett and others have conclusively shown that PGDT clinically is the right thing to do for patients over decades. In this issue of Critical Care Medicine, Ebm et al (4) in this well-written, very detailed, and methodological approach have shown that the clinically effective PGDT is now the right thing to do because it is cost effective across a wide range of assumptions. However, Bennett, a gentle genius of a clinical scientist, would question why an intervention that decreases complications and length of stay and improves outcomes for over 2 decades would need this contemporary and complex (to many clinicians) validation (5–7). Furthermore, many of these high-risk patients undergo elective surgery with consent. They will be exposed to the complications and death nonetheless. So why not minimize the risk with the best evidence-based therapy possible? Is the next step in the generalization of an effective medical intervention “show me the money”? In a popular movie, Tom Cruise (Jerry Maguire) played the role of a professional sports agent and his very accomplished client, a professional football player (Cuba Gooding Jr), trademarked the term “show me the money.” The movie was essentially a cost-effective analysis of a well-accomplished athlete’s value to the professional football team. From the professional football player’s viewpoint with a history of success, it was simply “show me the money” which was a multiyear multimillion dollar contract. However, the team wanted additional data to assure its investment. This analysis is quite similar to PGDT. In the case of PGDT, an economic analysis or to “show me the money” is more complex. It has now given way to a “probabilistic simulation model that enables an estimation of costs and effects of the immediate care as well as the financial and clinical impact over a patient’s remaining lifetime whenever clinical data are incomplete and effectiveness of an intervention is uncertain” (8). Thus, proving cost-effectiveness can be more complex than clinical effectiveness. Although the results of this study are generalizable, there are other considerations when applying to other healthcare systems. For example, in the United States, the Affordable Care Act, valuebased purchasing, relative-value units, public disclosure of costs of procedures, 24-hour intensivists, telemedicine, and Leapfrog volume standards must be factored into this model. These developments have changed the economic landscape of surgical practice in the United States (9). In the last 2 decades, less invasive surgical interventions have revolutionized the care of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms (10), cardiac valve surgery, oncologic, orthopedic, and many other indications. These developments have extended these surgical procedures to be more commonplace in octogenarians and previously contraindicated risk groups. Surgical cases that took up to 8 or more hours under general anesthesia are now being done in one-quarter the time even under local anesthesia. Thus, hospital stays that were 16 days in duration as used in this study are now 2 to 3 days. This diminishes hospital costs, which is a significant driver in this cost-effectiveness analysis. These improved efficiencies are frequently followed by lower third-party reimbursement rates that can be negated with increased volumes to decrease operational costs. This minimizes the ability to offset the investment costs, which is a significant assumption in this cost analysis. Furthermore, the longitudinal follow-up period for an outcome analysis for these procedures can be up to a decade (11). Thus, what is the overall economic impact when all these considerations are factored into PGDT? In the future, more noninvasive monitoring techniques such as ultrasound will allow for earlier screening and hemodynamic optimization of high-risk patients.
