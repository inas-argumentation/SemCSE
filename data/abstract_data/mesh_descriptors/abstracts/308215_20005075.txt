Toward a multidisciplinary approach to the treatment of obstructive sleep apnea in the obese child
Costa and Mitchell concluded that adenotonsillectomy cured less than half of obese children with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Although the authors recommend studies on the efficacy of additional treatments, no implications for daily practice were given. I would therefore like to suggest some practical conclusions. Obese children have a high prevalence of OSA, which also influences several obesity-related comorbidities. Optimal treatment should therefore target OSA and obesity simultaneously. Adenotonsillectomy is not capable of doing this for the majority of cases because of its low efficacy and possible postoperative weight gain. Consequently, adenotonsillectomy can no longer be considered as a routine first-line treatment of obese children. However, this statement does not imply that adenotonsillectomy should be completely abandoned. Further studies, either by clinical assessment or advanced techniques including imaging, are needed to identify children most likely to benefit from adenotonsillectomy. Meanwhile, patients selected for adenotonsillectomy will need to be incorporated into a weight management program to prevent postoperative weight gain. This endeavor requires a multidisciplinary collaboration between sleep physicians and members of a pediatric obesity clinic. Additionally, a follow-up sleep study is mandatory to document the efficacy of adenotonsillectomy. Recent studies showed that weight loss, both spontaneous as well as by bariatric surgery, has a high success rate in relieving OSA. Furthermore, OSA itself could positively influence the success of a weight management program. Although no data exist on the effects of weight loss in young obese children with OSA and on its long-term effects and compliance, I suggest considering weight loss as a first-line treatment.
