Differences in 25‐hydroxyvitamin D concentrations may explain most of the black‐white breast cancer disparities noted in young women
The recent article by Ademuyiwa et al presented analyses of the black-white disparities in breast cancer survival for young women. On a multivariate model, black women were found to have a hazard ratio (HR) for overall survival of 1.59 (95% confidence interval [95% CI], 1.55-1.64) compared with white women, and an HR of 1.56 (95% CI, 1.52-1.62) for disease-specific survival. Those with inflammatory histology had the highest HR (1.56; 95% CI, 1.47-1.67). The authors did not offer an explanation for the black-white disparities. Herein, I outline the evidence that differences in 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) concentrations may explain the majority of the observed disparities. The evidence that solar ultraviolet light B (UVB) exposure and vitamin D play important roles in breast cancer incidence and mortality is quite strong. Ecological studies based on geographical variations in breast cancer incidence and/or mortality rates have consistently found inverse correlations with respect to indices of solar UVB doses. An ecological study for breast cancer mortality rates for white American women for the period 1970 through 1994 found that a model with UVB (b 5 20.71), urban residence (b 5 0.31), and alcohol consumption (b 5 0.26) explained 84% of the variance. A similar study for black women found that a model with lung cancer, an index for smoking and diet (b50.55), UVB (b 5 20.38), and urban residence (b50.37) (P 5 0.03) explained 64% of the variance. A review of many such ecological studies along with mechanisms that explain the role of vitamin D in reducing the risk of cancer is presented in the study by Moukayed and Grant. The role of vitamin D in reducing inflammation related to cancer risk is discussed in the study by Krishnan and Feldman. For the period 2001 through 2004 in the United States, white females aged 20 to 59 years had mean 25(OH)D concentrations of approximately 27 ng/mL, whereas black females aged 20 to 59 years had mean 25(OH)D concentrations of approximately 14 ng/mL. For these values, the ratio of breast cancer incidence rates for black women versus white women, based on casecontrol studies, is approximately 1.7. A study regarding breast cancer survival versus 25(OH)D concentrations over a 9-year period in Norway found an HR for breast cancer-specific mortality of 2.32 (95% CI, 1.20-4.55) for women with 25(OH)D concentrations <18 ng/mL compared with women with 25(OH)D concentrations of 25 to 32 ng/mL, which is in agreement with the findings of Ademuyiwa et al. In a recent article, it was noted that there are black-white disparities in all-cause and cancer-specific mortality rates for 13 types of cancer after consideration of socioeconomic status, stage of disease at diagnosis, and treatment and that differences in mean 25(OH)D concentrations could explain many of these disparities. Most if not all of these 13 types of cancer are considered to be vitamin D sensitive. Thus, there is reasonable evidence that increasing 25(OH)D concentrations among both black and white Americans to >30 to 40 ng/mL would both decrease cancer incidence and mortality rates as well as reduce the black-white cancer disparities.
