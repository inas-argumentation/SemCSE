Erectile dysfunction in diabetes: ‘NO’ role for the endothelium?
In the current issue of the Journal, Browne et al. have investigated the role of generalised endothelial dysfunction in the pathophysiology of diabetic erectile dysfunction (1). In a comparative study of 11 impotent and 11 potent men with type 2 diabetes, they found no difference in forearm blood flow responses to acetylcholine [an endotheliumand nitric oxide (NO)-dependent vasodilator] or to nitroprusside (an NO donor and hence endothelium-independent vasodilator). Does this mean that endothelial dysfunction, a well-recognised accompaniment of type 2 diabetes (2–5), has nothing to do with erectile dysfunction in this condition? Erectile dysfunction is approximately three times more common in diabetic when compared with non-diabetic men, and diabetic men develop erectile dysfunction approximately 5–10 years earlier than agematched non-diabetic subjects (6). There is increasing evidence that erectile dysfunction may be a reliable marker of coronary atherothrombosis (7), that it is more prevalent among patients with coronary artery disease than in the general population (8), that a significant proportion of patients with angiographically proven coronary disease have erectile dysfunction and that erectile dysfunction may become evident prior to angina symptoms in almost 70% of patients (9). Moreover, the degree of erectile dysfunction may relate to the degree of coronary atherosclerosis present (10). Given the close association between endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerotic disease, it therefore seems logical that erectile dysfunction might be an indicator of endothelial dysfunction, particularly because NO is closely involved in the relaxation of corpus cavernosum smooth muscle that is necessary for penile erection. Indeed, human and animal studies have demonstrated that endothelial dysfunction plays a major role in erectile dysfunction (11–13). In the light of this, the findings of Browne et al. might seem surprising. On the other hand, given that diabetics exhibit marked generalised endothelial dysfunction even in the absence of other coexisting pathologies, it may be more difficult to demonstrate a further degree of endothelial impairment in such patients when compared with non-diabetics. Moreover, sources of NO other than the endothelium are physiologically important in penile erection, and in particular neuronally derived NO; and indeed, autonomic neuropathy plays a major role in the development of diabetic erectile dysfunction and appears to be an independent cardiovascular risk factor (14). It would be interesting and instructive to know whether the impotent subjects in the study of Browne et al. had any evidence of autonomic dysfunction over and above the potent subjects. Collectively, the evidence points to an important role for NO derived both from the endothelium and from nitrergic nerves in the initiation and maintenance of erection; and that dysfunction of either, or both, may be important in the causation of erectile dysfunction. This applies equally to diabetics as to non-diabetic patients with other cardiovascular risk factors or those with established atherosclerotic disease. In recent years, the use of phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors has revolutionised the treatment of erectile dysfunction. These drugs are effective in diabetics, although less so than in non-diabetics (15). Their efficacy is related to increasing cavernosal levels of cyclic guanosine-3¢, 5¢-monophosphate (cyclic GMP), 1 which is the effector signalling molecule for NO. It seems logical that preventing or treating erectile dysfunction in diabetic patients should address optimisation of both endothelial and neuronal function, in order to maximise available NO in the corpus cavernosum. Other therapies which improve endothelial function include statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers; furthermore, good blood pressure control is key to maintenance of good endothelial function, as is regular aerobic exercise and weight loss in obese patients. The intervention likely to have the greatest impact in preventing autonomic neuropathy is good glycaemic control. All of these therapies have proven macroand microvascular benefits in diabetes; their specific effectiveness in preventing or treating erectile dysfunction, however, remains unclear. What is clear is that erectile dysfunction is closely linked to the presence of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or to risk factors for such disease, including diabetes; and that the degree of erectile dysfunction, as assessed by validated questionnaires, may reflect atherosclerotic burden. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm the usefulness or otherwise of erectile dysfunction as a marker of latent atherosclerosis over and above traditional cardiovascular risk markers. It is conceivable that the presence and severity of erectile dysfunction could even be used to monitor response to treatment of cardiovascular disease, but at present this remains highly speculative.
