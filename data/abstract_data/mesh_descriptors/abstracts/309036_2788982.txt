Learning from history: understanding the carcinogenic risks of nanotechnology.
S maller than the average microscope can see, nanomaterials have been used in commercial products to improve industrial design and marketability for the past several years. Titanium oxide nanoparticles in sunscreen block ultraviolet rays yet allow for smooth application of the lotion and absorption into the skin. Carbon nanotubes in bicycle frames and tennis rackets make them stronger and lighter. Researchers are also using nanoparticles to direct cancer drugs to tumor tissue both in the lab and the clinic. But with these advances, some researchers have noted that the public and environmental health effects of manufacturing these materials are still unclear. “This is not an issue to cause alarm,” said Agnes Kane, M.D., Ph.D., chair of the department of pathology and laboratory medicine at Brown University in Pro vidence, R.I., who studies the health effects of environmental and occupational exposure to asbestos. “But there is a lot of uncertainty.” Researchers are now investigating not only whether nanomaterials are carcinogenic themselves but also whether their manufacturing can create harmful, possibly cancer-causing, by-products. “We still need to develop a set of questions to determine if [nanomaterials] are safe,” said Andrew Maynard, Ph.D., chief science advisor for the Project on Emerging Nanotechnologies at the Woodrow International Center for Scholars in Washington, D.C. “However, we do know a little more in terms of carbon nanotubes because of their analogy with asbestos.” Previous studies have shown that inhaled mineral dusts such as quartz and asbestos fi bers can lead to lung damage and cancer. Some scientists wonder whether nanoparticles, which are by defi nition 100 nm or less in at least one dimension, could penetrate even deeper than asbestos — which, when inhaled, causes infl ammation and free radical damage — into the cell nucleus and affect cellular DNA directly. A study published in Nature Nan otechnology in July 2008 by Maynard and Ken Donaldson, Ph.D., of the University of Edinburgh, examined whether carbon nanotubes may act like asbestos. Carbon nanotubes, like asbestos fi bers, are long, thin, and needle shaped. The researchers injected both long ( ≥ 10 – 20 μ m) and short carbon nanotubes ( ≤ 5 μ m), as well as asbestos as a control, into the abdominal lining of mice. Like asbestos, the long nanotubes caused scarring and infl ammation of the lining, which scientists consider a precursor to mesothelioma, a cancer of the pleura and peritoneum of the lung. The short tubes had no effect. A research group at the National Institute of Health Sciences in Japan reported in the Journal of Toxicological Sciences in February that carbon nanotubes injected into mice led to actual tumors. Certain metal particles can lead to reactive oxygen radicals that cause oxidative stress and DNA damage in the body. For example, the nanomaterial known as quantum dots used to make computer or television screens brighter also contains cadmium, which is highly carcinogenic. The effects of quantum dots on the environment and public health are still unclear, but many electronics end up in the landfi lls and, Kane said, cadmium can accumulate up the food chain. “Once it’s in the environment, it could come back at us,” said Kane. The world production of carbon nanotubes is expected to increase by $1 billion – $2 billion by 2014, according to Donaldson, so scientists, such as Chris Reddy, Ph.D., at the Woods Hole Institute of Oceanography in Woods Hole, Mass., are investigating the possible carcinogenic effects of carbon nanotube manufacturing on the environment before these materials become more widely used. “We have taken on being a proactive guide to make sure there won’t be a problem down the road,” Reddy said. For example, nanotube manufacturing may have carcinogenic by-products. Heating carbon at high temperatures with metal catalysts is a common way to make carbon nanotubes. These metals, such as known carcinogens iron, cobalt, and nickel, can contaminate the fi nished tubes. A study reported in Nanotechnology in April 2008 by Reddy and colleagues analyzed nanotubes from different manufacturers. Some contained up to 30% metal impurities and the different nanotubes varied in composition, demonstrating that there is not one type of carbon nanotube. This fi nding could complicate toxicological studies on nanotubes. “A toxicologist can’t buy a standard nanotube,” said Reddy.
