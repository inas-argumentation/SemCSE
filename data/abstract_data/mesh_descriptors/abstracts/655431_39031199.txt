AN EXAMPLE OF NON-INHERITED RADIATION RESISTANCE
The determination of radiation response in microorganisms is complicated by oxygen effects, the presence of protective chemicals, post-irradiation temperature effects, the composition of the recovery medium, and-with ultraviolet lightphotoreactivation and catalase reactivation (Zelle and Hollaender, 1955). In addition to these it soon becomes evident to the experimenter in this field that the physiological state of the organism has a profound effect on the result obtained; this can be studied by measuring the effectiveness of radiations on organisms grown in media modified as to pH, and carbon-nitrogen ratio, by studying effects at different phases in the growth curve (Stapleton, 1955), or more decisively by use of synchronous cultures to detect variations in radiation response within each division cycle. This type of physiological adaptation which is quite unrelated to the radiation resistance of evolutionary origin has received little attention from bacteriologists in recent years and with the exception of induced enzymes the concern has been primarily with the variation of characteristics of a culture that can be explained by the selection of mutants. We have studied a number of these "adaptations" and we are reporting a study of what is in our hands the most readily reproducible procedure for a modified physiological state leading to an increased resistance to radiation damage, that which results when the cells are grown in the presence of certain inorganic salts.
