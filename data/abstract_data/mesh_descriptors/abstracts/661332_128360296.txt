The Crown Jewel, an Enigmatic Solitary Mass of Follicular Origin: Answer.
DISCUSSION Trichoadenoma (TA) is yet another misnomer in dermatopathology because it is a distinct benign cutaneous adnexal tumor of follicular, but not of glandular origin.1 First described by Nikolowski almost 60 years ago, it is a rare enigmatic tumor with predilection for the face (57.5% of cases) or buttocks (24% of cases) of adults.2 It has, however, rarely been reported on the thighs, neck, back, and genital area.2 We add this case to the limited repertoire of TA’s arising on the back. Clinically, the solitary nodule of a trichoadenoma typically measures between 0.5 to 3 cm, appears skin-colored or gray, and has a slow growth process. The clinical history is most often nonspecific, and an excisional or punch biopsy are key to establishing the diagnosis.1 Furthermore, it is believed to be composed of numerous infundibular structures suggestive of a maturity of follicular differentiation between that of trichoepithelioma (less mature) and trichofolliculoma (more mature).3 Unusual cooccurrences with sebaceous carcinoma, syringocystadenoma papilliferum, and trichoblastoma have been reported.3 Histology typically reveals a well-circumscribed tumor populated by egg-shaped infundibulocystic structures.1 These structures may morphologically be independent or be connected by strands of epithelial cells, in a “back-to-back continuity”.1 The cyst content is of pale keratinous material, and certain cysts may be ruptured. Closer inspection of each cyst lining is expected to show a discrete granular layer with keratohyaline granules, concentric epithelial cells, and palisading of basal cells at the periphery, confirming morphological differentiation toward the infundibular portion of the pilosebaceous unit.1,2 Cornification foci, budding of the horny cysts, is observed between the mature cysts. The tumor presented in this case demonstrated these classic features of the cystic component of a trichoadenoma, yet showed a predominance of the solid component, composed of isthmic-catagen–type epithelium, of the small round blue cells densely packed between the cysts. To our knowledge, this solid variant of trichoadenoma has not been previously described. As for the stroma of TA, it is usually paucicellular and fibrotic.1 Clefting between the tumor capsule and the adjacent dermis is commonly observed. A foreign body’s granulomatous reaction in the dermis is typical, yet was absent in our specimen.1 We deem the compact hyperkeratosis conferring the esthetic “jewel” of the “crown” of our specimen to be akin to a lichen simplex chronicus–type phenomenon, from probable constant friction on the back of our patient. We considered in our differential diagnosis other benign cutaneous adnexal tumors such as a pilar sheath acanthoma, a desmoplastic trichoepithelioma, and an eccrine poroma. First, a pilar sheath acanthoma, although of infundibulocystic differentiation as well, usually also has elements of isthmic differentiation, with PAS + pale epidermal cells.4 Second, our main reason for excluding the diagnosis of a desmoplastic trichoepithelioma was the definite absence of germ follicular figures, common to desmoplastic trichoepitheliomae.5 Although desmoplastic trichoepithelioma also has cords of epithelial cells in a sclerotic stroma, the cystic component is minimal, and the tumor clinically is almost always found in the face of women. Had there been more diagnostic uncertainty, we may have considered staining with immunohistochemical assays for Ber-EP4, as desmoplastic trichoepithelioma is usually Ber-EP4 positive, whereas trichoadenoma is Ber-EP4 negative.5 Third, although the anastomosing basaloid strands of an eccrine poroma may seem similar to the solid component of our specimen of trichoadenoma, eccrine poromas reside in the epidermis, not dermis, and are packed of “poroid” cells. The absence of ductal differentiation makes a diagnosis of poroma unlikely.6 To conclude, trichoadenoma of Nikolowski is a rare benign follicular tumor of infundibular differentiation. A compact hyperkeratosis may allude to the appearance of a bright eosinophilic jewel on a violet crown. Although a predominantly cystic variant is invariably observed in previously reported cases, we suggest considering a solid variant of trichoadenoma among the differential diagnosis of benign follicular tumors.
