Conformations of antibiotics in active sites of aminoglycoside-detoxifying enzymes
Aminoglycoside antibiotics are a class of aminocyclitol antibiotics that were among the first antibacterial agents to be clinically useful (1,2). One of the largest groups of aminoglycoside antibiotics is that in which the aglycone is 2-deoxystreptamine (2-DOS). Generally, these aminoglycosides are 4,5or 4,6-disubstituted at the 2-DOS ring, and can be pseudotri-, tetra-, or pentasaccharides (Fig. 1). Aminoglycosides with 4,5and 4,6-disubstituted 2-DOS rings can also be classified as neomycinand kanamycingroup antibiotics, respectively. The substituents on the 2-DOS ring are numbered 1–6, and this ring is labeled as the unprimed ring, or ring B. The sugar ring, attached by a glycosidic linkage to the 4-position of the 2-DOS ring, is labeled as the prime (′) ring or ring A. The sugar ring, attached by a glycosidic linkage to the 5or 6-position of the 2-DOS ring, is labeled as the double-prime (′′) ring, or ring C. Some aminocyclitols, such as butirosin A and amikacin, have a 2-DOS ring that is modified at N-1 with an (S)-4-amino-2-hydroxybutyryl group (AHB), which is denoted with a triple prime (′ ′′). The 2-DOS ring of isepamicin is modified at N-1 with an (S)-3-amino-2-hydroxypropionyl group. Aminoglycoside antibiotics bind to the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit, and interfere with the normal protein biosynthesis, which eventually leads to cell death (3). Aminoglycosides bind to 16S RNA, and disrupt protein synthesis (4). They are used for the treatment of serious infections caused mostly by staphylococci and enterococci. However, the clinical effectiveness of these antibiotics is significantly reduced by the emergence of strains resistant to their action. The covalent modification of aminoglycoside antibiotics, by pathogenic bacteria, leads to their detoxification (5,6). The extensive use of antibiotics, such as aminoglycosides, over many years, has put tremendous pressure on bacteria.
