Benign Breast Disease and Cancer Risk: New Perspectives
The difficult question of the relationship between benign breast disease (BRD) and cancer has troubled pathologist and surgeon alike for many decades. Despite a great deal of literature on the subject, there is still no clear answer. Different investigators have given widely varying estimates of the cancer risk, but the explanation for this variance may be that in many of these studies both the methodologies and the terminologies have varied widely as well. The epidemiology of breast disease has been intensively studied in the last 20 years, especially with reference to breast cancer. Benign breast disease and breast cancer have some epidemiologic features in common. A family history of previous breast cancer is higher in both conditions and the protective effect of long-term oral contraceptive use may be present in both. However, the predictive power of a previous personal history of BBD is weak and is of no practical value to the clinician. The same can be said for personal endocrine risk factors such as late menarche and age at first pregnancy, which may be statistically significant indicators of risk but are hardly of any clinical value. Family history of breast cancer can be important if it is in a premenopausal first-degree relative and it may be of even greater importance if associated with a biopsy showing epithelial atypia.' Of course a personal history of a cancerous lesion in the other breast is currently the best risk indicator available, with a lifetime risk of 10% for a second occurrence of cancer. To date biochemical markers have been of no value, and the controversial Wolfe classification of mammographic parenchymal patterns' is, as yet, unproven, and no prospective follow-up has been reported. Several papers have reported that the Wolfe patterns do not correlate with increased cancer risk, but many of these studies have been criticized for statistical and methodologic deficiencies3 Thus, the simple clinical diagnosis of BBD is not very helpful in the assessment of future cancer risk. This is not entirely surprising when we consider t h t variety and imprecision of clinicopathologic terms used to describe the condition. It might be thought that histologic assessment would give a clearer guide t o cancer risk, but again there are several problems that reduce the value of histology as a marker of risk. The main problems are the subjective nature of histologic reporting, the lack of prospective studies, and the lack of agreement on precise terminology between pathologists. This is well illustrated by the use of a completely different word to describe the same process of epithelial hyperplasia in Europe and America. As Azzopardi has pointed out in his book Problems in Breast Pathology,' his preferred term for epithelial hyperplasia within ducts or ductules is epitheliosis since the hyperplasia involves the epithelium and has many forms including the solid and the papillary.
