Re: Prostate specific antigen testing policy worldwide varies greatly and seems not to be in accordance with guidelines: a systematic review.
available at http://jurology.com/ Editorial Comment: The recent American Urological Association guidelines on prostate cancer screening only address the age at which it is appropriate to discuss prostate specific antigen (PSA) testing and, to some degree, the frequency if a patient wishes to be screened after informed consent. Imagine the firestorm if the panel had suggested thresholds for biopsy! Yet this article indicates that there is clearly a knowledge gap regarding how best to use the results of PSA screening. Many in the urological community will make impassioned pleas that we need to decrease the testing threshold and screen more often, citing their last 45-year-old patient with a PSA of 2.6 ng/ml in whom they performed biopsy, found Gleason 9 prostate cancer and performed prostatectomy. While these cases tug on our heartstrings, they do not provide proof that we are doing the patient a service. After all, patients do have disease recurrence and even die after “successful” prostatectomy. This systematic review (and the controversy around the recent guidelines) underscores the need for additional and better long-term studies using meaningful end points such as overall and metastatic-free survival to determine the best way to screen and even whether we should be screening at all. Abstract available at http://jurology.com/available at http://jurology.com/ TESTIS CANCER AND ADVANCES IN ONCOLOGIC THERAPY 1767 Editorial Comment: These 2 articles tell a similar story, although the differences in the way the data are presented and what the authors highlight in the discussions have led the mainstream media to interpret them differently. In effect, both articles clearly illustrate that we continue to overtreat low risk, localized prostate cancer, using aggressive interventions with well-known side effects for men who likely have little or nothing to gain in terms of survival from the treatment. However, while one of the articles stops there, simply calling on providers and patients to be more selective in the application of aggressive interventions, the other implies that overtreatment is being driven by financial incentives for the provider. This last point is not supported by the data presented. The study by Daskivich et al (for the sake of full disclosure I am a listed author) explores the relationship between age, tumor risk and comorbid disease in survival of men diagnosed with and treated for localized prostate cancer in 1994 and 1995. The study reveals that the majority of patients undergo aggressive therapy consisting of surgery or radiation, despite many being older and/or sicker. Importantly it also shows that older and sicker men (and frankly even younger and healthy men) with low risk disease are much more likely to die of something other than prostate cancer. Conversely if a patient has high risk disease, his risk of prostate cancer mortality is considerable even if he is older and/or sicker. The take home message here is that we need to be more selective about whom we aggressively treat—no more, no less. Jacobs et al correctly assume that low risk disease often does not require treatment and then look at changes in patterns of care from 2004 to 2009. They note that overall treatment rates remained stable during the study period but observe that use of intensity modulated radiotherapy and robotic surgery increased, while use of less technologically advanced forms of external beam radiation treatment and open surgery declined. Clearly these newer and more expensive technologies were rapidly adopted ahead of evidence to support their superiority compared to older techniques. However, the authors imply that a possible explanation for this occurrence was that there were financial incentives, including “ownership opportunities, growing market share and fee-for-service reimbursement,” that drove the changes. Specifically in the case of robotic surgery it is hard for me to understand how financial incentives would drive providers to want to use this approach preferentially. After all, the professional fees are virtually identical and most urologists do not have an ownership interest in the robot. The important message here is that we need to be careful how we interpret the data. The solution to this problem is not through a policy that outlaws ownership or decreases reimbursement. The solution is high quality, comparative effectiveness research studies that document outcomes in a way that patients can understand, coupled with a change in the way urologists view low risk prostate cancer, which includes increased use of active surveillance.
