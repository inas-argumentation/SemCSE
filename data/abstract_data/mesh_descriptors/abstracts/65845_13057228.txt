Since the management of many conditions requires the exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and since investigation is often designed to monitor the maintenance, for example, of normal plasma potassium during the correction of an abnormality such as potassium depletion in diabetic ketoacidosis, it would appear to be fundamentally incorrect to assess the usefulness of investigations in the way in which Dr Sandler has done. In his section on the value of normal results he points out that a normal result could have been useful in 13°, of the admissions. This percentage is too high to be casually dismissed with the benefit of hindsight. In addition, it would be useful to know whether a judicious request for a single resultfor example, a serum potassium or blood urea concentration-led to a single investigation or a set piece group of results which may have swelled the figures for non-useful findings. We were also surprised that emergency investigations were entirely at the discretion of the preregistration house officer; this makes it very difficult to relate Dr Sandler's results to circumstances in many other hospitals in the UK and therefore totally invalidates his extrapolated estimate of financial saving nationwide. Also the percentage of chemical pathology budgets spent on out of hours emergency work is very small: if dramatic savings are to be made then audits of outpatient and daytime investigations in all departments should be undertaken. We believe that Dr Sandler's comments give an unjustifiably biased view of the role of emergency medical investigation, and in the absence of information on the positive value of the "normal result" we hope that his paper will not be used as a justification for the restriction of emergency laboratory services.
