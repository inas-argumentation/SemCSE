Breast Cancer Risks and Screening Practices among Women Living in a Resource Poor Community in Tshwane, South Africa
To the Editor:Despite breast cancer being believed to be a diseaseof the developed world, the incidence is increasingespecially in the developing world, where 69% ofbreast cancer-related deaths occur (1). The averageage of women in sub-Saharan Africa at diagnosis isapproximately 10 years lower than that of breast can-cer patients in western nations (2). In South Africa,breast cancer is the most common cancer in white,colored, and Asian women, with Black women havingthe lowest incidence rates (3). Unfortunately only22% of Black female patients present with early stagesI and II breast cancer, in contrast to nearly 69% ofthe non-Black patients (4). Matatiele and Van denHeever (5) report an alarming increase in the inci-dence of breast cancer among young Black women ofless than 35 years of age and it appears as thoughthese young Black women are more likely to be diag-nosed with a more virulent form of the disease. Apf-felstaedt and Baatjes (6) do not paint a positivepicture of the care of South African women diagnosedwith breast cancer as only 2,000 of the 8,000 newlydiagnosed patients are treated in health care facilitiesoffering some state of the art diagnostic and treatmentservices. Early diagnosis is therefore of cardinalimportance. Early detection and treatment services inSouth Africa are lacking despite the fact that morethan 3,000 women annually die of a disease which, ifdetected early, has an excellent chance of being cured(7).The aim of our study was to determine the level ofknowledge of breast cancer among Black women liv-ing in a resource poor community in Tshwane, SouthAfrica, speciﬁc risks, and screening practices. Ourstudy was based on a cervical and breast cancer pre-vention and screening project situated in the speciﬁccommunity. We conducted a quantitative survey usingstructured interviews with a self-developed question-naire as survey instrument. Sampling was convenience.All women in 18 years and older who reported at theproject between March and October 2008 and werewilling to participate were recruited for the study.Recruitment continued until the sample totaled 299(n = 299), and the participation rate was 100%.Descriptive statistics and Chi-squared test were usedto analyze the data.Our sample (n = 299) represented various culturalgroups and was primarily between the ages of 20 and39 (87.6%). Most (73.9%) were literate, unemployed(93.6%), and unmarried (71.3%). The mean age was30.7 years. The income of the sample was low as99.3% had no personal income or an income of lessthan 300USD per month. The majority (89.6%) of theparticipants reported they had given birth to at leastone child. The sample’s knowledge of the warningsigns of breast cancer was low as the greatest percent-age (80.6%) were unaware of any warning sign. Only18.4% identiﬁed a lump in the breast as warning signwhile less than 4% could identify one or more of theother warning signs. Dimpling of the skin was theleast known risk factor. Only two respondents wereable to identify all seven warning signs. Chi–squaredtest revealed a signiﬁcant association between theknowledge of the warning signs and the level of liter-acy (v²=0.15; p = 0.01;df= 1).We found the sample (n = 299) had a largely protec-tive reproductive history as only a small percentage(4.7%) had started menstruating before the age of 12;the majority (89.3%) had their ﬁrst child before the ageof 30, while less than 40% reported having used oralcontraceptives.
