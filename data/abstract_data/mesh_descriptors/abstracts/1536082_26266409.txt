A genetic linkage map of the mouse Chromosome 9 region encompassing the Friend virus susceptibility gene 2 (Fv2)
Forty years ago Charlotte Friend first described a cell-free extract that was capable of inducing erythroleukemia in adult mice (Friend 1957). Since that time, the study of Friend erythroleukemia virus has been a powerful system for studying both the initiation and the progression of leukemia as well as the mechanisms that regulate erythropoiesis. Friend virus is a complex of two retroviruses, a pathogenic and replication-defective spleen focus-forming virus (SFFV) and a replication-competent helper virus (F-MuLV) (BenDavid and Bernstein 1991; Kabat 1989). The erythroleukemia induced by Friend virus proceeds through at least two stages. Early after infection, there is a rapid polyclonal expansion of erythroid progenitors, driven by the interaction and activation of the erythropoietin receptor (EPO-R) by gp55, the envelope glycoprotein encoded by SFFV (Li et al. 1990). The progression of the disease from this premalignant stage to the later leukemic stage requires the acquisition of further mutations that lead to the expansion of a leukemic clone. Two major genetic changes occur during this progression of Friend leukemia: mutations that inactivate the p53 tumor suppressor gene (Mowat et al. 1985) and transcriptional activation by proviral insertion of either Sfpil or Flil, members of the ets family of transcription factors (Ben-David et al. 1991; Moreau-Gachelin et al. 1988). In addition to these acquired alterations that are selected for during the evolution of Friend leukemia, a number of host loci govern susceptibility to erythroleukemia induction by Friend virus. These host loci affect either the progression of the disease or interfere with the ability of Friend virus to infect its target cell. Three mouse mutations that cause anemia—the Dominant white spotting (W), Steel (Si), and flex tail O loci—all govern susceptibility to Friend virus infection (Axelrad 1968; Bennett et al. 1968; Steeves et al. 1968). Because these loci play important roles in the normal development of erythroid progenitors, mutations at these loci may affect the expansion of infected cells during the early stages of the disease. Several other loci have been identified solely on the basis of their ability to confer resistance to Friend virus infection. These genes were originally identified as host resistance genes that segregated between inbred strains of mice. Two of these genes, Friend susceptibility gene 1 (Fvl) and Friend susceptibility gene 4 (Fv4), function by blocking some aspect of the retroviral life cycle. The recently cloned Fv1 encodes a retroviral gag-like protein that blocks retroviral infection at several points during the viral life cycle by an unknown mechanism (Best et al. 1996; Jolicoeur 1979). The Fv4 locus encodes an endogenous ecotropic retroviral envelope protein, which is thought to interfere with the interaction between Friend virus and its receptor, thereby blocking infection (Kai et al. 1986).
