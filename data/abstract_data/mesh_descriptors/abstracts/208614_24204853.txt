Bacterial cell wall hydrolases as novel alternatives to antibiotics: a critical comment on Parisien et al.’s (2008) interpretation of the topic
Dear Sir, We would like to comment on interpretation of bacterial cell wall hydrolases (BCWH) as novel alternatives to conventional antibiotics made in the Journal of Applied Microbiology by Parisien et al. (2008), since, in our opinion, the interpretation is not up-to-date as well as it contains several confusing and ⁄ or misleading thoughts. Firstly, by replacing the generally accepted classification of BCWH (Strominger and Ghuysen 1967) with their own one the authors ‘lost’ the entire class of extracellular BCWH secreted by microorganisms belonging to different taxonomic groups (Kislukhina et al. 1990). This type of enzyme is of special importance in view of the clinical use of BCWH (see below). Proteases capable to lyse bacteria were not given a place in the authors’ classification as well; in this latter regard, it seems that the authors did not think about the question of where does the borderline between ‘proteases’, ‘lytic proteases’ and ‘lytic enzymes’ lie (Biziulevičius 2006) at all. Secondly, it is difficult to understand why the authors after they have successfully cited the scientifically found Isaac Ginsburg’s concept of activating in situ the autolytic system of pathogenic bacteria using cationic peptides do not develop the idea further, rather are speaking about autolysins as (i) exogenous antimicrobial agents. In light of the fact that a prophecy presented in the most recent review on the topic by the same Ginsburg (2004) that the peptides ‘might one day be constructed as drugs to activate autolytic systems especially of antibiotic-resistant bacteria’ is turning into reality (Biziulevičius and Kazlauskait_ e 2007) the authors’ position becomes even more strange. Thirdly, we disagree with the (ii) and (iii) points regarding the advantages of BCWH therapy. Today’s understanding of how do BCWH really act in vivo conditions is still not far from its zero point; therapeutic application of any protein, especially when by non-oral route, from the immunological point of view is always more or less problematic. Taken on the other hand, health benefits of oral application of BCWH (or of activation of microbial autolysis within the gut) resulting from the immunostimulatory microbial lysis products released in situ in the intestines (Sava 1996; Biziulevičius and Kazlauskait_ e 2007) might be considered as an advantage of BCWH therapy. Fourth, while accepting that virolysins are now most commonly referred as endolysins the authors remain using the former term. Though the given arguments are rather reasonable, when speaking about exogenous application of purified BCWH of phage origin (lysis from without) the term bacteriophage endolysins or simply endolysins, we think, is a more proper one; the term enzybiotic, a hybrid word from enzyme and antibiotic, might suit even better (Veiga-Crespo et al. 2007). Fifth, the most striking fact, however, is that the authors do not provide any information about the BCWH preparations authorized for human and ⁄ or veterinary medical use in a lot of countries (mostly European and Asian). These, as far as we know, include: native hen egg-white lysozyme (HEWL), HEWL hydrochloride, HEWL dimer (Lydium KLP), lysozyme from Bacillus subtilis G-28, lysoamidase from Lysobacter sp. XL1, lysosubtilin from B. subtilis SK-52, Fermosorb (an acid-protected form of lysosubtilin), and Polyferm (an acid-protected B. subtilis 103 neutral protease preparation) (Sava 1996; Biziulevičius and Žukait_ e 2001; Malinowski 2001; Biziulevičius et al. 2007; Donovan 2007); all except for the first three are microbial preparations of extracellular BCWH (the latter were emphasized above as ‘lost’ when classifying BCWH). We think that in regard to the above the authors simply missed a good chance to show the reader of how realistic is the subject (novel alternative to antibiotics) they propose.
