Dioxin and cancer: the never-ending story
Dioxins and dioxin-like compounds are a group of chlorinated chemicals produced unintentionally by combustion processes, including waste incineration, by some types of chemical manufacturing and paper manufacturing, and by other processes. One congener, 2,3,7,8TCDD, contaminates phenoxy herbicides and chlorophenols; it gained notoriety because of its presence in ~gent Orange' and the explosion at an Italian chemical factory in Seveso, Italy, that contaminated the surrounding regions with dioxin. Dioxins are extremely potent animal carcinogens and consequently they have become emblematic of man-made environmental carcinogens, which are perceived by the public as causing cancer at even the smallest doses. The public's fear of dioxins has been stoked by publicity given to the evacuation of Times Beach, Missouri (United States) because of soil contamination with dioxins and to the linking of Agent Orange to cancer in Vietnam veterans. While the findings in animal bioassays have made the dioxins feared carcinogens, risks to human heakh have not been readily characterized. Research findings on the mechanism of dioxin carcinogenesis have shown that risks in animals cannot be extrapolated directly to humans and that such extrapolations need to be mechanistically based) Epidemiologic studies of dioxin-exposed groups represent an alternative but complementary approach to toxicologic research. They have the potential to provide estimates directly of the risks of cancer and other diseases, thereby avoiding the uncertainty associated with cross-species extrapolation. With this rationale, substantial efforts have been made to investigate cancer and other diseases in dioxin-exposed populations. The groups available for investigation include the accidentally exposed residents of Seveso, 2 soldiers in the Vietnam conflict, 3 and workers who manufacture or apply phenoxyacid herbicides and chlorophenols. 4"6 In this issue of Cancer Causes and Control, Becher and co-workers 7 report findings on cancer mortality in 2,479 workers from four German plants that manufacture phenoxy-acids, herbicides, and chlorophenols. Patterns of exposure differed across the plants. Measurements of TCDD levels in tissue fat documented exposure to dioxin in two of the cohorts and outbreaks of chloracne, indicative of high exposures, had taken place in these same two plants. The methods of the investigation were straightforward: four parallel retrospective cohort studies with pooling of the data. In the largest cohort, that of Boehringer Ingelheim workers (n = 1,144), overall cancer mortality was elevated (standardized mortality ratio [SMR] =116, 95 percent confidence intervals [CI] = 104-129), but SMRs for all cancers were below 100 for the other three cohorts. By primary cancer site, comparison of results across cohorts was compromised by small numbers of deaths. The pooled analysis showed excess deaths for cancers of the buccal cavity and pharynx (SMR = 295, CI = 135-560), lung cancer (SMR=143, CI=105-190), non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (SMR = 326, CI = 119-710), and lymphatic and hematopoetic cancers pooled (SMR = 168, CI = 89-286)• Analyses by years since first exposure did not show convincing gradients of excess cancer with increasing latency. The findings from this new pooled analysis add to a slowly enlarging body of epidemiologic evidence from dioxin-exposed workers. Key populations comprise the 18,910 production workers and sprayers included in the International Agency for Research on Cancer's (IARC) • 4 10-country registry and 5,172 US workers from 12 plants under investigation by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH). s The findings from these three analyses are not fully consistent. The IARC and NIOSH studies showed excess soft-tissue sarcomas, a finding confirmed in a case-control study of an expanded case group from the IARC registry, s By contrast, no soft tissue sarcomas were observed in the German workers, although only 1.2 were expected. The excess of lymphato-hematopoietic tumors found in the German cohorts was not present in the other two groups. Respiratory cancers were in excess in both the German and the NIOSH cohorts, but confounding by cigarette smoking could not be addressed. Overall, the new report by Becher and colleagues reinforces concerns that dioxins and related compounds are carcinogenic in humans, although the relatively small sample size of the study limits the informativeness of site-specific analyses.
