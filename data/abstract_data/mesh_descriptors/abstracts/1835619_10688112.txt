Antibody-guided scintigraphy: targeting of the “magic bullet”
The concept of using labelled polyclonal antibodies for the diagnosis of cancer was introduced in 1948 by Pressman [1] and their potential clinical usefulness for radioimmunodetection was exploited some 30 years later by Goldenberg [2]. The discovery of monoclonal antibodies (MoAbs) in 1975 [3] represented a quantum leap in this area, leading to the development of what is today known as radioimmunoscintigraphy and to the concept of "targeting" [47]. The possibility of directing high-specificity radiolabelled antibodies to a tumour was indeed very exciting, providing the chance to turn into reality the "magic bullet" idea postulated by Paul Ehrlich at the beginning of the twentieth century [8]. However, the problem that the target might be reached by only a small percentage of the bullets, thus resulting in high untargeted background activity, was evident from the outset [9, 10]. Properly selected MoAbs can be very specific for turnout cells in vivo, but only a small percentage (less than 1%) of the total MoAbs injected effectively binds to the desired antigenic target, that is, tumour cells. The remaining 99% of the MoAbs (and therefore if they are labelled with radioactive isotopes of the radioactivity) will distribute in the rest of the body, mainly in the bloodstream and liver. This in itself would prevent MoAbs being used for either radioimmunodetection or radioimmunotherapy. However, the unbound antibodies are progressively eliminated and the target/non-target ratio increases within 2-3 days to a value which allows the visualization of a tumour when the MoAbs are labelled with relatively long-lived isotdpes (such as iodine131 or indium-Ill). This approach has led to promising diagnostic and therapeutic protocols [ 11-14]. Yet, the relatively low target/non-target ratio still prevents radioimmunoscintigraphy and radioimmunotherapy from having widespread clinical application. Indeed, the problem of cancer targeting is complex. Let us try to simplify it by considering two fundamental aspects: the first is related to the bullets (the antibodies) and the second to the target (the tumour). As for the bullets, not all antibodies are the same. Even if the ideal tumour-specific antibody does not yet exist, there are sufficient tumour-associated MoAbs with good specificity and affinity for several tumour antigens. Advances in molecular biology and recombinant DNA technology will probably offer a significant contribution in this field in the near future [15]. Chemical changes induced by labelling can sometimes alter the antibody immunoreactivity and cause autoradiolysis. Recent methods of labelling and conjugating with new chelating agents are proving important advances in the development of safe and stable procedures [16, 171. In an attempt to speed up blood clearance, MoAbs have been split up into bivalent or monovalent fragments or reduced to just the hypervariable region. However, this reduces their binding affinity. Antibody fragments, such as F(ab)2 or Fab fragments, have a faster blood clearance than whole antibodies, to the extent of allowing the use of short-lived radionuclides such as technetium-99m. This improvement, however, does not yield tumour-to-background ratios high enough to allow good diagnostic sensitivity and low-risk therapeutic applications. The second issue to be considered in the optimization of targeting is related to the tumour itself. The distribution of antibodies targeted to tumour antigens is not uniform; rather, accumulation occurs in the periphery and perivascular areas of the turnout, thus limiting the concentration of MoAbs. This happens in all tumours larger than 1-2 cm [18]. One of the reasons is that, as a tumour gets bigger, the interstitial pressure becomes greater than the blood capillary pressure, resulting in the slowing down and eventual cessation of the flow of fluid towards the centre of the tumour. A second obstacle to deep penetration by MoAbs is the "binding site barrier", which depends on various factors and in particular the binding affinity between antibodies and the corresponding antigens [19]. This results in preferential accumulation of the antibodies in the perivascular margins and poor penetration into the tumour, due also to a reduction in free antibody molecules available for binding with deeply situated neoplastic cells. Various studies [20, 21] have confirmed the existence of this obstacle to the deep infiltration of antibodies into the neoplasm. It has also been noted that increasing the binding affinity causes a further increase in this non-uniform distribution and that this happens especially when using highly specific MoAbs with low initial plasma concentrations [20].
