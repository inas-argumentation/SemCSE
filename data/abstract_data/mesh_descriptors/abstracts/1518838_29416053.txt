Vitamin D and parathyroid hormone: a new piece in the puzzle of cardiovascular risk.
Chronic vitamin D deficiency increases parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels and can determine secondary hyperparathyroidism. PTH receptors have been identified in endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle, and cardiomyocytes, leading to the hypothesis that inappropriate PTH levels could affect the calcium and phosphate homeostasis in cardiovascular system. Additionally, vitamin D deficiency upregulates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, resulting in a higher risk of cardiovascular disease. However, a causal link between vitamin D deficiency, hyperparathyroidism, and cardiovascular diseases has never been demonstrated, even if several authors have shown a relationship between low levels of vitamin D, high levels of PTH, risk of cardiovascular diseases, and mortality. Recently, the Copenhagen City Heart Study demonstrated that lower plasma 25-hydroxy vitamin D [25(OH)D] levels were associated with a higher risk of ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, and early death in 10 170 men and women during 29 years of follow-up. Additionally, the same authors performed a meta-analysis of 18 population studies on ischemic heart disease and 17 on early death, and they confirmed an increased risk of ischemic heart disease and early death, as shown in the main study. Similarly, the Whitehall study demonstrated that a doubling in 25(OH)D concentration was associated with 20% lower vascular and 23% lower nonvascular mortality in 5409 men during 13 years of follow-up. A meta-analysis performed by the same authors and including 12 prospective studies with 42 565 patients and 4632 vascular deaths and 18 prospective studies with 77 894 patients and 11 734 deaths from all causes showed that individuals in the top vs. bottom quarter of 25(OH)D concentration had on average 21% lower vascular mortality and 28% lower total mortality.
