Antibiotic Resistance by Enzyme Inactivation: From Mechanisms to Solutions
We are addicted to antibiotics. Relatively routine modern medical procedures from heart surgery to joint replacements rely on prophylactic antibiotic therapy to ward off postoperative infection. Immune suppression is also common now in a growing patient population, including those who have undergone organ transplants, cancer chemotherapy, the elderly, and those with chronic diseases associated with infections such as cystic fibrosis and HIV/AIDS, all of which are reliant on infection control through antibiotics. Without antibiotics, modern medicine would look very different. Paradoxically, as the number of medical procedures that rely on antibiotics multiplies and life quality and span increase as a consequence, fewer antibiotics are being developed by the pharmaceutical sector. There are many reasons for this state of affairs, ranging from economic and regulatory challenges to rapid global movement of infectious agents and the realization that new antibiotics are not easily discovered by using modern high-throughput target-based approaches. Meeting these challenges will require the input of stakeholders across the drug to patient pipeline. Nevertheless, perhaps the most pervasive problem is that of antibiotic resistance; this problem is one that can largely be addressed by new research. What is now clear is that antibiotic resistance is a global phenomenon that is not confined to pathogenic bacteria. In fact, environmental bacteria can be highly resistant through the same mechanisms that are found in pathogens. 4] Therefore an understanding of the origins, evolution, and mechanisms of clinical resistance needs to include nonpathogenic bacteria as likely reservoirs of existing and emerging resistance elements. We have termed the entire collection of antibiotic resistance genes the “antibiotic resistome”. The resistome includes the genes that circulate in pathogenic bacteria, those required for self-resistance in antibiotic producers, and the plethora of cryptic resistance genes found in virtually all genomes of bacteria. The global density of resistance genes and the means to mobilize them across microbial species and genera explains decades of observation in the clinic that drug resistance is inevitable. The consequence is a constant need for new drug discovery. Resistance elements are ubiquitous across the globe. In addition to this, selection pressure, which is the result of the modern era of clinical and agricultural use, has fueled the emergence of individual strains of pathogens resistant to multiple antibiotics and often to a single class of antibiotics through a number of distinct mechanisms. This combinatorial resistance is particularly problematic for the development of new therapies. Mechanisms of antibiotic resistance include barrier to cell entry, active efflux, target alteration, and the action of enzymes that modify or otherwise inactivate the antibiotics. Compounding these direct mechanisms are indirect mechanisms of antibiotic tolerance including cell growth condition (planktonic, spore or biofilm formation) or intrinsic physiology such as the impermeant outermembrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Understanding the molecular mechanisms of antibiotic resistance is essential for the discovery of new agents and the development of new strategies to extend the lifetime of known agents. This area is directly at the interface of the disciplines of chemistry and microbiology, and success requires the cooperation of researchers in a number of fields. Here, we outline recent advances in our knowledge of the chemical biology of resistance enzymology and how these studies can be leveraged in development the next-generation antimicrobial therapies.
