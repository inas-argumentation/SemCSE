HIV Postexposure Prophylaxis Starter Packs Were Not Designed to Help or Hinder Adherence.
TO THE EDITOR—It was concerning to read the conclusions of the systematic review by Ford and colleagues [1] promoting full 28-day starts of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) over traditional starter packs usually containing 1–7 days’ worth of drugs dispensed at the initial medical visit, often within a busy emergency department (ED). The premise appears to be based on an analysis trying to suggest that starter packs do not promote adherence and may even undermine it [1]. The review also appears to be the justification for the formal opinion expressed in Section 4.6 of the recent World Health Organization (WHO) HIV PEP guidelines [2]. However, the premise is based on a flawed assumption regarding the primary purpose of starter packs. Starter packs are used to promote early access at multiple sites within a local healthcare system; to ensure continuity of care during followup; and to test the exposed client’s tolerance and adherence without significantly wasting expensive HIV drugs. They were never designed to improve adherence, which is perhaps one reason why the authors were unable to find any published studies that directly tested their hypothesis. The authors placed their entire argument on a single study by Kim et al [3], which makes a historical comparison within a rural South African program between an earlier period of limited resources but using starter packs, and a later period of enhanced resources but using the full PEP regimens. This implies significant risk of confounding [4, 5]. Kim et al [3] also concluded that their program was not sustainable at the end of the study period. Information bias [4, 5] was also incorporated into the analysis by Ford et al [1], when at least one program (ie, HIV Postexposure Prophylaxis With Darunavir/r [PEPDar]) providing a 3day starter pack was misclassified as providing a full 28 days of PEP at the initial visit [6]. The WHO systematic review is also likely affected by dissemination bias due to a majority of current HIV PEP programs not having dedicated funding or protected time to publish their own experiences [7]. For example, over the past decade, the Calgary regional program has consolidated all forms of follow-up (occupational and nonoccupational) into a specialized clinic within a catchment area of about 1.5 million people, employing one medical consultant at 0.2 fulltime equivalent to manage distribution of publicly funded starter packs, as well as following all referred clients. In the real world, rapid access to PEP is efficiently done through EDs during the evening and night when many occupational and most nonoccupational cases typically present for care. As with other chemoprophylaxis, adherence relies primarily on active participation of patients. Factors undermining adherence are multifactorial, including perception of risk, drug tolerance, and other issues unrelated to provision of starter packs. Occupational cases often suffer from overadherence, where it is challenging to discontinue prophylaxis in very low-risk scenarios involving exposed individuals, who are used to being in control (eg, physicians, police). Nonoccupational cases often suffer from underadherence, whereby providers or parents are desperately trying to convince exposed individuals, who are ambivalent. Some vulnerable groups may deliberately reduce their risk perception as a coping mechanism (eg, victims of domestic violence). Finally, the WHO makes the above “strong recommendation” based on “lowquality evidence” [2], whichis an oxymoron and goes against the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation process [8].
