Be careful: They are getting older and so are those around them
‘‘The management of octogenarians with coronary artery disease remains a major therapeutic challenge.’’ They are at ‘‘the highest risk of procedural complications owing to the high prevalence of associated comorbidities [1].’’ We have heard it before, and to the readers of the Journal, it is a weekly if not daily reminder, as well. These two articles on percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in the elderly are a reality check on you; yes, I am speaking of you and what you are doing today. In one study [1], octogenarians represented 11.5% of patients undergoing PCI, and while the study of nonagenarians was smaller by its design, it gives us a glimpse of where we are headed, as we get older and our patients’ age with us [2]. Personally, I have noted a marked shift in the age of my patients when I moved from a fair-sized Arizona city to a smaller community. The median age of my patients undergoing PCI was 64 years in Tucson, but is 71 years here in this smaller rural and semiretirement community (Personal database). In Tucson, averaged over two decades, 7% of my patients were over 80 and only four patients undergoing PCI were >90 years of age. They each did splendidly, by the way. In my new service area, 19% of the PCI procedures are now in patients over 80-years-old and so far so good. The large multi-center report from Australia states that 11% of patients treated were over 80 years of age; more recent than my database and a better reflection of what you are currently seeing, as well. Since women develop coronary disease at a later age, there were a disproportionate number of elderly women, but more important, there were more complications, higher mortality at follow-up, and more comorbidity overall. Over 90-years-old and undergoing PCI: the Cath Lab staff will be shaking their heads and if things do not go well, so will you. Nevertheless, what about very old patients who may still be active, alert, and lively? In the report from Israel, in over 5.5 years, only 65 patients were over 90-years-old, a relatively small number. Of course, we do not know the denominator, but unless this group is particularly aggressive, surely this group represents a very small percentage of their overall volume. What can we learn from this report? Most of you properly suspected, and now you have it on paper. Expect multivessel disease, expect complex lesions, and expect that 1 in 6 patients, more so in those that present with an unstable ischemic picture, will have had a serious complication by hospital discharge, and if not then be on the lookout at one and six months. All this should not come as a surprise, given the age and high frequency of comorbidities and multivessel disease. What can we do to mitigate these predictable complications? Bleeding events correlate with poor outcomes [3]. Bivalirudin as an antithrombotic should be considered; since all nonagenarians will have some degree of renal impairment (28% of the over 90 had Cr > 1.4 with many more likely having subclinical halving because of age-related glomerular loss) and 11% of those over 80 had evidence of renal failure, with more having sub-clinical age-related renal cortical loss, as well [4]. Avoidance or using lower doses and shorter duration of therapy with a GpIIb/IIIA inhibitor or enoxaparin since both may accumulate in the elderly, renal-impaired patient. Should we be doing more or less PCI in the elderly in lieu of surgery or even medical therapy despite lesions that are apparent? The hard answer does not exist although most elderly patients prefer to avoid surgery. When multivessel, complex disease is present, surgical revascularization must be considered. Many patients benefit from more complete revascularization, despite the higher risk in the elderly [5]. Furthermore, the operative risk may be mitigated with a better-thanaverage surgeon, off-pump, or robotically assisted surgery in selected patients [6].
