PTSD in parents after childhood cancer.
A new study has helped to shed light on what might be an underappreciated sequela of childhood cancer—the psychological burden borne by parents. The Swedish cohort study recruited parents of children recently diagnosed with cancer from four of the county’s six paediatric oncology centres in 2002–04. Of an initial 325 eligible parents, 259 (80%) agreed to complete a questionnaire for post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) at or soon after their child’s cancer diagnosis. This cohort was then followed up, with the questionnaire readministered at up to six more time points; two while their child was undergoing treatment and four after their child’s remission or death. At the fi nal time point, 5 years after remission or bereavement, 169 (52%) of 325 parents undertook the questionnaire again. Unsurprisingly, PTSS decline over time, but were ongoing at the latest time point. At the fourth time point (after the conclusion of treatment), 49 (45%) of 109 mothers and 33 (32%) of 103 fathers qualifi ed as having full or partial post-traumatic stress disorder. At the seventh time point (5 years after remission or death of their child), this proportion was 17 (19%) of 88 mothers and 11 (14%) of 81 fathers, representing ongoing morbidity. Jimmie Holland (Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, New York, NY, USA), widely credited for establishment of the subspeciality of psycho-oncology, remarks that “the data confi rm what most parents well understand—the threats to family and life reverberate for years after the initial treatment, irrespective of outcome. For the bereaved parents, and particularly mothers, trauma is most severe and impressive. However, parents of the children considered successfully treated were also traumatised.” These data are the fi rst known attempt to quantify this burden over time and through diff erent stages—from diagnosis, to treatment, to remission or death of the child. Lisa Ljungman (Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden), the study’s lead investigator, commented to The Lancet Oncology that “even as future research needs to deepen the understanding of these symptoms, and the potential economic consequences they might cause, clinical awareness of these symptoms is warranted”. Her team has collected data for eff ects on parents’ work situation, sick leave, and household income, with plans to publish these economic eff ects in a subsequent paper.
