Response to Editorial Comment to Effect of constipation on serum total prostate‐specific antigen levels in men
This study tried to establish the relationship between constipation and raised serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA), and concluded a direct relationship between them. There are several other factors that are responsible for the raised serum PSA, which might act as confounding factors. These factors must be taken into account before measuring the serum PSA. In this study, serum total PSA values were measured in 136 constipated patients before and after 4-week treatment for constipation. A total of 86.7% (118/136 patients) of constipated patients regularly required chronic manual maneuvers and disimpaction to relieve their constipation. It is proven that digital rectal examination (DRE) and other maneuvers, such as transrectal ultrasound and colonoscopy, can give rise to raised PSA. Therefore, in these 86.7% patients, manual disimpaction might be the cause of raised serum PSA. As the serum PSA was measured after 4 weeks of treatment, it is very important to note the gap between the last digital disimpaction and serum PSA measurement. Similarly, 31.6% (43/ 136) of patients did not improve with oral laxatives, and laxative enema was given to them. Whether this laxative enema has any role in raising the serum PSA by mechanical stimulation of prostate is to be established. All the patients were advised to carry out regular exercise and to consume a high-fiber diet for 4 weeks for the treatment of constipation. Out of these, 28.6% (39/139) patients responded to it in the first 2 weeks, and the rest required other measures, such as laxatives, enema and manual disimpaction along with dietary modification. Both weight loss and a high-fiber diet have a direct impact on the serum PSA value. Weight measurement should be carried out after 4 weeks of regular exercise and a high-fiber diet to document any weight loss.Also, it is very important to document any fat reduction in their diet, along with high-fiber consumption. Fleshner et al. placed 21 men with elevated PSA and no evidence of cancer on prostate biopsies on a low fat, highfiber diet. Of the participants, 45% showed significant reductions in PSA and, although dietary fat reduction was the major hypothesized reason, it is possible that the increased fiber was at least in part responsible for the change. It is also important to note the composition of soluble and insoluble fiber in their diet. Tariq et al. found that soluble fiber might produce a modest reduction in PSA, and the mechanism could relate to increased sterol losses suggested by the association between changes in serum PSA and fecal lithocholic acid excretion, despite no change in serum sex hormones.
