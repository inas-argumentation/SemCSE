Executive Function in Children: Introduction
In the developmental literature, concepts of executive function (EF) have been relatively late emerging. Our understanding of the normal development of EF, and the potential for disruption of these skills due to early brain pathology, continues to be relatively limited, despite the potential for these areas to inform both child and adult theory and practice. It is only in recent times that researchers have begun to include assessment of EF in study protocols, recognising that executive dysfunction may play an important role in outcome and recovery from cerebral disorders impacting on the developing brain. The first step to understand the scope and impact of EF in children is to establish an understanding of the normal sequence of its development. Historically, it has been argued that EF emerges only during late childhood and adolescence, and so plays a minor role in the behavioural features of brain dysfunction during infancy and early childhood. However, there is a growing body of developmental research (reviewed by Anderson in this issue), which describes sequential improvements of EF through childhood, coinciding with growth spurts in frontal lobe development, and implicating an important role for anterior brain regions, and the prefrontal cortex specifically, in the development and mediation of EF. While this may be the case, it is also apparent that these cerebral regions are dependent upon other cerebral areas for input, making it difficult to isolate the involvement of frontal regions from those of other developing cerebral areas in the expression of EF. It may be that the gradual maturation of EF observed through childhood reflects the integrity of cerebral development throughout the brain. Further, the development of executive functions may be inextricably associated with the gradual emergence of other cognitive capacities, with ample evidence for associated gradual increments in skills such as language. It is now apparent, based on behavioural and imaging research plotting the normal development of EF, and from work with samples of adult patients with brain damage, that EF can no longer be conceptualised as a unitary concept, but incorporates a range of skills including planning, attentional control, and cognitive flexibility. For each of these executive domains, unique developmental trajectories have been plotted. Further, clinical findings suggest that deficiencies in each of these sub-skills may be related to dysfunction within specific regions of the anterior cerebral system. Thus, future assessment models for EF must move away from simplistic unidimensional approaches to take account of these findings and address the specific elements of EF and their interactions. The assessment of EF in children has provided a number of challenges, both theoretical and practical. Until recently, most clinical measures of EF had been designed primarily for adult populations. As a result, many were unattractive and irrelevant for children and possessed limited supporting normative data.
