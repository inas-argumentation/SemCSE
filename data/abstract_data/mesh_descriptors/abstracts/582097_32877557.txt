Neuropathological changes in ovine fetuses caused by tickborne fever
TICKBORNE fever is a rickettsial infection of sheep caused by Anaplasma phagocytophilum and transmitted by ticks. After exposure, susceptible animals develop an acute febrile reaction, and in pregnant ewes abortion may occur as a sequel (Sumption 2000, Garcia-Perez and others 2003). However, to the authors’ knowledge, the pathological changes in aborted fetuses have not been reported. This short communication describes neuropathological changes in aborted ovine fetuses from ewes with tickborne fever. In a flock of 250 ewes, approximately 150 aborted in late pregnancy. Serological investigations for toxoplasmosis (Toxoreagent; Eiken Chemical), Border disease (Nettleton 2000), Chlamydophila abortus (Stamp and others 1952) and tickborne fever (Webster and Mitchell 1988) were performed on 16 of the ewes. Blood smears were prepared and stained with Leishman’s stain for the detection of intracytoplasmic parasites. A postmortem examination was carried out on nine fetuses submitted, and their brains and samples of lung, liver, heart, diaphragm and placenta were collected and placed in formol saline. After fixation, the brains were sliced coronally as described by Buxton and others (1997) and, along with blocks of the other tissues, processed to paraffin wax; sections 5 μm thick were cut and stained with haematoxylin and eosin. Serological investigation of the 16 serum samples yielded positive results for toxoplasmosis in one (low titre), Border disease in another (low titre) and tickborne fever in 11; all samples were serologically negative for C abortus. Intracellular parasites (Fig 1) with the morphology of A phagocytophilum (Urquhart and others 1987) were detected within neutrophils in the blood smears from three ewes. Histopathological examination of the brains of the nine fetuses revealed multifocal leucomalacia of the cerebral white matter cores in six, and in two of these there were additional leucomalacic lesions in the cerebellar white matter (Fig 2). The lesions were clearly defined and stained intensely and homogeneously eosinophilic with a degree of glial nuclear loss. In the adjacent surrounding neuropil there was sometimes microvacuolation, mild astrocytosis and marked congestion of capillaries, while the cytoplasm of many oligodendrocytes had a swollen appearance. Scattered swollen axons were occasionally observed in the foci (Table 1). No significant lesions were observed in the liver, lung, placenta, diaphragm or heart apart from varying degrees of congestion. The evidence suggested that the flock in question suffered a severe incident of tickborne fever (Sumption 2000).
