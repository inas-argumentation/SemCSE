The duality of TNF signaling outcomes in the brain: Potential mechanisms?
Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) is a cytokine that belongs to a superfamily of ligands that regulate important processes such as embryonic development and immunity as part of its normal function. TNF is also a critical player in various pathologies, including rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune disease, and cancer (reviewed by Wallach and Kovalenko, 2009). In the CNS, the role and regulation of TNF under normal and pathophysiological conditions is an area of intense investigation (reviewedby Tansey andWyss-Coray, 2008) and TNF signaling has been shown to have several important functions within the CNS (reviewed in McCoy and Tansey, 2008), including injury-mediated microglial and astrocyte activation, regulation of blood brain barrier permeability, febrile responses, glutamatergic transmission, and synaptic plasticity and scaling (Selmaj et al., 1990; Merrill, 1991; Sedgwick et al., 2000; Beattie et al., 2002; Leon, 2002; Pickering et al., 2005; Stellwagen and Malenka, 2006). Within the last decade, TNF has been postulated as a potential contributor to neuronal death in Parkinson's disease (PD) based on initial observations that TNF levels were increased in CSF and post-mortem brain tissue from PD patients (Boka et al., 1994; Mogi et al., 1994). With this in mind, Chertoff et al. (2011) investigated the extent to which controlling the chronic expression of TNF levels in the mouse substantia nigra (SN) promoted degeneration of DA neurons or protected them from an oxidative neurotoxin lesion. To induce chronic overexpression of TNF in the SN, adenoviral vectors, the CRE/loxP system, and hypomorphic mice were used. The authors reported that four-fold overexpressionof TNFmRNAdriven by the engrailedpromoter in TH+ neurons attenuated the nigrostriatal neurodegeneration resulting from an intrastriatal 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) lesion, whereas chronic overexpression of higher levels of TNF mRNA relative to those achieved by the engrailed promoter were associated with astrogliosis and progressive loss of TH+ neurons in the absence of 6-OHDA.While these studies do not explore themechanismunderlying the dual actions of TNF, they do demonstrate that this dual action can occur at the level of the nigrostriatal pathway and provide further evidence that understanding the outcomes of TNF activation is imperative to understanding TNF-associated pathology. Chertoff et al. correctly state that their data “is in line with the current thinking that cytokines can be deleterious or beneficial for neuronal survival depending on multiple factors,” and the concept of duality is central to the paper.
