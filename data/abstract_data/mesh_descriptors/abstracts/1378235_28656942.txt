Oncoplastic surgery with omental flap reconstruction: a study of 200 cases
The authors of the article ‘‘Oncoplastic surgery with omental flap reconstruction: a study of 200 cases’’ should be commended for their work demonstrating that conservative breast surgery (CBS) reconstruction with omental flap (OF) is a viable treatment for the partial mastectomy defects [1]. As a multi-professional team, it has been our opinion that CBS reconstruction should be performed to improve body image for patients facing breast cancer. However, the success of the whole procedure can be defined as the best possible aesthetic outcome that does not sacrifice oncological safety. In the field of evidencebased medicine, the group went on to highlight that the OF has minimal donor-site morbidity and deformity, as well as oncological safety outcome. They emphasized that there is a limit to the adaptable volume, but concluded that the present technique is an attractive option in CBS reconstruction. The authors studied this technique retrospectively, and used it in 200 patients who underwent CBS, mostly immediately. The success rate of LOF was 99.5% and the rate of complications was 12.0 and 2%, general and laparoscopy-associated, respectively. In 12.0% of patients, the volume of the flap was insufficient and when applied to total reconstruction, volume insufficiency occurred in more than 30% of patients. The authors observed that aesthetic outcomes were mostly satisfactory and approximately 80% of patients were rated as good or excellent by BCCT.core software. During a median follow-up period of 90 months, 1.0% had local recurrence. In recent years, there has been a surge in the popularity of IBR. In fact, restoring body image has been shown to have an important effect on the psychological recovery of BCS patients. Although many of the present data described are valuable, the clinical relevance to be drawn from these data deserves clarification. Our review of the present study yielded the following some comments. First, this is not a homogeneous study population, including different types of oncological surgery, techniques, quadrants, and adjuvant treatment (radio and chemotherapy). To extrapolate shortand long-term outcome as well as aesthetic results in this sample to the typical population seeking breast reconstruction would not be entirely accurate. Second, it is unclear what perioperative guidance the authors recommend for patients in terms of the decision-making process for different reconstruction techniques. In spite of the fact that there is no consensus concerning the best technique, the criteria are determined by the surgeon’s experience and the size of the defect in relation to the size of the remaining breast [2]. The main positive aspect of the procedure utilized should include reproducibility, low interference with the cancer treatment, and long-term results. Probably, all these goals are not achieved by any single technique and each procedure has advantages and limitations [2, 3].
