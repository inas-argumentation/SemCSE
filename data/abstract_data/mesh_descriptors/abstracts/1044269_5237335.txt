Small molecule: large hopes
On 10 May 2001, the Food and Drug Administration of the United States of America approved the agent imatinib mesylate (Glivec®, Novartis Pharmaceuticals Australia Pty Ltd, North Ryde, NSW, Australia), formerly known as STI571 or CGP57148, for the treatment of patients with chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) in blast crisis, accelerated phase or in chronic phase after failure of interferon-α therapy. This small molecule, a signal transduction inhibitor, is the first of this new class of therapeutic agents to be registered for clinical use and is causing enormous excitement in the haematological and oncological communities. In this orally administered agent we see the end-product of painstaking basic science, developments in biotechnology and careful clinical observation. Another reason for all the excitement is that the clinical introduction of imatinib mesylate vindicates the model of cancer that has underpinned several decades of cancer research: cancer is a genetic disease that, at least sometimes, results from a single aetiological somatic genetic lesion that, when identified, will lead to the development of potent and effective anticancer therapies.
