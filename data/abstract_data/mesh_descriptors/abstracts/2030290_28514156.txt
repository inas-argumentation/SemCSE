A potentially novel approach for the gene therapy of HIV-1 disease
The acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) is caused by human immunodeficiency virus type 1 or type 2 (HIV-I, HIV-2), resulting in permanent infection of the host and ultimate fatal outcome. Because of this dismal prospect substantial efforts are currently being directed at trying to develop a gene therapy to correct or block the pernicious effects of HIV. A number of different genetic approaches are currently being tried in vitro, some of which have resulted in clinical trials. The antiviral approaches include use of the following (reviewed in [1, 21): -Transdominant mutants of HIV-1 regulatory proteins that compete with the native HIV-1 molecules Decoys that sequester regulatory proteins and thus prevent their action -Ribozymes that recognize and cleave certain specific sequences -Antisense constructs or oligonucleotides directed against specific sequences -Genes that render HIV-l-infected cells susceptible to killing by drugs -Boosting the immune response against HIV-1 by expressing certain HIV-1 antigens. Delivery systems for these genetic approaches include: retroviral vectors, adeno-associated virus vectors, liposomes, adenovirus capsids linked to a poly-L-lysine linker, and direct injection of naked DNA. AIDS patients are currently being enrolled in clinical gene therapy trials, and results are still forthcoming [3].
