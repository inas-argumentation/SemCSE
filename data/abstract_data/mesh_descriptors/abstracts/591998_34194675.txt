Synchronous penile metastasis from a rectal carcinoma
Dear Editor: Despite its rich vascularization and extensive circulatory communication with neighboring organs, the penis is a rare site for metastasis. Most primary tumors that metastasize to the penis originate from genitourinary organs (e.g., bladder and prostate) and the rectosigmoid area. Because the first report of rectal carcinoma with metastasis to the penis was described in 1870, about 50 cases of penile metastasis from the rectum and rectosigmoid colon have been reported. A 69-year-old man presented for evaluation of hematochezia, constipation, and voiding difficulty that had persisted for about 3 months. He also detected several rapidly growing nodules under the skin of the penile shaft. There was no discharge per urethra and no history of high-risk sexual exposure. Digital rectal examination revealed a circumferential ulcerative tumor, 3 cm from the anal verge. We also observed several 5-mm-sized nontender subcutaneous nodules under the skin of the penile shaft. Enlarged inguinal lymph nodes were not palpable. Colonoscopic biopsies of the main tumor confirmed an adenocarcinoma. His serum carcinoembryonic antigen level was increased, to 62.2 ng/ml, but his prostate-specific antigen level was in the normal range (0.6 ng/ml). There were no abnormal findings on urinalysis. A computed tomography scan of the abdomen and pelvis showed distinct multiple hepatic metastases. Penile magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed multiple metastatic lesions on the corpus cavernosum and spongiosum. An incisional biopsy of the penile nodule at the corpus cavernosum was taken for tissue diagnosis, which confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma from the rectal carcinoma. He was treated with radiotherapy, consisting of 20 fractions totaling 5,000 cGy to the rectal lesion, along with concurrent chemotherapy with capecitabine (1,650 mg/m per day). This was followed by a modified FOLFOX-6 regimen (oxaliplatin 85 mg/m intravenous [IV] on day 1, leucovorin 20 mg/m IV on day 1, 5-FU 400 mg/m IV bolus on day 1, and 5-FU 1,200 mg/m IV continuous infusion on days 1 and 2). Follow-up evaluation after six cycles of FOLFOX-6 showed that both the rectal mass and the penile nodules were decreased in size and that he showed symptomatic improvement, but the size of the metastatic liver lesions had increased. During the ensuing 6 months after diagnosis, he has retained good performance status. Various mechanisms have been suggested for metastasis to the penis, including retrograde venous spread, retrograde lymphatic route, perineural space extension, and direct extension. Currently, the route involving retrograde venous spread from the pudendal venous system into the dorsal venous system of the penis is considered most likely. About 60% of patients with penile metastasis present with symptoms of multiple infiltrative nodules. Both sides of the corpus cavernosum are involved in nearly 70% of patients, whereas the corpus spongiosum and glans penis are each involved in 10% of patients and the preputium in fewer than 10%. The common clinical presentations of penile metastasis are difficulties in voiding, perineal pain, asymptomatic nodules, and priapism. Our patient had asymptomatic penile nodules on both sides of the corpus cavernosum and spongiosum, without symptoms of urinary obstruction or hematuria. Penile MRI is the most reliable noninvasive method for the preoperative evaluation of lesion extent and the presence of tunica albuginea or urethral involvement.
