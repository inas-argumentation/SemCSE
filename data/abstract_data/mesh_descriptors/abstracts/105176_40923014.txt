Pelvic inflammatory disease and myocardial infarction.
We read with interest the article by Liou and colleagues about the relation between pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and myocardial infarction (MI) [1]. The study, which sampled a Taiwanese nationwide population-based database, showed that women with PID, especially those 55 years and older,weremore likely to have developedMI than the control population during 1–3 years of follow up. PID, an infection of the upper genital tract, occurs in only 1–2% of women of reproductive age nowadays [2,3], which is a substantial decrease in incidence since the 1980s, but Liou and colleagues mentioned that the prevalence of PID was 13% of Taiwan's women during 2004–2005 [1]. According to the demographic information published at the website, among the 1 million beneficiaries sampled in the databasewhose insurance recordswere available, women agedmore than 12 years account for approximately 440 000 people [4]. Surprisingly, the study cohort included 68 453 PID patients tantamount to 16% of the population of inclusion and even higher in the subgroups of reproductive ages, which seems to be a substantial over-representation of PID prevalence. This result was most likely to have been caused by loose inclusion criteria, for two reasons. First, Liou and colleagues classified patients as having PID with the International Classification of Diseases, ninth revision, clinical modification (ICD-9-CM) codes 614, 615, and 616. ICD-9-CM codes 614 and 615 refer to inflammatory diseases of the ovary, fallopian tube, pelvic cellular tissue, peritoneum, and uterus (but not the cervix), which are characteristic of PID. However, ICD-9-CM code 616 refers to inflammatory diseases of the cervix, vagina, and vulva, which affect only the lower genital tract and are unrelated or have not yet progressed to PID. Because Taiwan is a tropical country with a high incidence and recurrence rate of vulvovaginitis, inclusion of patients with code 616 diseases is inappropriate. Second, PID usually occurs in young, sexually active women [2,3] and rarely occurs inwomen past the reproductive age, who should have been excluded from this study. Therefore, the significant increase in MI incidenceofPIDpatients agedN55 years remainsdoubtful in this study. Even though the sample size of MI in young PID patients was small, a reexamination of whether patients with PID that is defined bymore strict criteria (ICD-9-CM codes 614 and 615, and aged 15–44 years) were likely to have MI and other comorbid medical disorders would be interesting. It is well known that there is an increase in cardiovascular disease and cardiovascular risk factors when estrogen is deprived [5]. Several mechanisms of estrogen action could be important for cardiovascular prevention: estrogen can protect against lipid peroxidation [6], increases vasodilatation and inhibits the response of blood vessels to injury and the development of atherosclerosis [7]. A large observational study reported that women with hormone replacement therapy (HRT), including estrogen only or combined estrogen and progestin, had a lower overall risk of MI compared with never users [8]. Some randomized controlled trials, however, demonstrated that combinedHRTdoesnot confer cardiac protection and may increase the risk of coronary heart disease among healthy postmenopausal women [5,9]. The discrepancy between observational and clinical studies indicates that estrogen has complex biologic effects and may influence the risk of cardiovascular events through different pathways. Therefore, use of HRT should be treated as a confounding factor which should have been identified and adjusted in this study. Nevertheless, this study has given us some novel insights into the prognosis of PID. Investigators should begin to design and undertake prospective clinical trials to better elucidate the relation between PID and MI.
