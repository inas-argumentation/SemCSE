Going round in circles to avoid proteolysis.
Recently, several circular proteins known collectively as cyclotides have been discovered in plants. As yet, their role is undefined but recent reports of anti-microbial activity suggest their involvement in plant defence. The interest in this family of proteins is fuelled largely by the broad range of biological activities associated with its members in mammalian systems; for example, uterotonic, anti-HIV and haemolytic activities – a repertoire that makes these proteins potential targets for the design of therapeutic agents. The circular nature of these small, disulphide-rich proteins is conferred by a head-to-tail cyclic peptide backbone. The peptides are ∼30-amino-acids long, and the backbone contains six highly conserved Cys residues. These form three disulphide bonds, giving the protein a stable structure that has been determined for three members of the family. In all cases, these bonds are arranged in what is known as a ‘cysteine knot’, a motif that comprises an embedded ring formed by two disulphide bonds, the connecting backbone segments of which are penetrated by the third disulphide bond. This motif has been found in many small disulphide-rich proteins and appears to confer very high stability and resistance to proteolytic cleavage.The recent discovery of two new macrocyclic peptides from Momordica cochinchinensis (from the Cucurbitacaea family) suggests that these cyclic peptides are more prevalent than originally thought. The two peptides, MCoTI-I and MCoTI-II, are trypsin inhibitors. They are both 34-amino-acids long, and consist of a cyclic peptide backbone and three disulphide bonds. These findings prompted Felizmenio-Quimio et al.[1xSee all References[1] to use NMR spectroscopy to deduce the three-dimensional structure of MCoTI-II, for comparison with the known plant cyclotides. In this paper, the authors report the presence of a cysteine knot framework that is associated with a β hairpin. In addition, a third strand of β sheet is loosely connected with this hairpin and the molecule contains several turns. Given the similarity of this three-dimensional structure with those of known plant cyclotides, and the presence of a cyclic backbone, the group conclude that the plant cyclotide family can be expanded to include these two cyclic trypsin inhibitors.The authors also point out that the overall fold of MCoTI-II is very similar to that of the squash trypsin inhibitors EETI-II and CMTI-II, except that the two latter proteins are not cyclic. Given that MCoTI-II and the squash inhibitors are functionally analogous, the group discuss the possible evolutionary advantages of cyclization. After discussing several possibilities, the authors suggest, by a process of elimination, that proteolytic protection is the most likely reason for these trypsin inhibitors to adopt a cyclic form. For example, because MCoTI-I is a seed component, it will have to survive (i.e. avoid breakdown) for long periods of time before germination.In demonstrating the expansion of the plant cyclotide family to include trypsin inhibitors from the Cucurbitaceae family, this group have underlined the importance and functional variability of these proteins. The significance of this expanding collection of natural circular proteins will not be lost on the pharmaceutical industry for which cyclization is an important method of producing small, stable peptides.
