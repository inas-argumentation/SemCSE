Do subspecialized neurosurgeons experience higher complication rates for nonsubspecialty emergent surgery?
Dear Editor, There is a growing trend towards subspecialization in neurosurgery, especially at large acedemic centres. Subspecialization is largely necessary due to the increasing complexity of neurosurgical care including the sheer volume of knowledge and competency in surgical management [2]. Most hospitals continue to delegate a single neurosurgeon on call (on any given night) who is required to medically and/or surgically manage all neurosurgical emergencies. It is likely that a subspecialized neurosurgeon with a narrow scope of elective practice will frequently experience one of the many neurosurgical emergencies. Also the, fundamental assumption that surgeons' experience is a significant contributor to operative morbidity and mortality is an underlying principle of residency and fellowship training. Thus, lack of exposure in nonspecialized neurosurgical procedures may result in loss of skill, expertise, and/or confidence. We performed an extensive systematic review of the literature to determine if subspecialized neurosurgeons (with a narrow scope of elective practice) experience higher complication rates for nonsubspecialized neurosurgical procedures. We searched electronic databases of Medline, Embase, Cochrane, and Scopus for English articles regarding complication rates of specialized neurosurgeons operating on nonsubspecialty neurosurgery only excluding case reports. Only 2 articles met inclusion criteria. Both included articles were editorials. Rossi [10] was the first in the literature to suggest that the dilution of surgical experience can lead to a decrease in skill. Schackert and Reulen [11] emphasize the need for subspecialist neurosurgeons to keep their skills honed particularly in the area of emergency treatment and regard the lack of as a disadvantage or danger of subspecialization. Although expert opinion suggests that complication rates can be higher as a result of increasing subspecialization, the weak level of evidence and the paucity in the literature warrants future studies on this topic. There are several studies comparing the complication rates of general neurosurgeons to subspecialized neurosurgeons performing specialized neurosurgery [1,2,4-7,9,12]. Some of these studies have favored the lower complication rates of subspecialized neurosurgeons, whereas others have shown no difference. Although these studies have revealed interesting information, they represent the converse of our study question. Conclusions drawn from these studies do not apply to our topic for 2 main reasons: (1) general neurosurgeons are frequently exposed to all forms of neurosurgical pathology and (2) these cases are mostly done in an elective setting which can contribute to decreased operative morbidity and mortality. Therefore, they would not be comparable to situations of high acuity like those experienced on call. Although prospective studies would provide the most accurate answer to our research question, its labor and resource intensive nature make it impractical to conduct. The major impediment is the availability of at least 2 neurosurgeons (specialized and general) to be on call on any given night. An alternative research design is through retrospective chart reviews comparing complication rates preferably cross institutionally. A single institution study may be limited as a particular center may only have neurosurgeons that are either “specialized” or “general” but not both. A third option is through qualitative methodology assessing surgeons' attitudes regarding their operative experiences on call nights. If in fact the quality of care suffers as a result of subspecialized neurosurgical practice, it might be prudent to only suggest “general neurosurgeons” take call. Another solution would be to request multiple specialized neurosurgeons to take call on a given night and each only respond to emergencies in their specific area of expertise to the discretion of the primary neurosurgeon on call. This solution could favor increased regionalization of neurosurgery as opposed to group practice [3]. However, this might be challenging given the current workforce shortage of neurosurgeons as well as the decade-long process of training new personnel [5,8]. A third solution might be to restrict subspecialized neurosurgeons from exclusively dedicating their elective practice to their area of subspecialty expertise. This allows them to have increased exposure to general neurosurgery.
