Use of serum progesterone concentration for timing breast MRI examinations and avoiding false-negative findings.
AJR 2010; 195:W85 0361–803X/10/1951–W85 © American Roentgen Ray Society Use of Serum Progesterone Concentration for Timing Breast MRI Examinations and Avoiding FalseNegative Findings A 33-year-old woman with an extensive group of pleomorphic calcifications detected in her left breast at mammography underwent stereotactic-guided 9-gauge vacuum-assisted biopsy. The histologic study revealed high-grade invasive ductal carcinoma associated with high-grade ductal carcinoma in situ. We performed MR mammography to help determine the size and focality of the tumor. Because of the urgency of the case (a young woman with extensive carcinoma proposed for primary chemotherapy), the study was performed without regard to the timing of her hormonal cycle. Subtracted MR images showed extensive contrast enhancement of the upper outer quadrant of the left breast. Moreover, other ill-defined rounded contrast-enhancing areas in the upper and lower outer quadrants of the contralateral breast were seen (Figs. 1A and 1B). We suspected a bilateral lesion and performed selective second-look sonography of the right breast. The lower outer quadrant contrastenhancement correlated with a well-defined solid oval lesion measuring 1 cm in diameter. Histologic study after sonographically guided 14-gauge biopsy showed the lesion was benign. Other contrast-enhancing lesions had no correlation at sonography. To rule out a false-positive finding because of hormonal fluctuations, we questioned the patient about her menstrual cycles and discovered they were irregular. We read with interest the experience of Ellis [1] in timing breast MRI examinations in premenopausal women without cyclical menses. In particular, we noted the utility of serum progesterone concentrations in determining the follicular phase, which is the point in the menstrual cycle when normal glandular tissue is least affected by disproportionate enhancement. As a result, we rescheduled the patient for 5 days after her menstruation to determine her serum progesterone concentration and perform MRI-guided breast biopsy on the same day. The serum progesterone concentration was 0.53 ng/mL, corresponding with the follicular phase, and we repeated MR mammography with the aim of biopsying the lesion. The left breast findings did not vary from the previous study; however, in the right breast, only the previously biopsied lesion (with an artifact from a metallic marker deployed after the procedure) enhanced Figs. 1C and 1D), confirming the false-positive findings because of hormonal fluctuations and thus avoiding MRI-guided biopsy. We regularly perform MR mammography studies at our center, usually without regard to the timing of patients’ menstrual cycles, because of the low rate of false-positive findings and the difficulties involved in coordinating patients’ agendas. However, we appreciate the article by Ellis [1], and we believe that serum progesterone concentrations can be helpful in nonurgent cases of young women with dense breasts, such as in screening high-risk women, in whom progesterone determination may help avoid misdiagnosis, false-positive findings, and unnecessary biopsies, as in the current case.
