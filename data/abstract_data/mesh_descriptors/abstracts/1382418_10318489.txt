The Power Of Drug Co-Administration: Smaller Doses Better Outcomes
It is widely accepted that cardiovascular disease has been one of the main causes of death in the developed world. Such disease is often associated with diabetes, hypertension and hypercholesterolemia, all features which can define the so-called metabolic syndrome. Traditionally, each condition is treated with its own specific pharmacology. However, there is now increasing interest in the coadministration of drugs which are often reformulated into fixed-dose combinations [1]. The benefits are multiple; for example, drugs can be used in lower doses which may reduce both the side effects and the economic costs. Furthermore, treatments can be personalized to individual circumstances with patients benefiting from the pleiotropic properties that some drugs may possess. Birnbaum’s group has previously drawn attention to the benefits resulting from the co-administration of low-dose atorvastatin combined with other agents that target specific pathological conditions associated with cardiovascular disease [2, 3]. In these earlier papers they demonstrated that co-administration of low dose atorvastatin with either pioglitazone [2] or sildenafil [3] is associated with a significant reduction in infarct size in an animal model of myocardial ischemia– reperfusion injury. In a new paper published in a recent issue of Cardiovascular Drugs and Therapy, by the same group, [4] Manickavasagam S. et al. highlighted more specifically this important paradigm. The authors used a combination of cilostazol and low dose atorvastatin to treat rats for 3 days. They then investigated the ability of these agents to protect against myocardial infarction following 30 min ischemia and 4 h reperfusion. Interestingly cilostazol alone induced a small but significant reduction in infarct size, an increase in the activation of PKA, Akt and eNOS and a down regulation of PTEN. Atorvastatin alone had a similar effect, but even less marked, on infarct size, PKA and Akt. However these positive outcomes are much more increased when the agents are co-administered. These results demonstrated how two different agents may act synergistically, via different pathways, to activate the same prosurvival targets and to induce a better recovery of the ischemic-reperfused myocardium. Numerous groups have previously shown that the lipid lowering HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (statins), in particular atorvastatin, are able to induce cardioprotection against ischemia–reperfusion injury. This protection is present when atorvastatin was administered either for short periods of time (up to 3 days) prior to the ischemic event [5] or just at the onset of reperfusion [6, 7]. This protection has been shown to involve the up-regulation of the so-called RISK pathway [8] in which eNOS phosphorylation and hence activation, is a main target [9]. However, in order to obtain this beneficial effect, high doses of statins are necessary—a situation which may not always be well tolerated in man [10]. Additionally, in animal models, a chronic (2 weeks) treatment with atorvastatin did not demonstrate protection against myocardial infarction [5].
