A response to "developing standards in behavioral surveillance for HIV/STD prevention".
In their article “Developing Standards in Behavioral Surveillance for HIV/STD Prevention,” Rietmeijer, Lansky, Anderson, & Fichtner (2001) make an important foray into the challenging area of developing standardized measures for behavioral surveillance to meet the needs of public health officials and scientific investigators involved in HIV/STD prevention. The authors argued for examining “the last sexual event” as an indicator for a proportion of the population who have some minimum risk factor for HIV or other sexually transmitted infections. They stated that this approach has fewer problems with recall than those of retrospective methods that ask respondents to aggregate behaviors across multiple sexual encounters over time. The conceptual underpinnings of the last-event assessments are based by the authors on what has been referred to as “critical incident” methodology (Flanagan, 1954). In this response, we raise empirical, theoretical, and methodological concerns with last event methods while arguing for a research agenda that examines these issues in a more definitive manner. We have little disagreement with the observation that last-event assessments may be less cognitively challenging than asking respondents to aggregate their behavior for some time period (although measures covering periods of 3 to 4 months are often reliable). However, the authors assume that the most recent event is in fact recent in time. Without knowing how long ago the recent event was, it would be difficult to know how uniform the improvements in event recall actually are with the last event method. Recent behaviors may, for instance, be anywhere from 24 hours to several months distant depending on the population, time of year, ethnicity, gender, and age of the respondent. Teenagers, for instance, may have highly variable rates of sexual contact over time, as do some adults (Michael & Joyner, 2001).
