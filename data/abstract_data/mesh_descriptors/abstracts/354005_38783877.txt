In patients at high CV risk, a Mediterranean diet plus olive oil reduced diabetes more than advising a low-fat diet
Question In patients at high risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD), what is the efficacy of 2 Mediterranean diets compared with advice to follow a low-fat diet for primary prevention of diabetes? Methods Design Subgroup analysis of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) (Prevencin con Dieta Mediterrnea [PREDIMED] trial). Current Controlled Trials ISRCTN 35739639. Allocation {Concealed}*. Blinding Blinded ({physicians}*, laboratory technicians, outcome assessors, and outcome adjudication committee). Follow-up period Median 4 years. Setting 11 primary care centers in Spain. Patients A subgroup of 3833 community-dwelling men 55 to 80 years of age and women 60 to 80 years of age (mean age 67 y, 62% women) without CVD or diabetes, who had 3 major cardiovascular risk factors: smoking, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, overweight or obesity, and family history of premature CVD. Intervention Mediterranean diet (Med diet) plus extra-virgin olive oil (EVOO), 50 mL/d (EVOO provided at no cost) (n =1261); Med diet plus mixed walnuts, almonds, and hazelnuts, 30 g/d (nuts provided at no cost) (n =1311); or advice to follow a low-fat diet (n =1261). All participants received dietary advice at baseline. Med-diet groups received quarterly individual and group training; the low-fat diet group received annual information leaflets for 3 years and then quarterly individual and group training. Outcomes New-onset diabetes (fasting plasma glucose 7.0 mmol/L [126.1 mg/dL] or 2-hour plasma glucose 11.1 mmol/L [200.0 mg/dL] after 75-g oral glucose load). Patient follow-up 92%. Conclusion In patients at high risk for cardiovascular disease, a Mediterranean diet plus extra-virgin olive oil reduced new-onset diabetes compared with advice to follow a low-fat diet. Hazard ratios were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, fasting glucose, dyslipidemia, hypertension, total energy intake (kcal/d), adherence to Med diet, physical activity, education, and alcohol intake. Commentary Although such intensive interventions as the Diabetes Prevention Program (1) can effectively reduce risk for diabetes, these programs typically emphasize weight loss and reduced caloric intake, which are difficult to maintain. The Med diet is promising because it offers a flavorful alternative, with a wide range of choices and no restrictions on calories. Consistent with findings of a previous systematic review (2), the secondary analysis of the PREDIMED trial by Salas-Salvad and colleagues found that, in patients at risk for heart disease, the Med diet plus EVOO reduced risk for diabetes by 40% compared with advice to follow a low-fat diet. The benefits of the Med diet plus EVOO cannot be explained by weight loss alone because the groups did not differ for weight loss at follow-up. Although the Med diet plus EVOO was effective, its clinical application is less clear. Should clinicians prioritize educating patients about this diet, focus on motivational counseling, encourage physical activity, or emphasize weight loss for patients at risk for diabetes? Other practical considerations for clinicians include differing definitions of the diet itself; potential for conflation with westernized versions of Mediterranean cuisine; moderate cost of the diet (especially fish, EVOO, and produce); and preparation, cooking time, and skills associated with the diet. Further, most people probably will not have the extensive support provided in this study (e.g., free EVOO and quarterly dietitian visits); the lack of such support might reduce any benefits. Despite the pragmatic concerns, clinicians should consider recommending the Mediterranean diet to patients ready to make lifestyle changes, given the substantial evidence of its benefits.
