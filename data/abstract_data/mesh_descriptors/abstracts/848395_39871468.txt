PET/CT imaging of recurrent prostate cancer
Prostate cancer and a variety of other malignant entities exhibit increased choline metabolism, which is the rationale behind using 11Cand 18F-labeled choline for positron emission tomography (PET) and PET/computed tomography (CT) imaging. The value of PET using radiolabeled choline for detecting the primary prostate cancer is, at present, discussed controversially. While some workgroups report encouraging results, others see only limited usefulness [1–3]. In particular, the problems in differentiating prostate cancer lesions from benign prostatic hyperplasia and prostatitis hold back from a general recommendation for using PET in this setting. Moreover, in the light of recent reports on the diagnostic efficacy of endorectal coil magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) supplemented by magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), PET faces a strong competing modality. While also providing functional information on choline metabolism, this procedure offers excellent soft tissue contrast. Particularly, in evaluating extra capsular tumor growth and seminal vesicle invasion—information highly important for the further therapeutic strategy—MRI/MRS seems to be the imaging modality of choice [4]. Another future option when performing MRI may be the utilization of diffusion-weighted imaging, which should become increasingly interesting with the use of three Tesla scanners [5, 6]. However, by employing new examination protocols, for example, dynamic imaging, the diagnostic efficacy of PET and PET/CT in the detection of the primary prostate cancer has also the potential to improve in the future [7]. An intrinsic advantage of PET as a whole-body procedure in comparison to MRI/MRS is the ability to provide metabolic information not restricted to the prostate gland but also on local or distant metastases. Particularly, combined PET/CT utilizing radiolabeled choline showed promising first results in the detection of metastatic spread [8, 9]. The relevance of this advantage regarding primary staging may, however, be debatable because widely used prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening has led to significant downstaging at first diagnosis. The prevalence of lymph node metastasis at the time of diagnosis decreased in a study investigating prostate cancer patients from 23% in 1984 to 2% in 1995 [10]. The percentage of biochemical relapse after radical prostatectomy (RPx) or radiation therapy may exceed 50%, particularly, in patients with disease at stage T3 and positive resection margins [11, 12]. Therefore, it may be speculated that one of the major future indications for PET imaging in prostate cancer patients may lie in the localization of recurrent disease after definitive therapy. Biochemical relapse is, in general, assumed if PSA increases to a value ≥0.2 ng/ml after reaching the nadir. A rising PSA may be attributed to local recurrence in the prostatic bed or metastatic spread. Localizing recurrent disease is of crucial importance for the further therapeutic strategy. If local recurrence after RPx is suspected with the absence of distant metastases, salvage radiotherapy with or without antihormonal treatment is generally targeted at the prostatic bed [13]. Transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy provides a detection rate of 25–54% and is particularly low in cases with PSA levels <1.0 ng/ml [14, 15].
