Liver transplantation in human immunodeficiency virus/hepatitis C virus–coinfected patients: Response needed!
In the past 2 decades, there has been tremendous progress in the health care of patients infected with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and liver transplant services have tried to contribute positively to this momentum. Mortality due to liver disease is significantly overrepresented in the HIV-infected population, and the case for access to liver transplantation has been compelling for quite some time. Also, the clinical experience with liver transplantation in HIVinfected patients extends over almost 2 decades, but despite this, the practice has not gained widespread support. This is despite the fact that results have firmly established that HIV per se is absolutely not a contraindication to liver transplantation. Key outcomes after transplantation, including patterns of rejection and infection and graft and patient survival, match those seen in non–HIV-infected patients in the absence of a coexisting hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. Furthermore, no evidence has emerged that posttransplant immunosuppressive therapy negatively affects the prognosis of HIV disease. Concerns about outcomes after liver transplantation for patients coinfected with HIV and HCV surfaced early because some programs (including my own) encountered evidence of more aggressive HCV recurrence or inferior graft survival rates. An influential single-center study of 35 HIV/HCV-infected patients receiving liver transplants between 1999 and 2005 showed inferior survival rates (51% at 5 years) and more rapid progression to fibrosis. These and other studies were retrospective in nature and were subject to publication bias, so the design and funding of a National Institutes of Health–sponsored study (Solid Organ Transplantation in HIV: Multi-Site Study) were highly welcome. In this issue of Liver Transplantation, Terrault et al. report the outcomes of 89 HIV/HCVcoinfected recipients of liver transplants who were recruited to this prospective, multicenter cohort study between 2003 and 2010. In the study, the care protocols were not rigidly protocolized, and this gives a real-world sense to the experience. The patients were also carefully selected and managed with respect to HIV disease; the median CD4þ count was 283 cell/mL, 88% were negative for HIV RNA, and anti-HIV therapy was restored for 80% within 1 week of transplantation. Two control groups were constructed: one logical and one possibly inventive. The former group included 235 HCV-monoinfected liver transplant recipients from the participating centers, and the latter group was based on an analysis of data from the US Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients for all recipients who were older than 64 years. The 3-year patient and graft survival rates were 60% and 53%, respectively, and HIV was the sole baseline factor significantly associated with these inferior outcomes. The study also found an increased risk of acute cellular rejection in the HIV/ HCV-coinfected patients, but it did not find evidence of more severe HCV-related disease.
