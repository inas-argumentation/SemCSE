Early interventions for schizophrenia aim to improve treatment outcomes.
New Orleans—When such early symptoms of schizophrenia as disorganized thoughts, social withdrawal, and paranoia begin to emerge, it can be a frightening experience for affected individuals and family members. But an emerging body of evidence also suggests that this early stage of illness might provide an opportunity for interventions, even very simple ones, that may help to prevent the development of fullblown schizophrenia. Several preliminary clinical trials have probed whether early interventions, including medication, behavioral therapies, or -3 fatty acid supplementation, may reduce the likelihood that an at-risk individual will develop schizophrenia, with some reporting positive results. The studies complement findings suggesting that delayed treatment leads to poorer long-term outcomes for patients with schizophrenia. During a symposium at the American Psychiatric Association’s (APA’s) annual meeting in May, scientists from institutions around the world discussed their progress in this area, including the launch of large multisite trials that aim to further refine tools to identify those at risk and to study which interventions are most effective and when they should be initiated. “We’re trying to close the barn door before the horses get out,” said Jeffrey Lieberman, MD, chairman of the department of psychiatry at the Columbia University College of Physicians and Surgeons in New York, who was chair of the APA symposium. Lieberman explained that schizophrenia follows a predictable course, usually emerging in late adolescence or early adulthood with recurrent bouts of illness, and ultimately progressing into a disabling condition. Unfortunately, patients often do not receive treatment until late in the course of their illness. Rene S. Kahn, MD, PhD, of the Rudolf Magnus Institute of Neuroscience in Utrecht, the Netherlands, explained at the symposium that imaging studies have revealed that patients with schizophrenia experience a progressive loss of brain volume during the course of their illness, with a 1% reduction in brain volume in the first year alone (Cahn W. Br J Psychiatry. 2006;189:381-382). The losses also correspond with clinical outcomes, explained Kahn, with those experiencing the greatest losses over time having the poorest outcomes. Psychotic episodes themselves appear to contribute to the damage, as do factors such as cannabis use, he noted. “The longer we wait [to treat], the bigger the damage done,” said Kahn. He acknowledged that there is some controversy surrounding whether antipsychotic drugs protect against brain loss or contribute to it. He noted data suggesting that patients who had more exposure to certain antipsychotics had less brain loss, but that studies in monkeys had suggested brain loss with antipsychotic exposure. One large international trial found that patients treated with olanzapine for first episode psychosis had reduced brain loss, but treatment of such patients with haloperidol was associated with substantial brain loss (Lieberman JA et al. Arch Gen Psychiatry. 2005;62[4]:361-370). Another study in macaques found that exposure to haloperidol or olanzapine for more than 1 year reduced brain volumes by 8% to 11% (Dorph-Petersen et al. Neuropsychopharmacology. 2005; 30[9]:1649-1661). Kahn hypothesized that schizophrenia is a neurodevelopmental disorder in which brain development begins to go awry around age 12 or 13 years. He suggested that earlier intervention may help to stop the progression and improve outcomes.
