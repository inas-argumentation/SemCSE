Queen Elizabeth Hospital for Children, (“Queens”) where this story begins, was situated in the East End of London, in an area of overcrowding and social deprivation, and a place for the first settlement of immigrants into the country. The hospital itself was founded in response to a cholera epidemic in 1867, and had continued to receive children with diarrhoeal diseases until 1998 when it was closed as part of the re-organization of paediatric services in London. It cared for many children with acute diarrhea and had a dedicated infectious diseases ward (Observation Ward) for admitting patients into individual cubicles. In the late 1950’s and early 1960’s gastroenteritis was divided into bacterial and nonbacterial episodes, where it was assumed that a viral cause was responsible for the non-bacterial episodes but evidence was not forthcoming. In the early 1970’s John Walker-Smith took up the post of Senior Lecturer in Child Health in the Academic Department of Child Health of the joint St Bartholomew’s (Barts) and London Medical Colleges based at Queens, and Consultant Paediatrician at Barts. He had an interest in acute and chronic diarrheal diseases, a morphological background, and the ability to perform small intestinal mucosal biopsies in children. It was natural for him to pursue the causes of acute gastroenteritis, and to perform small bowel biopsies in children with chronic diarrhoea in order to facilitate diagnosis and treatment. An initial plan to study diarrhoeal stools for viral particles by electron microscopy was held up by the insistence of virologists that pre and post convalescent sera should be collected. However, the approach was vindicated with the ultrastructural identification of rotavirus in duodenal biopsies (1) and stools (2). Thus, the electron microscope was seen as an important tool in the study of the gut and I came to Queens in 1975, as an electron microscopist with a zoological background, and joined John in the delights of pediatric gastroenterology. A two-pronged approach was adopted with the setting up of rapid negative staining EM of acute diarrheal stools for viral particles (3) and the application of transmission EM to mucosal biopsies in chronic diarrhea. Examination of the causes of acute gastroenteritis demonstrated a mix of etiologies including viral, bacterial and parasitic organisms. Rotavirus (4) was the major viral cause but adenovirus, astrovirus (5), Norwalk-like (6) and small round viruses (7) were also frequently detected. Serogroupable enteropathogenic Escherichia coli were the predominant bacterial isolates, but Salmonella (8), Shigella, Campylobacter, and Aeromonas caviae (9) were also prevalent. Giardia lamblia and Cryptosporidium (10) were the predominant parasites. Co-incident with this prevalent background of infectious diarrhoea came the observation that traveler’s diarrhea was an important part of the mix of local diarrheal disease. Children born in the UK were being taken back to their ancestral homeland (including Bangladesh, India, Turkey) to visit family members. The young age and the exposure to infectious agents that they had not experienced in the UK put them at risk of acquiring “traveler’s diarrhea”. Indeed, such was the severity of some cases being admitted to Queens on their return to the UK that “Traveler’s diarrhea with a vengeance” was the title used to publish these observations in 1979 (11). In this paper we described one child (case 5) who had acquired chronic diarrhea in India, and was admitted to Queens in a parlous state. A proximal small intestinal mucosal biopsy was performed and we observed severe crypt hyperplastic villous atrophy and the presence of bacteria adhering to the surface epithelium. E coli O128 was isolated from the stool, and indirect immunofluorescence confirmed that this was the serotype adhering to the mucosa (Fig. 1A). The notable finding was that transmission electron microscopy of the mucosa demonstrated that the microvilli were absent at the sites of bacterial adhesion and some bacilli appeared to be sitting on pedestals formed from the apical membrane and the underlying cytoskeleton (fig 1b) the attaching-effacing lesion. Had we but realized it, here was the evidence of how enteropathogenic Escherichia coli interact with human intestine. In the late 1970’s it was known that enteropathogenic Escherichia coli did not produce toxins and were not invasive, but if given to adult volunteers they caused diarrhea (12). In 1980 Ulshen & Rollo (13) reported a case report of a child with chronic diarrhea and failure to thrive, who had enteropathogenic Escherichia coli in the stool. They did two important things (in addition to realizing the implication of their observation) performed a proximal small intestinal mucosal biopsy, and applied transmission electron microscopy to the sample.
