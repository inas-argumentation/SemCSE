An Inverse Relation Between cagA‐Positive Strains of Helicobacter pylori Infection and Risk of Esophageal and Gastric Cardia Adenocarcinoma
Helicobacter pylori infection is an established risk factor for distal gastric adenocarcinoma [1], with cag A-positive strains conferring a greater risk than that of cag A-negative strains [2]. The relationship between H. pylori and proximal gastric or esophageal adenocarcinoma is less clear, although previous studies found no association between H. pylori infection and tumors of the gastric cardia [3]. This article by Chow et al. investigates the relationship between H. pylori infection and cancers of the upper gastrointestinal tract using data from a large epidemiological study of gastric and esophageal adenocarcinoma. Patients from a defined geographical area with gastric or esophageal adenocarcinoma, identified by rapid reporting systems, were asked to attend for interview. For those patients who died before the interview, demographical information and medical histories were obtained from surviving relatives. The patients in the population-based control group, recruited by a combination of randomdigit telephone dialing and from health care financing administration files, were frequency-matched by age, gender, and race to index cases. H. pylori status was determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) serology on samples obtained at the time of interview from all surviving patients and controls; in those found to be H. pylori –positive, serorecognition of CagA was assessed using a recombinant CagA ELISA. The patients were divided into two groups: esophagogastric cardiac carcinomas and distal gastric carcinomas; their association with H. pylori was measured and was expressed as relative risk. The authors found that H. pylori infection was not significantly associated with an increased risk of distal gastric cancer (including the subgroup seropositive for CagA). Proximal gastric and esophageal carcinomas were negatively associated in CagA-seropositive cases (odds ratio, 0.4; 95% confidence interval, 0.2–0.8). These results imply that infection with a cag A-positive strain of H. pylori may protect against the development of adenocarcinomas of the proximal stomach and esophagus. Interestingly, there was no negative association with H. pylori status per se.
