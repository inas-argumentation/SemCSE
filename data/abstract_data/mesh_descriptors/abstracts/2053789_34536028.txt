Screening Breast MRI in Women with a Personal History of Breast Cancer
To the Editor: The American Cancer Society guidelines recommend the use of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as adjunct to mammography to screen women at increased risk for breast cancer. However, a personal history of breast cancer is not included among the accepted indications and the role of MRI in screening these patients has yet to be clearly established (1). Mammography has limitations in women who have undergone prior curative-intent treatment. Post-treatment changes can mimic or obscure new malignancies on mammography (2,3). Conversely, MRI has been reported to have high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing post-treatment changes from recurrent carcinoma (4,5). In our experience, personal history of breast cancer is a frequent indication for screening breast MRI in clinical practice, however, there has been little published data to support MRI screening in these patients. A retrospective review of MRI reports from September 2007 to December 2010 from our institution identified 308 screening breast MRI examinations in 181 women with a personal history of breast cancer and no other risk factors. Of the 181 patients, 117 had been treated with breast conservation therapy and 64 were post-mastectomy. Sixteen underwent biopsy (8.8% [95% CI: 5.4–14%]). Four malignancies were detected in 4/308 exams (1.3% [95% CI: 0.35–3.3%]) and 4/181 patients (2.2% [95% CI: 0.61–5.6%]), yielding a positive predictive value (PPV) of 27% (95% CI: 7.8–55%). Of the four cancers detected, there was one ductal carcinoma in situ detected in the contralateral breast 7 years after the initial diagnosis. Two were invasive ductal carcinomas: T3N3 in the contralateral breast 5 years after the initial diagnosis and T1aN0 in the ipsilateral breast 10 years after the initial diagnosis. One was a metastatic internal mammary lymph node detected 2 years after the initial diagnosis of an invasive ductal carcinoma. Of the four women with MRIdetected cancer, only one had a preoperative MRI prior to treatment of the original cancer; this was the patient diagnosed with metastatic cancer. The data on screening MRI for women whose only risk factor is a personal history are sparse. A 2010 retrospective study of 144 women with a personal history, but no family history, of breast cancer, reported a biopsy PPV of 39% and cancer detection in 12% of women during 7–9 years of follow-up. The study concluded that screening MRI may particularly benefit those without a preoperative MRI and who have not taken hormonal therapy (3). A 2014 retrospective study comparing screening MRI in 208 women with a personal history with 345 women with a strong family history of breast cancer demonstrated a higher absolute risk factor for detection of cancer in the cohort with a personal history (2.8% versus 2.0%, respectively) (6). Historically, cancer-detection rates in mammography have been reported per exam. Screening mammography in women with a personal history of earlystage breast cancer has a reported cancer detection rate of 0.68% (7). The cancer yield per exam reported in the literature for screening MRI for patients at high-risk ranges from 0.53% to 3.8% (3,8–14), which is similar to the cancer yield per exam of 1.3% for the 181 women in our review. Women with a personal history as an independent risk factor may have similar outcomes from MRI screening as other high-risk women in whom screening MRI is currently recommended. Although MRI is highly sensitive for breast cancer detection, it is associated with lower specificity than mammography. Our BI-RADS 4/5 PPV of 27% compares favorably to other screening MRI studies, which have reported PPV ranging from 19.2% to 39% (3,8,14).
