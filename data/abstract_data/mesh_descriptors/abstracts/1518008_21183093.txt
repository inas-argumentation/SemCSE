When comparing DIS with psychiatrist diagnosis, the DIS demonstrated only 29% sensitivity, missing most of the psychiatrist-determined positive cases. Moreover, false positives were 3.4 times more likely for men and 4.4 times more likely for individuals with mild MDD. Contrasting the limited sensitivity of structured interviews, questionnaire-based case identification is rightly criticized for limited specificity. The specificity of these instruments has been noted to be approximately 80%, which suggests that by this method, 2 of every 10 cases without depression will be misidentified. It is unfair, however, to assume that individuals with false-positive results experience no distress or impairment, or that they scored above the threshold randomly. Although there should be concern about shortcomings of questionnaires, false positives due to minor depression and other psychiatric disorders, while undermining specificity, are relevant insofar as psychiatric diagnosis often involves multiple overlapping and inextricably interrelated diagnostic entities. Moreover, false positives due to a reference standard with low sensitivity (as Eaton et al demonstrated with the DIS) should mitigate criticism of questionnaires’ supposed inaccuracy. Finally, questionnaires may offer increased sensitivity for domains such as mental health that are often difficult to discuss in person. This may be especially relevant for men, who are more reluctant to engage with health professionals over issues of depression and other mental health concerns. If a man is comfortable completing a questionnaire but reluctant to answer an interviewer, this disagreement should hardly be regarded as questionnaire instrument error. Thombs et al argue that scores above cutoff thresholds on questionnaires are not equivalent to clinical diagnosis, a point that is uncontested. However, it is also clear that diagnoses reached by structured interview are also not equivalent to clinical diagnosis. Each method offers its own benefits and limitations, neither is necessarily more valid, and both ultimately depend on respondents’ open and accurate self-report.
