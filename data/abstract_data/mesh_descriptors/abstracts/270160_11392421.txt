KANAMYCIN IN UROLOGIC PRACTICE: PRELIMINARY OBSERVATIONS
Kanamycin* has been administered to a series of 38 patients (33 men and 5 women), ages 18 to 80 years, predominantly past middle age. The duration of the infections were from months to several years, and they had failed of cure with prior antimicrobial agents. Pretherapy cultures and sensitivity tests on urine specimens and prostatic secretions were done on all patients. Posttherapy cultures were made on all but five patients two weeks or longer following discontinuance of kanamycin. Many of these infections followed corrective surgery for lesions of the urinary tract, predominantly transurethral resection for benign hyperplasia of the prostate. There were a few patients with inoperable carcinoma of the prostate, with a minimum of residual urine. Later, one patient passed a nonopaque calculus of the kidney and another patient had prostatic calculi. All but three patients had sufficient pus in the urine before institution of therapy to produce grossly a haziness or cloudiness. One method of evaluating the bacteriostatic and/or bactericidal effects is reduction of the pyuria and sterile cultures during the administration of a drug. Urine usually became clear within forty-eight hours after the institution of kanamycin, with the exception of Enterococci. Cultures were sterile in all patients during administration of kanamycin, when cure was eventually attained. The Pseudomonas infection did not have any substantial reduction of pyuria. In many instances Pseudomonas was isolated during the administration of kanamycin. The Enterococci that frequently emerged during therapy were often not isolated in the original cultures when the predominant pathogen was of a bacillary type. The emergence of the Enterococci a t this time was associated with lack of pyuria, and the patient remained asymptomatic. In many instances the Enterococci persisted for many months, resembling a carrier state. While the tube dilution method of testing sensitivity may be more reliable than the disk method, it certainly is not practicable for the average hospital or clinical laboratory and is more or less limited to use in medical school laboratories. We have found the disk method to be generally a reliable index of what can be expected in vivo. We have found Difco Proteose No. 3 agar satisfactory for all but a few of the more rare and fastidious bacteria encountered in urinary tract infections. We believe that the direct inoculation of fresh urine or prostatic secretion has advantages, first, because the bacterial population corresponds more closely in vitro and in vivo and, second, it is timesaving since many of the readings may be made after overnight incubation. We prefer to streak out the inoculum with a large platinum loop, and the yield of growth is quite consistent.
