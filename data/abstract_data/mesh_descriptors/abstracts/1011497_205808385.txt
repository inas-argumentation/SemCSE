Minocycline-Associated Pancreatitis.
To the Editor: Acute pancreatitis is an inflammatory condition with an associated mortality rate of around 2% but can reach as high as 15%–30% in the presence of multiorgan failure.1,2 Drug-induced pancreatitis is an uncommon phenomenon, often overlooked in the clinical setting. We present an unusual case of minocycline-induced pancreatitis in a patient without a history of cystic fibrosis. A 52-year-old man with a history significant only for a recent open reduction and internal fixation of a right fibula fracture, presented with a sudden severe and persistent epigastric abdominal pain. He experienced amechanical fall 54 days previously and sustained a distal fibula fracture, which required an open reduction and internal fixation 7 days prior the current presentation. At that time, he was started on a course of minocycline (100 mg twice daily) as a prophylactic agent. His examination was remarkable for epigastric tenderness, hypertension at 150/90 mm Hg, elevated lipase (994 U/L), and leukocytosis (21 K/mL). Of note, the rest of his chemistry panels, triglycerides, and liver enzymes were within the normal limits. Although an ultrasound showed a normal liver and biliary tract, computed tomography demonstrated peripancreatic fat stranding consistent with pancreatitis (Figure 1). After excluding the more common causes and because of its temporal association, the patient’s minocycline was discontinued for concerns of drug-induced pancreatitis, and aggressive fluid resuscitation was initiated. Three days after conservative management, marked clinical improvement was seen, and there was sustained resolution at an outpatient follow-up appointment. Acute pancreatitis is the inflammation of the pancreas most often presenting clinically as severe epigastric pain.3 Both incidence and prevalence have been rising sharply in the United States, with around 40 cases per 100,000 adults per year and a worldwide prevalence spanning around 5–80 per 100,000 adults.4 Drugs have been implicated as a cause of acute pancreatitis since the 1950s, known as drug-induced pancreatitis, with rates ranging from 0.1% to 2%.5,6 However, the exact incidence is still unknown and generally limited by inadequate studies or lack of rechallenge tests.6,7 Currently, there are 525 offending drugs that have been reported in the World Health Organization database, only 30 of which has a definable mechanism.8 Minocycline is commonly prescribed for the treatment of acne, cellulitis, rheumatoid arthritis, and urethritis, with common side effects of nausea and vertigo.9,10 Meanwhile, hepatotoxicity, pigmentation, systemic lupus erythematosus, and pancreatitis are rare. Possible mechanisms that have been commonly described include allergic reactions, hypersensitivity, and direct toxicity.11 To the best of our knowledge, only 2 case reports of minocycline-induced pancreatitis have been published, and both occurred in patients with cystic fibrosis in the year 2001. As a result, this is the first case of drug-induced pancreatitis caused by minocycline in an individual without cystic fibrosis. Badalov et al8 classified the likelihood of having drug-induced pancreatitis in 5 subcategories.7 Drugs with at least 1 case report and a positive rechallenge test are categorized as Class Ia. Drugs with at least 1 case report and a positive rechallenge test, but without the evidence exclusion of other causes, are Class Ib. Those with at least 4 cases of consistent latency are classified as Class II, whereas minocycline was placed in the Class III drugs.
