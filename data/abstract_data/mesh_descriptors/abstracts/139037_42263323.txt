Choroidal and Cutaneous Melanoma: Distinctly Different Cousins
Johanna M. Seddon, M.D. Epidemiology Unit, Massachusetts Eye and Ear Infirmary, Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA Although choroidal melanoma and cutaneous melanoma share similar embryologic origins from neural crest tissue, these two cancers differ significantly both epidemiologically and biologically. Choroidal or ciliochoroidal melanoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults with an annual incidence of about 6 cases per 1 million population in the United States.1,2 Ocular melanoma may involve the choroid and/or adjacent ciliary body and lead to vision loss and death. Less commonly, iris or conjunctiva may be involved. Despite the higher incidence in less pigmented individuals, the role of ultraviolet exposure as a risk factor remains unclear. In fact, the only known, consistent risk factors are age and ethnicity.3,4 In contrast, cutaneous melanoma has an annual incidence of 100 cases per 1 million in Caucasian populations.5 Ultraviolet exposure has been declared by the International Agency for Research on Cancer to be the primary cause, with fair-skin coloration, dysplastic nevi, number of nevi, hair or eye color, freckles and skin type as additional, well-established risk factors.6 In addition to welldescribed tumor susceptibility genes found to be important in familial cutaneous melanoma variants, our understanding of oncogenic signaling molecules in cutaneous melanoma continues to advance. The overwhelming discrepancy between where we stand with these two cancers is due, at least in part, to the rarity of choroidal melanoma. Epidemiologic studies, as well as prognostic studies, are fraught with difficulties in a disease with such a low incidence. Isager et al., in the epidemiologic report published in this issue of Ophthalmic Epidemiology, found that over the past 54 years the incidence of choroidal melanoma in Denmark has not changed. In contrast, they found that the incidence of both iris melanoma and cutaneous melanoma has increased over this period.7 Although it is often difficult to arrive at the assumption of stability when commenting on the incidence of such a rare cancer, their careful observation and corroboration of this disease occurrence in the Danish Cancer Registry lead them to arrive at the conclusion that the choroidal melanoma incidence rate was stable, in contrast to that of iris melanoma and cutaneous melanoma. Despite the development of highly effective methods of providing local ocular treatment to patients with choroidal melanoma,8,9 40–50% of all patients with this diagnosis succumb to systemic metastasis.10,11 Our current ability to predict which choroidal melanoma patients go on to develop metastasis is evolving, with ongoing studies in molecular and genetic profiling holding promise.
