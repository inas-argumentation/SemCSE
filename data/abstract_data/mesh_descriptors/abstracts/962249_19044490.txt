Combined medical-surgical approaches to treatment of obesity.
Obesity is a condition in which the energy stores of the body, as fat, are excessive. Any effective treatment must create a negative energy balance to burn off this excess fat. In the great majority of people this can be achieved by eating less, sometimes with the moral support of a slimmingclub. Such people probably never ask for medical help, so obese patients who come to the hospital are a highly selected sub-group in whom simple remedies have not worked to their satisfaction. The first step in treatment is to decide if the gain to the patient from weight loss is worth the effort involved. The younger and more overweight the patient, the more there is to gain by weight loss. It is a reasonable, practical target to generate an energy deficit of about 500 to 800 kcal/day, which represents a loss of about 1 to 1�i lb of adipose tissue per week. More rapid weight loss than this is associated with a significant loss of lean body mass. Some people who sincerely want and need to lose fat cannot achieve this energy deficit, either because their total energy expenditure is less than 1500 kcal/day, or else because they cannot voluntarily maintain the necessary dietary restriction. Surgical procedures force patients to eat less, with the added penalty of discomfort, morbidity, and mortality: they have no useful effect that could not have been achieved if similar dietary restriction had been imposed without surgery. Surgery is inappropriate and ineffective in patients with a low energy expenditure. Optimum treatment for obesity involves an assessment of the state of energy balance of the patient, and choice of the most tolerable effective treatment.
