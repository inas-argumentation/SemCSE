Sarcoidosis and obesity: twin problems for the patient.
SARCOIDOSIS is a multi-organ disease characterized by the formation of epithelioid-cell tubercles (granulomas), usually without caseation. It is of unknown cause, with pulmonary involvement being the most common presentation. Patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis may experience not only exertional dyspnea, but also physical and mental fatigue.1 These symptoms create a serious impediment to the quality of life in patients with this condition. In addition, many patients receive corticosteroids for organ dysfunction, which can lead to myopathy—already related to the sarcoidosis in some patients—and exacerbate the fatigue. Fatigue and respiratory impairment lead to inactivity, resulting in weight gain, which in turn leads to stronger avoidance of exercise. Obesity magnifi es the effect of weight bearing on the demand of breathing during exercise. Patients with sarcoidosis can develop obesity and redistribution of body fat from chronic steroid administration. Systemic corticosteroid use is associated with osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, impaired immune response and wound healing, and alterations in glucose and lipid metabolism, factors that can lead to inactivity, fatigue, and additional weight gain. The increased volume of intra-abdominal and intrathoracic fat raises pleural pressure and reduces end-expiratory volume. As a result, obese individuals at rest exhibit expiratory fl ow limitation and respiratory resistance,2 generating further mechanical compromise and increased ventilatory demand in individuals with sarcoidosis. Obesity contributes to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), an additional factor that can exacerbate sleeprelated hypoxemia in patients with infi ltrative lung disease, such as sarcoidosis. OSA is associated with signifi cant risks for pulmonary hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and stroke.3 In patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis who exhibit daytime hypoxemia, obesity can worsen gas exchange, increasing the risk for cardiovascular complications. An ‘overlap syndrome’ is already recognized in patients with combined chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and OSA.4 As for patients with COPD, health-related quality of life (HRQOL) questionnaires with respect to mobility, working capacity and the activities of daily living have been developed and validated for sarcoidosis.5 In this issue of the Journal, Gvozdenovic and colleagues compare the impact of high body mass index (BMI) on symptoms of fatigue and dyspnea, health status, and spirometric measurements in 184 patients with sarcoidosis and sexand age-matched healthy adults.6 They show that patients exhibited more fatigue (as assessed by the fatigue scale), exertional dyspnea, poorer health status (per St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire) and lung function, and higher BMIs than control subjects, and confi rm that health and fatigue status was poorly related to lung function, as shown by others.5 Because fatigue is experienced by so many patients with sarcoidosis,1 health and fatigue status assessments may eventually turn out to be a more reliable way of gauging response to immunomodulatory therapy. Rehabilitation for management of physical fi tness, coping and self management may be an effective option for some patients. Fatigue should be taken seriously in the management of sarcoidosis patients.
