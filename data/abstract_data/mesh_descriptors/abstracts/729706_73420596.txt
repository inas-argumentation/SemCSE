Transient Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Palsy Post–Total Thyroidectomy
Recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) injury is an infrequent complication of thyroid surgery and can result in either temporary (i.e., neuropraxia) or permanent disability. The exact mechanism of neuropraxia is still debated, as well as how to best prevent intraoperative injury. Although intraoperative visual integrity can be ensured and nerve monitoring of the RLN used, vocal complications can still occur. We report a case highlighting a patient with complete spontaneous vocal cord recovery despite initial severe dysphonia and dysphagia. We discuss the current theories of neuropraxia and present methods to best prevent this injury intraoperatively. A 68-year-old woman with hyperthyroidism was referred to general surgery for an enlarging, uncomfortable left neck mass. Associated symptoms included weakness and dizziness. Vital signs were normal, and physical examination was significant for a 5-cm immobile left neck mass, without regional adenopathy. The patient had no symptoms of severe RLN or tracheoesophageal compression, such as dysphonia, dyspnea, and dysphagia. CT imaging of the neck revealed a roughly 175-cm heterogeneous mass in the left thyroid lobe, extending substernally and displacing the trachea and esophagus to the right of midline (Fig. 1 A and B). Total thyroidectomy was performed due to concern of follicular neoplasm after biopsy. Endotracheal anesthesia was used intraoperatively and both RLNs were identified and protected as they entered the larynx. They were periodically tested with a surgeondirected, intermittent, Medtronic Nerve Integrity Monitor–Response 3.0 intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM) system. At the end of the procedure, the only notable finding on nerve stimulation was minor bilaterally decreased amplitude on electromyography of the vocal cords 2 cm outside the larynx. Postoperatively, the patient had severe dysphonia and dysphagia, extending her hospital stay to five days. Speech evaluation revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia, weak laryngeal elevation, and severe coughing with larger food boluses. Fiber-optic laryngoscopy revealed bilateral true vocal cord immobility in a paramedian position, with a patent airway. The patient had no dyspnea, so there was no discussion of early tracheostomy. Interestingly, modified barium swallow revealed no significant pharyngeal residue, laryngeal penetration, or tracheal aspiration, possibly because of compensatory swallowing strategies. Because the patient was not deemed an aspiration risk, she was discharged and scheduled with an otolaryngology consult and outpatient speech therapy to treat both dysphagia and dysphonia. One month postoperatively, the patient continued to have significant dysphonia, which completely and spontaneously resolved three months postoperatively. The average incidence of temporary RLN paresis is 9.8 per cent, whereas the incidence of permanent RLN paresis is 2.3 per cent.1 Most resolve within weeks, with peak recovery at six weeks, and 97 per cent recover at nine weeks. The proposed causes include traction, clamping, thermal injury, ischemia, and ligature entrapment. Stretch-induced RLN injury is a potential mechanism of nerve injury postthyroidectomy, particularly of the right RLN in bilateral dissection. During retraction, the shorter and more oblique right RLN lacks redundancy in its course, which predisposes it to in situ stretch, perhaps causing ischemia of the vasa nervorum1 (Fig. 2 A). On the contrary, another study showed increased RLN diameter in the time between initial visualization and complete thyroid removal, presumed to be from edema (Fig. 2 B). Therefore, it is possible that stretch may cause vasa nervorum ischemia, in turn causing edema exacerbating neural dysfunction.
