Is routine chest radiography necessary for the initial evaluation of fever in neutropenic children with cancer?
We hold the term ‘‘standard of care’’ in such high regard that as one utters this term, one cannot help but think of Moses descending from The Mountain with clinical standards etched in stone tablets. But what exactly is a standard of care? How are standards of care established, who establishes them, what does it take, and how long does it take for a community of physicians to acknowledge and adopt a standard of care? Sometimes a standard of care might arise from a landmark study. For example, when Gaston et al. reported a randomized trial of penicillin prophylaxis versus placebo for young children with sickle cell anemia, a clear and dramatic benefit of penicillin was demonstrated [1], and the pediatric community was quick to adopt penicillin prophylaxis as the standard of care for these children. Sometimes standards of care evolve more slowly over time as a series of studies consistently and convincingly produce similar findings. Or it may take panels of experts to put such studies together for us and articulate evidence-based standards of clinical practice. In many instances, the answer is not so clear, because studies have conflicting results and physicians have conflicting opinions, and a single standard does not emerge. Sometimes, even when a series of studies point in the same direction, a standard of care consistent with these studies is still not adopted. This seems especially true when an old, entrenched standard is challenged by a new one. The role of the routine chest radiograph in the initial evaluation of children with fever and neutropenia falls into this category. In the largest study of this question to date, Renoult et al. (Pediatr Blood Cancer 2004; 43: 128–224) meticulously reviewed the course of 170 children hospitalized with fever and neutropenia, 157 of whom had chest radiographs on admission [2]. Only 5% of these children had lobar pneumonias, and only 1% of the asymptomatic children had pneumonia. The authors further point out that none of the children had their initial therapy modified on the basis of the radiograph. They conclude that pneumonia is uncommon in children with fever and neutropenia, especially in those children who have no signs or symptoms of respiratory illness. There are two striking aspects to this study. The first is that the results are remarkably similar to three other reports of the role of the chest radiograph in children with fever and neutropenia. Similar series have been reported across time and place—1988 in Oakland [3], 1991 in Dallas [4], and 1997 in Rochester, NY [5]. The overall incidence of pneumonia in these studies ranged from 3.7% to 6.0%, and in asymptomatic children it ranged from 0% to 2.3%. Taken together, these four studies include 540 episodes of fever and neutropenia; in only 5 of these 540 episodes were asymptomatic children found to have pneumonia. This finding did not alter the initial management of these five children, and all five recovered. The authors of all four studies reached the same conclusion: pneumonia is uncommon in this setting, and the chest radiograph need not be performed routinely for all children with fever and neutropenia. In addition, two other studies examining the role of the chest radiograph in adults with fever and neutropenia show a relatively low incidence of pneumonia (4–9%), particularly in asymptomatic patients [6,7]. Finally, the current report [2] and the Rochester study [5] include 53 children with fever and neutropenia who did not have chest radiographs at initial presentation, and all 53 fared well. Although this is a small number of children, it suggests that children with fever and neutropenia and no signs of respiratory disease can be successfully managed without a chest radiograph. The second interesting aspect of this study is that routine chest radiography is still a part of the initial evaluation of children with fever and neutropenia in some centers. This suggests that the three previously reported studies are not convincing enough, or that the prevailing sentiment at some centers is that a more cautious approach is still warranted. The rationale for obtaining a chest radiograph in patients with fever and neutropenia likely dates back to the 1970s when Sickles et al. found that patients with fever, neutropenia, and pneumonia on chest X-ray were less likely to have cough, sputum production, and rales than non-neutropenic patients with pneumonia [8,9].
