Medical Management of Obesity in Women: Office-Based Approaches to Weight Management
The Problem Obesity is a women’s health issue because obesity disproportionately affects women. The 1999 to 2000 NHANES survey pegs obesity rates (body mass index [BMI] $30 Kg/m) at 30.5% of the U.S. population. For U.S. women, the prevalence is even higher. Of women, 33.4% are obese compared with 27.5% of men in that survey. For minority women, the situation is even worse. Obesity rates are 39.7% for Mexican American women and 49.7% for African American women compared to 30.1% for NonHispanic white women. The reasons for this gender discrepancy are both biologic and psychologic. Female sex hormones, particularly progesterone, adversely influence food intake and energy expenditure, and the menstrual cycle and exogenous hormones for contraception or symptoms of menopause may adversely affect weight. Life cycle events such as pregnancy and menopause may result in excessive weight gain or retention, at least for a subset of women. Psychologic factors may also play a role. The American culture’s excessive drive for female slimness may promote excessive restrictive eating patterns, which can then trigger binges of overeating. The concern, of course, is the morbidities that accompany obesity, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancer. In particular, attention has recently focused on the risk that being overweight imposes for type 2 diabetes. This disease currently affects over 6% of Americans, and the rates are rising.
