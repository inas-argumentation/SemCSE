ASSOCIATION BETWEEN ELEVATED BODY‐MASS INDEX AND IMPAIRED SENSE OF SMELL IN OLDER PEOPLE
To the Editor: The article by Ghusn et al.' adds to the growing understanding of factors that determine the potential benefit of CPR in the nursing home population. The apparent equivalent survival of nursing home patients to community-based patients may be explained by factors that reflect the mix of nursing home patients, which may not be captured completely by the definition of the various comorbidities of which Alzheimer's disease appears to be rather low. This may confound the understanding of the patient population and make it difficult when comparisons are made with long-term care facilities in various parts of the country and abroad. In Canada, for example, nursing homes have an extremely high prevalence of residents with dementia caused by Alzheimer's disease and other conditions as one of the major reasons for entrance into a long-term care faciliry.r' Moreover, most nursing homes in Canada are not used for shortterm programs and, therefore, are responsible for a population of very frail individuals. The Baycrest Centre study, for example, reflects a population whose average age is similar to that in the Ghusn et al. study, but dementia is a prominent co-mobidity in from 50 to 70% of residents in Baycrest Centre and other long-term care facilities in Ontario.v 3 Therefore, the burden of illness and disability may further confound the understanding and estimation of potential beneficial outcome of CPR and should be considered when protocols and policies are developed for patients and residents in the long-term care system. For those individuals whose cognitive function is substantially impaired and who require long-term care facility placement, the likelihood of survival from CPR is so remote that such interventions may have little, if anything, to offer to this population whether the event is witnessed or not.
