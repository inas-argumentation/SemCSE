Choline is metabolized through phosphorylation that produces phosphorylcholine through choline kinase, further incorporated into phosphatidylcholine, a major component of biological membranes and a member of the lecithin group of yellow-brownish fatty substances. Choline is also metabolized through acetylation through choline acetyltransferase, which produce acetylcholine. Thus PET/CT imaging with C-choline and F-fluorocholine can be used to identify tumoral tissue where an increase in cell proliferation as well as the activity of the enzyme choline is associated with an increase in choline component and has long been used in work up of prostate cancer. Apart from prostatic adenocarcinoma, F-choline PET/CT is known to have good performances in other neoplastic cell type, such as hepatocellular-carcinoma, glioma, bone tumor, soft-tissue tumor, and lung cancer. Besides, F-choline PET/CT can be useful in detecting hyperplastic tissue, such as thymomas and parathyroid adenoma. But for lung cancer, even for bronchioloalveolar cancer subtype, although F-choline PET/CT is a reliable tool for lung tumor staging and to differentiate malignant and benign lung lesions (through the phosphorylation and acetylation pathways), a superiority of F-choline over FDG has not been demonstrated in this setting, except for the detection of brain metastases, whereas its price is three to five times the price of FDG. F-choline PET/CT imaging, like F-FDG PET/CT scan, is also subject to several causes of false-positive, such as infection, tuberculoma, or inflammation. There is only one published case of mucinous cancer detected with F-choline PET/CT. It was a primary poorly differentiated mucinous rectal cancer. So in the case of the mucinous colorectal adenocarcinoma lung metastasis reported by Balata et al.; whether F-choline PET/CT could have significantly contributed to diagnosis remains uncertain. The differentiation degree could play a role in the avidity of tumor cell for F-choline.
