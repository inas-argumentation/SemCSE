Distinguishing type 1 and type 2 diabetes at diagnosis. What is the problem?
In this issue, Katz et al. (2) examine fasting C-peptide and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein-1 (IGFBP-1), CO2, and ketonuria as measures to help distinguish childhood type 1 diabetes (T1D) from type 2 diabetes (T2D) at diagnosis. Neither the implied assumption that a substantial number of children with newly diagnosed diabetes are indeterminate as to type nor that rational therapy requires a definitive diagnosis according to contemporary classification is certain. Furthermore, immediate classification of diabetes, while generally appropriate for clinical purposes, should be recognized as more contextual than etiologic and categorical (each case is T1D or T2D) rather than dimensional (each case is on a continuum between two clinical extremes linked by a range of overlapping phenotypes) (1). Katz et al. (2) do not investigate the magnitude of the problem of classification in their population but, rather, investigate the characteristics of those whom they certify as T1D or T2D (defined by obesity, ability to be weaned from insulin, and a close family history of T2D). Not only does such determination involve what we define asT1DandT2Dbut in some settingsmay also require consideration of maturity onset diabetes of the young (MODY) (3) or atypical diabetes mellitus of African-Americans (aka ADM, Flatbush diabetes) (4, 5). Among 700 newly diagnosed 5to 19-year-old patients from three university centers in Florida over a 5-year period, 3% of those initially classified as T1D were later considered to have T2D and 8% of those initially diagnosed as T2D were reclassified as having T1D.Most of themisdiagnoses as T1Dwere of children diagnosed with ketosis or ketoacidosis, while the misdiagnoses of T1D as T2D were typically overweight youngsters without diabetes-specific antibodies, but a subsequent clinical evolution more consistent with T1D. In this academic setting, the proportion of patients in whom classification was problematic was less than 5% of newly diagnosed patients (6). False-positive and false-negative islet autoantibody results are common when comparing reports from different laboratories and challenge attempts to differentiate T2D fromT1D.Katz et al. (2) found that 6 of 32 or 18% of presumptive T2D, representing 3% of the entire population of newly diagnosed patients, were glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD)-65 antibody positive and considered unclassifiable by this criterion and excluded from the study. As with autoimmune thyroid disease, there is a spectrum of glandular dysfunction associated with islet cell autoimmunity from latency (no disease) to prediabetes (impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance) to a clinical phenotype of T2D, probably precipitated by insulin resistance from overweight, to ketosis-prone T1D. In the UK Prospective Diabetes Study of T2D, the finding of islet cell autoimmunity was age related; 21% of 25–34 yr olds were islet cell antibody (ICA) positive, 34% glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA) positive, and 20% positive for both antibodies, decreasing to 4, 7, and 2%, respectively, in those aged 55–65 yr. There was significant association of antibody positivity with less overweight, higher hemoglobin A1c concentrations, and more rapid deterioration of beta-cell function (7). Among 764 Swedish patients aged 15–34 yr with newly diagnosed diabetes, 76% were classified as T1D, 14% as T2D, and the rest unclassified. A startling 47% of T2D and 59%of unclassified patients were positive for one or more diabetes-specific antibodies, with the antibodypositive T2D or unclassified patients, as in the UK study, being significantly lighter, with lower C-peptide concentrations, than the antibody-negative patients (8). Studies in US children with presumptive T2D have paralleled some of the findings in adults. Among 48 children, 8% were positive for the ICA512 fragment, 30% were GADA positive, and 35% insulin autoantibody positive; unlike in adults, there was no correlation between antibody positivity and degree of obesity (9).
