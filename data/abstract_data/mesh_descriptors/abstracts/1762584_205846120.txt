It takes a train to knock down global warming and obesity.
0091-7435/$ – see front m doi:10.1016/j.ypmed.2010. After all, fuel-consuming single occupancy vehicles and trucks contribute to both global warming and the obesity epidemic. Their consumption of fossil-fuels makes transportation one of the most important sources of non-renewable energy expenditure (Wen and Rissel, 2008; Zheng, 2008; Transportation Energy Databook, 2010; Frank et al., 2010). At the same time, this passive mode of transportation contributes to the deficit of human energy expenditure and that fuels the obesity epidemic. In other words, if human beings, and in particular Americans, while commuting to work, spent more physical energy and burned less fossil fuel, they would address two major, global problems with one commute switch. There is an abundant lack of physical activity and that is linked to obesity. In this month's issue of Preventive Medicine, four papers accrue additional evidence and provide useful pointers. Mauriello et al.(2010) report some promising results of a randomized trial of a population-based multiple behavior (physical activity 60 h/day for 5+ days/week, 5+ fruit/vegetable servings/day, TV ≤2 h/ day) obesity prevention intervention for adolescents, but they caution that the physical activity component had the least treatment compliance consistency over time, perhaps due to weather/ seasonal effects (Mauriello et al., 2010). In another randomized trial of weight loss maintenance with obese adults, Levy et al. (2010) report that the maintenance-tailored intervention led to a 31% relative reduction in weight regain in the intervention compared to the control group between the18and 30-month follow-ups, with the proviso that the largest reductions occurred mainly among the intervention group participants in the highest tertile of total weight loss at 18 months (Levy et al., 2010). In a population-based study in Finland, Korniloff et al. (2010) report an association between increasing leisure-time physical activity and decreasing BMI within the contexts of increasing metabolic syndrome and depressive symptoms, but they also remind us about the complications involved in disentangling potentially bi-directional effects in crosssectional studies (Korniloff et al., 2010). In another cross-sectional, population-based study in Poland, Kwaśniewska et al. (2010) report an association between any active commuting physical activity (independent of leisure-time physical activity) and less abdominal obesity (identified as the most important component within the context of metabolic syndrome symptoms), with the associations being strongest among men and postmenopausal women (Kwaśniewska et al., 2010). There is also ample data showing that commuters who use public transportation are physically, aerobically active when going to and from the stations and stops, moving around within stations, climbing stairs, etc. (Besser and Dannenberg, 2005; Edwards, 2008; Morabia et al., 2009, 2010). In 2003 Hill et al. computed that the weight gain responsible for the obesity epidemic in the US between 1988–1994 and 1999–2000 corresponded to a daily excess intake of 100 kcal/day (Hill et al., 2003). They concluded that obesity would start to decline if on average populations avoided storing this extra amount of energy either by increased physical activity orminor dietary restriction. Using data from surveys performed in Geneva, Switzerland from 1997 to 2001, we estimated how people on a mass scale could expend these 100 extra calories. We concluded that it would take approximately 60 min/day of slow walking or 30 min/day of brisk walking to do so (Morabia and Costanza, 2004). At present, these remain speculations and observational data. Hence the great importance of the results of a natural experiment, reported this summer. MacDonald et al. interviewed 498 people in 2006 and 2008, before and after the introduction of a new work commuter light rail transit (LRT) in North Carolina (MacDonald et al., 2010). Compared to those who kept commuting by car, the LRT commuters had an 18% reduction in BMI, corresponding to 6.5 pounds for a person 5′5′′ (165 cm) tall over seven months. Assuming 213 days in 7 months and 3500 calories per pound of weight, 6.5 pounds represents 22,750 calories lost in 213 days, that is, 107 kcal/day.
