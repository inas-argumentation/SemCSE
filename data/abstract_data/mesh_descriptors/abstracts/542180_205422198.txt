Grading chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy in adults.
www.Nursing2017.com February l Nursing2017 l 67 CHEMOTHERAPY-INDUCED peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), a common neurologic adverse reaction to some chemotherapeutic agents, causes pain, numbness, tingling, edema, or paresis.1 CIPN can impact a patient’s quality of life and sleep and may be a major cause of pain. One of the most common nonhematologic adverse reactions to cancer treatment, it’s associated with three primary chemotherapeutic agents used to treat ovarian cancer: paclitaxel, carboplatin, and cisplatin.2 All three drugs are known to cause paresthesia, numbness, neuropathic pain, or a combination of these.2,3 CIPN predominantly consists of sensory rather than motor symptoms.4 Nurses need to assess patients undergoing chemotherapy for the presence of peripheral sensory neuropathy and grade it accurately to preserve patients’ quality of life and safety. Timing of chemotherapy administration or dose adjustments, including potential discontinuation of the drug if CIPN reaches a high grade, may be indicated based on the severity of this common adverse reaction to cancer therapy.4 Managing symptoms The grade and length of time patients experience peripheral neuropathy depend on various factors, including the causative agent, dosage of the agent received, and grade when peripheral neuropathy is diagnosed. Peripheral neuropathy may persist throughout the patient’s life but may decrease in grade. The pain associated with CIPN may be managed with agents such as nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs, opioids, or adjuvant agents, such as tricyclic antidepressants.
