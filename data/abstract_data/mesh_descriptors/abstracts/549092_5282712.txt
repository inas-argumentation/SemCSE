Obesity and infertility: the importance of ART policies and practice standards.
Obesity is now identified as a medical illness that affects an increasing number of our patients with 34% of patients in the U.S. being obese (1). This disease increases the risk of oligoovulation (2) and overall risk of infertility (3), and we have an increasing number of obese patients. It is also known that obesity adversely affects the treatment for infertility. Women with a body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m have decreased in vitro fertilization (IVF) rates of clinical pregnancy and live birth; the odds ratio (95% confidence interval) of live birth for women with a BMI between 30 kg/m and 35 kg/m is 0.63 (0.47–0.85) and drops to 0.32 (0.16–0.64) for women with BMI >40 (4). Obesity also has a significant impact on pregnancy. There is an increased risk of miscarriage, preterm delivery, and medical complications such as hypertension and diabetes for obese women. Clearly, it is critical for our specialty to counsel patients prior to attempting pregnancy and develop policies to optimize the care of obese women and men seeking infertility treatment. The study by Kaye et al. (5), published this month in Fertility and Sterility, demonstrates the importance of developing policies to ensure optimization of patient care before and during infertility treatment. Currently, there is inconsistency in the delivery of information to obese women seeking IVF. One-third of programs do not have a formal written policy regarding the care of obese women seeking reproductive treatment. There is also a variation in the policies to delay infertility therapy until patients have the opportunity to lose weight, although there is a clear effect of obesity on the success of treatment and risks in the subsequent pregnancy. Sixteen percent of centers having no policy on putting a hold on therapy compared to 21.4% of programs having the strictest policy of not offering IVF for women with a BMI > 35–40 kg/m. The greatest variation in policies between centers is the care of obese women seeking treatment with donor oocytes. Leaders in reproductive medicine can work with IVF programs to develop standards to ensure optimal care for our obese patients seeking treatment for infertility. The study does demonstrate some recognition of the risk of anesthesia for obese patients at some centers. Anesthesia risk is clearly increased for the obese woman based on physiologic changes in lung and cardiovascular function. Over one-half of the anesthesiology departments affiliated with reproductive endocrinology and infertility centers had policies regarding anesthesia for oocyte retrievals, although more consistent counseling is indicated for obese patients prior to any procedure. Fortunately, this study demonstrated the majority of programs referred their obese patients to a dietitian/nutritionist and some centers have their own programs to encourage weight loss. Although the benefit of bariatric surgery is still unclear, two-thirds of the centers referred obese patients to specialist in bariatric surgery or other specialties to ensure that patients are aware of treatment options for obesity.
