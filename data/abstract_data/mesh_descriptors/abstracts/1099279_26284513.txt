Who (if anyone) actually gets steroid-sparing effects from aeroallergen immunotherapy?
The European study on the steroid-sparing effects of specific immunotherapy (SIT) in childrenwith asthma published in this issue of the Journal adds substantial credibility to this controversial claim in the list of potential benefits from aeroallergen immunotherapy. This well designed study involved 65 children randomized to receive standard Global Initiative for Asthma– guideline pharmacotherapy for mild to moderate persistent asthma with or without openly administered subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) with a house dust mite allergoid. Unfortunately, the authors’ original design to include a placebo-injected control group was not accepted by their ethics committee. However, the potential treatment bias of the open studywasmitigated by having clinical decisions on modification of inhaled steroid doses made by an algorithm executed by a remote third party. All of the expected clinical and immunologic outcomes of successful SIT were achieved, and 62 of 65 subjects completed the full 2-year study. Asthma control as assessed by the GOAL criteria did not change in either group. After 2 years of treatments, the reduction in initial inhaled fluticasone dose (mean 310 mg/d) was 54% among children receiving SIT compared with 29% in the control group. More clinically significant than the changes in mean fluticasone requirements is the observation that almost half of the children receiving SIT were able to reduce inhaled steroids by 2 or more dosage steps over the 2 years compared with only 19% of the control group. Despite this reduction in inhaled steroid requirement, a significant increase in morning peak flow was also observed. In this study of relatively mild asthma, there were only 3 exacerbations requiring oral steroids during the study. Methacholine sensitivity was unchanged by SIT. In 1997, our Hopkins study group published what was to become a controversial negative study of the clinical benefits of broad-spectrum SIT added to optimal pharmacotherapy and environmental controls in children with moderate to severe asthma.
