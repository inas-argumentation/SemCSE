Focusing on Patient‐Oriented Evidence that Matters in the Care of Patients with Diabetes Mellitus
After reading Dr. Haines’ well-written call to action, I worry that pharmacists are still stuck in the “lower glucose levels are better” paradigm when managing patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. This point of view, although very appealing and ultimately reasonable from a pathophysiologic standpoint, cannot be reconciled with current research findings. As demonstrated in a recent evaluation of review articles on the treatment of patients with type 2 diabetes, this misconception has been widely disseminated by experts in the treatment of blood glucose levels. The goal of all medical professionals is to help people live longer and better. We need to heed the literature that tells us how best to meet these goals. Patient-oriented evidence that matters (POEM) shows that tight control of blood glucose level (< 110 mg/dl) does not meet these objectives and may not be in the best interest of patients. While putting patients at risk for hypoglycemia, tight control does not offer the benefits that the pathophysiologic reasoning would predict. The United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) demonstrated that tight control of blood glucose level, compared with moderate control (< 270 mg/dl), did not prevent premature mortality, did not affect any single macrovascular complication of diabetes, and did not improve (or worsen) quality of life (Figure 1). Although tight control reduced the likelihood of experiencing 1 of the 21 composite end points of the study, most of the risk reduction can be attributed to the decreased need for retinal photocoagulation. This single end point accounted for 2.7 percentage points of the total 3.2% absolute risk reduction, which represents 84% of the total risk reduction. Eliminating this end point from the analysis would eliminate all benefit shown in the trial. On the other hand, tight control of blood pressure and cholesterol level has been proven to decrease mortality and morbidity in patients with type 2 diabetes and is much more valuable than blood glucose control. A recent guideline on setting priorities in diabetes care states, “We do not intend to suggest that glycemic control is an ineffective intervention, but rather that treatment of hypertension should be prioritized and stressed as the most important intervention for the average population of persons with type 2 diabetes.” Not all interventions in medicine offer the same benefit to patients. Too often we try to do everything at once for a given patient, whereas the wiser approach would be to prioritize patient care, starting with the values and wishes of the patient.
