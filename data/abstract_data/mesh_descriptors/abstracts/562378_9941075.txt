Delayed recovery of diabetic chorea following correction of hyperglycemia
Sirs: Hyperglycemia is a rare cause of adult-onset chorea [1]. The chorea occurs in patients with moderate to severe elevations of blood sugar and may be the presenting feature of diabetes mellitus [1]. In some clinical series, the abnormal movements resolve within 24 to 48 hours of serum glucose normalization [2, 3]. However, in others, there is no immediate improvement when hyperglycemia is corrected, and this phenomenon has led authors to question whether high blood sugar is really the principal cause for the movement disorder [1, 4]. In this report, we present a patient who developed generalized chorea in the setting of new onset diabetes mellitus. His symptoms gradually improved over three months despite rapid control of his glucose levels. We wish to emphasize that this delayed response to treatment was most likely the natural course of the disease. A 54 year-old man developed writhing movements of his lower extremities one evening during dinner. The symptoms progressed over 24 hours to involve his head, trunk, and all four limbs. He had no prior history of medical problems. He was taking no medications, and there was no history of neuroleptic use. Family and social history were unremarkable. The neurological examination, two days after onset, showed impersistence of sustained lateral gaze and tongue protrusion. There were generalized choreoathetotic movements involving all four limbs and the trunk. He was oriented to person, place, and time. Muscle strength, sensation, and deep tendon reflexes were normal. Laboratory testing was notable for a serum glucose level of 256 mg/dl and hemoglobin A1c of 10.5 % (nl: 4.7–6.4 %). Serum osmolality was 308 mosm/kg. Routine blood chemistries, hematology panel, sedimentation rate, antistreptolysin-O titer, thyroid function studies, serum ceruloplasmin, antinuclear antibodies, and anticardiolipin antibodies were normal. No acanthocytes were seen on the peripheral blood smear. DNA analysis of the Huntington gene was normal with CAG expansion sizes of 17 and 15 trinucleotide repeats (nl < 34). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination revealed 6 white blood cells with normal protein and IgG levels. The CSF glucose level was elevated (137 mg/dl, nl: 50–80 mg/dl). Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, with and without contrast, one week after onset of symptoms was normal. Glyuburide 5 mg q. d. resulted in good control of his diabetes and haloperidol 1 mg t. i. d. was associated with some improvement of chorea. His movements gradually resolved and disappeared after three months. The haloperidol was discontinued. He has remained symptom free over the past two years and remains on oral hypoglycemic agents with excellent control of his blood sugar. Repeat MRI of the head two years after the onset of symptoms was normal. The presence of acute onset, symmetric, generalized chorea in a 54-year-old man leads to a wide differential diagnosis. In our patient, an extensive evaluation excluded other causes of chorea, and the close association with the onset of diabetes suggested that hyperglycemia was the primary cause. Earlier work suggests that in approximately half of the patients presenting with the combination of chorea and diabetes mellitus, the simple correction of blood sugar did not lead to an immediate resolution of symptoms [1–6]. This delayed recovery has generally been interpreted to suggest that diabetes mellitus is not the sole cause of the abnormal movements because confounding neurological problems have often blurred the clinical picture. In contrast to our patient, many of these earlier reports have described unilateral chorea, and imaging has characteristically implicated structural lesions in the basal ganglia, including petechial hemorrhages, vascular malformations or myelinolysis as more likely causative factors for the movement disorder [5, 6]. Abnormal blood sugar values have been considered a secondary factor. There are only three earlier reports that describe patients with generalized diabetic chorea who lack acute imaging abnormalities [1, 2, 4]. In one patient, the movements resolved within 24 hours of correction of the hyperglycemia, while the remaining two cases had courses similar to ours [2]. One was an 81-year-old woman with new onset diabetes mellitus whose dyskinesias were initially controlled with the use of tetrabenazine, but they recurred twice following attempts to wean her from the medication [4]. Complete resolution without medication took about five months. The authors suggested a multifactorial etiology for the delayed recovery and speculated that “old age” and vascular lesions in the subcortical white matter might be causative.
