EDITORIAL: QUALITY OF LIFE AND PROSTATE CANCER TREATMENT
Prostate cancer and attempts to treat this condition may affect not only the quantity, but also the quality, of lives. Quantity of life is relatively easy to measure, although accurate ascertainment of cause of death and, therefore, cancer specific survival, can be more challenging. Because of the slow growth rates of many clinically localized prostate cancers, the impact of aggressive treatment with radiotherapy or radical prostatectomy among men with these cancers appears to be relatively modest. For example, among 28,424 men with moderately differentiated, clinically localized prostate cancer reported in the United States Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program from 1983 to 1992 the 10-year cancer specific survival was 77% for men treated expectantly, 76% treated with external beam radiotherapy and 87% treated with radical prostatectomy.l Given these relatively small differences, which may be true differences or simply the impact of bias introduced by case selection, how these various therapies affect quality of life may be important to consider when making treatment decisions for individual patients. However, quality of life is more difficult to measure than 10-year survival. To get a complete picture of quality of life in different disease states investigators tend to combine “generic” quality of life measures, which are meant to be applicable across diseases, with disease specific measures, which focus on issues or domains that are particularly important to the disease of interest. In the setting of prostate cancer, for example, the domains of urinary continence, sexual function and bowel function appear particularly important to characterize. Perrotte et a1 (page 398) describe men with evidence of residual or recurrent prostate cancer after primary therapy with radiation. Possibly curative salvage options for these patients who are likely to die of rather than simply with tumor include cryotherapy and radical prostatectomy. Although some patients in this situation would presumably be willing to tolerate moderate side effects for a possibility of cure, even if it is slight, it is reasonable to seek a treatment strategy that minimizes side effects. These investigators used several validated questionnaires, including the disease specific UCLA Prostate Cancer Index, to examine the relationships between side effects and various techniques for performing salvage cryotherapy. Interestingly, although the UCLA index can be used to calculate scores assessing prostate targeted quality of life in the domains of sexual, urinary and bowel function,z the authors chose instead to report subject responses to individual questions about different aspects of how they were doing, presumably because the pattern of individual responses is easier for clinician readers to interpret than continuous domain scores. Nevertheless, the results seem to indicate that the probabilities of complications, such as incontinence and sexual dysfunction (in those men who were sexually active before treatment), were considerable. Moreover, use of a urethral warming catheter appeared to reduce substantially some of these side effects. Unfortunately, because of the lack of a randomized comparison group, it is difficult to tell how the side effect profile of cryotherapy compares to alternative management strategies Or whether this therapy is really effective at all. Brachytherapy as a treatment for clinically localized prostate cancer appears to have become increasingly popular among patients during the last few years, as some men think it is quick, easy and nearly risk-free. Lee et a1 (page 403) examine the quality of life outcomes among 46 men treated with brachytherapy at a single institution. They measured the outcome of treatment at baseline, and at 1 and 3 months with the cancer specific FACT-G, covering 5 domains of quality of life that can be affected by cancer and a supplemental prostate cancer specific subscale which, together with the FACT-G, becomes the FACT-P. Subjects also completed the International Prostate Symptom Score (I-PSS) which was developed to measure the severity of lower urinary tract symptoms among men with benign prostatic hyperplasia. It may be hard for clinicians to have a sense of what degree of changes in these scores really matter to patients. A rule of thumb is that changes of more than half of a standard deviation in the baseline distribution of such a measure in the study population represent a moderate effect size that is likely to be clinically important.3 Impressively, mean scores on the physical well-being scale deteriorated more than a baseline standard deviation at 1 month, mean scores on the prostate cancer scale deteriorated almost a standard deviation and mean I-PSS increased more than 2 standard deviations. By 3 months mean scores of the first 2 scales improved toward the baseline values and were no longer significantly worse. I-PSS had improved but remained still significantly worse at more than a standard deviation above the baseline score. These results in terms of lower urinary tract symptoms are more striking when one considers that some symptoms that might be important to patients with prostate cancer undergoing radiation, such as painful urination, are not included in the I-PSS which was developed for men with benign prostate hyperplasia. These results suggest that brachytherapy can have a considerable impact, at least in the short term, on patient perceptions of some aspects of quality of life, including lower urinary tract symptom severity. Thus, brachytherapy does not appear to be risk-free. However, we do not know to what extent these findings are generalizable to other treatment sites. Moreover, how these findings would compare to measurements in similar patients treated with other modalities, such as external beam radiation or radical prostatectomy, is not known.
