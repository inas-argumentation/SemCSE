Can Anti‐HBc Testing Decrease the Residual Risk of Transfusion‐Related HIV Transmission?
According to Le Pont and coworkers [l], 3,500,000 donations from regular donors, were collected in 1987. 388 of these donations were HIV seropositive. Since the overall rate of HIV-seropositive donations was 29/100,000, and the donations from regular donors represented 85%, one can calculate that about 600,000 donations were collected from new donors, including 801 HIV-seropositive donations (133/100,000 donations). The authors appear to assume that with the removal of 161,000 (4.6% of 3,500,000) anti-HBc-positive units up to 95 (40% of 238) HIV-infected, but ‘false-negative’ donations of regular donors would have been excluded from the blood supply. However, the number of false-negative units among the 161,000 anti-HBc-negative donations from new donors that would have been needed for replacement of the excluded ones, cannot be estimated from the data provided and remains unknown. It is generally accepted that the rate of recently HIVinfected and therefore frequently false-negative individiuals is relatively high in new donors of preponderantly younger age groups, where the prevalence of anti-HBc is still low. Consequently, it is impossible to rule out that the donor-exchange procedure initiated by anti-HBc screening might ultimately result in an increment of false-negative donations rather than in a reduction. Such an exchange would be especially riskful where the rate of HIV-seropositive donations is already very low (e.g. the former Federal Republic of Germany: 1-2/100.000 unpaid donations).
