A call for evidence of benefits outweighing harms before implementing new technologies: comment on "Diffusion of computer-aided mammography after mandated Medicare coverage".
V ariability in the accuracy of screening mammography is well documented and varies across populations of women and radiologists. Variability in the accuracy of screening mammography has paved the way for new technologies to be developed that could improve breast cancer detection. New evidence reported in this issue of the Archives describes the diffusion and annual cost of one such technology, computeraided detection mammography (CAD) technology. Fenton et al have described a rapid increase in CAD use from 5% in 2001 to 27% in 2003 after Congressionally mandated Medicare coverage occurred in 2000. Supplemental fees for CAD use cost Medicare an estimated $19.5 million in 2003 alone. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) initially approved CAD for use in screening mammography interpretation starting in 1998, based on relatively few studies that included small numbers of breast cancer cases and radiologists using CAD at a single or few mammography facilities. Studies that used matched designs in which the sensitivity of mammography was compared before and after CAD was applied on the same mammogram have reported a small benefit of CAD in detecting breast cancer. The first data from community practice reported in 2001 compared groups of radiologists using CAD and groups not using CAD, and the results were less favorable than in matched design studies. In the largest study of CAD implementation in community practice, CAD use was associated with a substantially increased number of examinations with abnormal results and increased numbers of biopsies and a nonsignificant increase in cancer detection compared with facilities that did not use CAD. However, to my knowledge, no study has had a sufficiently large sample size to determine whether CAD use is associated with significantly higher rates of breast cancer detection or early-stage invasive disease, and no study has examined whether CAD use would result in greater reduction in breast cancer mortality. Meta-analyses of CAD use that summarize all existing studies and provide a stable estimate of the influence of CAD on mammography accuracy suggest that CAD use may increase cancer detection while at the same time significantly increasing false-positive testing. Based on meta-analytic summary estimates, if 100 000 screened women have CAD used in the interpretation of mammography, CAD will yield an additional 50 breast cancer diagnoses (0.05% of women screened) and 1190 falsepositive screening examinations of healthy women (1.2% of women screened), and result in 80 biopsies of healthy women (0.08% of women screened). Although it has been 12 years since the FDA approved CAD, there is not agreement on whether there is benefit in CAD use when interpreting mammography. On the one hand, it has been stated that the preponderance of evidence supports CAD use with screening mammography interpretation, and on the other hand, there is concern that CAD can be detrimental to mammography interpretation if not used correctly. In 2005, the Institute of Medicine report “Improving Breast Imaging Quality Standards” stated “More information is needed about CAD in practice—with special attention to how such factors as interpreting physician experience, lesion characteristics, practice settings, and specific CAD algorithms affect CAD performance— before it can be concluded that it will generally improve interpretation.” Computer-aided detection is just one example of a new technology being approved by the FDA (then covered by private insurers and/or Medicare) and used in large numbers of individuals before proven to be more effective than existing technology in clinical practice or in a well designed randomized controlled trial (RCT). Another example of technology creep is the dissemination of thinprep Papanicolaou tests. Thin-prep Papanicolaou tests were approved by the FDA in 1996 and marketed as having increased sensitivity to detect cervical dysplasia compared with conventional Papanicolaou tests but lower specificity. After FDA approval, insurance companies were willing to pay considerably higher fees for liquid-based smears than for conventional smears. That coverage drove a rapid conversion to this new technology in the US market-driven health care system. In 2009, an RCT showed that the accuracy to detect dysplasia is equal between conventional and liquid-based cytology; in other words, liquid-based cytology is not superior to conventional cytology as marketed by companies. Nonetheless, liquidbased cytology has almost completely replaced conventional cytology. Liquid-based cytology costs more than conventional cytology, and, because of the materials and expensive equipment required for analysis of specimens and because laboratories find the specimens more efficient to process, liquid-based cytology will probably continue to be used in the United States for cervical cancer screening despite the higher cost of thin-prep Papanicolaou tests compared with conventional cytology.
