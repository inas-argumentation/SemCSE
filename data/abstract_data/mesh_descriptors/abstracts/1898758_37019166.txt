Passive Smoking and Lung Cancer: The Ipsen Lecture 1987
Definitions and composition of environmental tobacco smoke Mainstream smoke (MS) is the tobacco smoke that is generated during a puff and is drawn through the butt end into the smoker's respiratory system. Sidestream smoke (SS) comes directly from the burning end of the cigarette (or cigar or pipe). "Passive smoking", "involuntary smoking", and exposure to "environmental tobacco smoke" (ETS) are used synonymously in the literature to describe the exposure of nonsmokers to elements of tobacco smoke generated by smokers in (mostly) enclosed spaces (1, 2). Sidestream smoke is characterized by higher concentrations of many of the toxic and carcinogenic compounds found in MS, including carbon monoxide, benzene, toluene, acrolein, acetone, pyridine, ammonia, methylamine, nicotine, aniline, cadmium, benzo(a)pyrene, benz(a)anthracene and radon daughters. In animal testing, undiluted SS has been bound to be more carcinogenic than MS (2, 3-5). ETS is frequently a substantial contributor to the level of indoor air pollution concentrations of respirable suspended particulates (RSP). Particles in SS are only slightly smaller than those in MS; the mass median diameter of ETS particles is between approximately 0.2 pm and 0.4 pm. Particles of MS, however, grow in size by coagulation when a smoker draws and holds a puff of MS into the mouth, whereas particles of SS are rapidly diluted in the surrounding air and have no opportunity to grow in size (2, 6-10). Data from large analytical epidemiological studies demonstrate dose-dependent relationships between active smoking and lung cancer; there are neither theoretical reasons nor empirical data supporting the existence of a threshold value, below which lung carcinogenesis does not occur. To the extent that MS is a potent carcinogen and SS only quantitatively different from MS, an excess lung cancer risk from exposure to ETS should be expected. Nevertheless, two important questions remain. First, is the excess risk expected from longterm exposure to ETS large enough to be empirically demonstrable with existing epidemiologic techniques? second, are the epidemiologic data supportive of, rather than compatible with, an etiological role of passive smoking in lung carcinogenesis?
