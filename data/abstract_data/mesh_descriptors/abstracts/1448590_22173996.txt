Maintenance bacillus Calmette-Guérin: why is there continued debate?
The goal of scientific research is to arrive at a reasonable estimate of the truth with respect to some underlying question of importance. In clinical medical research, multiple types of study designs help point investigators toward the truth, including cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, cohort studies, clinical trials, and meta-analyses. Methodologists and proponents of evidence-based medicine have argued that there exists a pyramidal hierarchy of study designs, suggesting that certain study designs more closely approximate the truth than others [1]. Randomized trials (and meta-analyses thereof) are generally allotted the highest rank on the pyramid, whereas observational studies are relegated to lower ranks. However, little evidence supports the concept of a hierarchy of study designs, and several thoughtful analyses have found that high-quality observational studies arrive at answers similar to those of randomized trials, especially when the effect sizes are large and consistent [2]. We therefore argue that the truth can be approximated in differentways, andwe propose that consistent results across study designs are a better indicator of truth than one large randomization. Two central reasons that studies might differ in outcomes (regardless of their specific designs) are bias and chance. Although great strides have been made toward designing good studies that are less biased and that are powered to be less prone to random error, subtle selection biases will always affect trial outcomes, and trials of maintenance bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) are certainly no exception. In this issue of European Urology, Ehdaie et al. [3] highlight several important limitations of existing maintenance BCG trials, including inadequate statistical power, differing inclusion criteria, varying quality of transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT), and different ways of defining BCG failure and outcome. The question is what to do when independent clinical trials find different results: Give up, or try to make sense of the disparate data? Pooling data in a meta-analysis provides a method for increasing the statistical power (ie, decreasing the risk of a type 2 error) and an opportunity to assess factors that might explain the observed between-study heterogeneity.
