Cancer dynamics and the success of cancer screening programs
In a recent perspective, Welch and colleagues describe the similarities and differences of screening programs for breast and prostate cancer [1]. I agree with their description of cancer dynamics and the three different existing hypotheses (previously put forward by Halsted, Fisher and Hellman) [2–4]. One factor that was not covered relates to the frequency of screening. The recommended frequency is different between cancers. For example, for breast cancer the recommend frequency of screening is every 2–3 years, for prostate cancer once a year or not at all, for colon cancer every 2 years and for cervical cancer every 3–5 years. The frequency relates to the disease biology but also to other factors such as cost, availability of a noninvasive test, and associated harms. The attached Figure 1 describes six hypothetical patients and applies to all cancers of different disease biology and dynamics. Patient 1 represents an individual whose screening biomarker increases slowly over time but does not cross the cut-off for a positive result. Patients 2 and 3 represent individuals whose biomarker values show positivity in screening cycles 4 and 3, respectively, but the rate of biomarker increase is low and these patients may never present with clinical or metastatic disease. These two patients fit with prostate or breast cancer patients who have localized disease and slow growing tumors (also known as indolent), and ideally, they should not be captured by screening, because a positive result may lead to overtreatment. Patients 4 and 5 are individuals who are likely to benefit from screening, since the biomarker will become positive in screening cycle 2 but there is a significant window of opportunity for early therapy, before the cancer becomes clinical or metastatic. For these patients, screening will be associated with stage migration (decrease of incidence of metastatic disease), as seen with prostate cancer [1]. Patient 6 will likely not benefit from screening since when the biomarker becomes positive, the cancer has already clinical manifestations or is metastatic. This patient represents a typical breast cancer patient with invasive carcinoma. For these patients, screening will not cause a stage migration (no change in incidence of metastatic disease), as is the case with breast cancer [1].
