Cardiovascular risk reduction in patients with severe mental illness.
Health care reform, as outlined in the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act, will provide many opportunities for cardiovascular nurses to focus on the management of chronic disease, much of which consists of optimizing cardiovascular risk reduction strategies in the management of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. There will be ample opportunity to address the 6 quality improvement dimensions in our healthcare system as referenced in the Institute of Medicine’s landmark report Crossing the Quality Chasm: safety, effectiveness, patient centeredness, timeliness, efficiency, and equity. A model of primary care that champions the use of systematic, patient-centered, coordinated care management processes, the PatientCentered Medical Home, is growing, with 2189 practices having achieved designation by the National Center for Quality Assurance, one of several certifying organizations, as of May 2011. The year 2012 will see the launch of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Accountable Care Organization pilot project. As a consequence of the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act and these associated initiatives, many persons who have previously experienced obstacles to accessing high-quality healthcare will now have better access to systems that are poised to deliver coordinated, evidence-based, patient-centered care. One of these underserved populations is persons with severe mental illness (SMI), in particular patients with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. It has long been recognized that those with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder die prematurely, on average 10 to 20 years earlier than the general population. Although, intuitively, suicide and trauma constitute a portion of premature death for many of these patients, physical illnesses account for 75% of mortality. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the most common cause of death. The prevalence of diabetes is high; the components of the metabolic syndrome are common in this population and are noted at an earlier age than in the general population. The reasons for this are complex. In 2009, the European Psychiatric Association, supported by the European Association for the Study of Diabetes and the European Society of Cardiology, published a position statement on CVD and diabetes in those with SMI. The report demonstrates that a great deal of the excess risk among those with SMI can be attributed to lifestyle factors. It is known that there is a high prevalence of cigarette smoking (nearly 80% of those with SMI are tobacco dependent; those with SMI and addictions tend to be heavier smokers, consuming 44% of all cigarettes smoked in the United States), a tendency toward unhealthy convenience food, and sedentary lifestyles. A genetic predisposition may contribute, as may high levels of inflammatory markers and stress hormones in some patients with SMI.
