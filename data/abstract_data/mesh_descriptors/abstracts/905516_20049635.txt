Thrombolytic therapy in acute myocardial infarction
I have witnessed two important steps in the early development of thrombolytic therapy in acute myocardial infarction (AMI). In the late 1960s to early 1970s, the concept that the coronary thrombus was not the playmaker in AMI was quite fashionable, as pointed out byMaroo in his Historical Sketch [1]. Yet, a European Working Party led by Marc Verstraete had shown in amulticenter trial that streptokinase (SK) reduced the rate of hospital mortality from 26.3% to 18.5% [2]. The 1960–1970s was also the time of the widespread acceptance of coronary care units (CCU) as the optimal healthcare model for the treatment of AMI; after the demonstration that led to a marked reduction in hospital mortality (from 20% to 25% to as little as 10% to 15%). With this as background, in the late 1960s Dioguardi and I decided to explore the possibility to carry out in Italy the first trial of SK therapy ever conducted in CCU, to see whether or not this treatment helped further reduce the rate of mortality. Even though our department was recognized at that time as a national leader in the field of blood coagulation and thrombosis, from the onset it proved to be very difficult to enlist the participations of a large number of CCUs. The main drawbacks were the fear of bleeding complications associated with the marked fibrinolytic state induced by SK; and the difficult acceptance of our suggestion that the thrombolytic therapy could be safely carried out even in hospitals not endowed with an expert coagulation laboratory. There was also much skepticism on the role of coronary thrombi in AMI, as mentioned above; and the concept of random allotment of patients to SK or to conventional treatment (at that time, the latter was i.v. heparin followed by oral anticoagulants) was difficult to sink in. In spite of the fact that we accepted the compromise to enroll only patients who were < 70 years old at the time of their AMI, only three CCUs in the whole country ultimately participated in the trial conducted from 1969 to 1971. Three hundred and twenty-one patients (164 treated with SK and 157 with heparin alone) were enrolled. At that time, the concept of the predefinition of the sample size of a clinical trial was not well established, so that the number of patients enrolled appeared large enough to give conclusive information, because it was actually close to those of the studies published until then! There were 19 deaths in the SK group (11.6%) and 18 in the anticoagulant group (11.5%), leading to our conclusion that SK would not reduce mortality in patients with AMI treated in CCU with the dosage and selection of cases used in this trial [3]. This statement was supported by our knowledge that another multicenter trial of SK vs. heparin carried out in Australian CCUs was also showing no significant difference in mortality rate [4]. Obviously, these results would be simply interpreted, a few years later and now, as inconclusive because of an inadequate sample size. Nevertheless, the SK trial helped make more confident on the thrombolytic agents Italian cardiologists, to introduce them to the concept and practice of randomized clinical trials and hence to pave the way to the resurgence of thrombolysis in the 1980s and to the seminal GISSI study [5]. I had the opportunity to be involved in the early phases of development of GISSI by the Italian Association of Hospital Cardiologists (AMCO) and by its charismatic leader, Fausto Rovelli. AMCO, with Rovelli et al., had the unquestionable merit to enlist the almost complete participation in GISSI of Italian CCUs that led to the enrollment of as many as 11 806 patients in one of the first megatrials of modern clinical cardiology. The Mario Negri Institute had the equally important merit to design a trial of stunning simplicity, so that it could be handled even by inexperienced clinical cardiologists. Another unique, almost unprecedented feature of GISSI was that it was the fruit of the voluntary efforts of all the involved parties, with very little on no external funds and trivial support by the commercial manufacturer of SK. My role and that of the Angelo Bianchi Bonomi Hemophilia and Thrombosis Center were only those of providing a 24-h phone service for calls coming from participating centers that were asking advice in case of hemorrhagic problems. In conclusion, my memoirs as a witness of the early development of thrombolytic therapy in AMI emphasize how the mistakes naively made in the Italian SK trial helped create among our cardiologists a background of knowledge that was pivotal for the successful design and performance of GISSI-1, a milestone in the field of modern clinical trials.
