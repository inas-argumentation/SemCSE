Gellin explained that such use could allow public health officials to more accurately monitor vaccination rates and identify groups who have not been vaccinated. Such tools could also be used to quickly identify adverse events associated with vaccines and provide more information on which subgroups might be susceptible. William Schaffner, MD, chair of the department of preventive medicine at Vanderbilt University in Nashville, Tenn, and former member of the board of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, said the plan is remarkably comprehensive. One particularly important element in today’s environment, Schaffner said, is the focus on enhancing the vaccine safety system. He noted that since the last plan, an uptick in vaccine skepticism has required physicians to spend more time explaining the risks and benefits of vaccines. “Every pediatrician will tell you their practice has changed,” Schaffner said. He noted this safety focus includes research and development on new and improved vaccines, better monitoring of safety signals, and efforts to identify in advance which patients might experience adverse events. Schaffner also applauded the plan’s emphasis on better communication with the public about vaccines. He explained that as more vaccines are introduced, particularly those targeting specific groups, it will be vital to make clear to the public why such vaccines are important and to put the risks in context. “Helping the average person assess the risks and benefits has to be part of our comprehensive approach,” he said. Schaffnernoted that thenewplanemphasizes theneed for theUnitedStates to be involved in global vaccine efforts. He explainedthattheUnitedStateshasanobligation topromoteglobalpublichealth, and that because diseases can be transported quickly around the world, doing so helps to improve US public health. “Our plan cannot stop at the borders of this country,” he said. Gellin said the next step will be to create an implementation plan and establish benchmarks for measuring the progress of the vaccine plan. This process should be completed by the end of the year, he said. Already, the Institute of Medicine has been asked to determine which vaccines of domestic or global importance should receive priority as enhanced research and development efforts move forward. Gellin also emphasized that successful implementation of the plan will require a coordinated effort among various government agencies and other stakeholders. In fact the plan specifies which agencies or groups are responsible for each of the plan’s goals. “We are trying to ensure that everyone recognizes their responsibilities moving forward,” he said.
