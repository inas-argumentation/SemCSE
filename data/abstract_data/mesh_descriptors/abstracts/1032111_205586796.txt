Congenital Bednar tumour (pigmented dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans)

Dermatological examination revealed wrinkling, irregular pigmentation, multiple actinic keratosis, significant telangiectasias and marked atrophy. On questioning, the patient later admitted she had been using a fluocinolone ointment once a day for months. An open-spray cryotherapy was used to treat the actinic keratosis. On administration of the spray, we observed the development of a blister in the treated area, containing air that easily moved with pressure. Soft tissue swelling was readily visible, and crepitus was palpated diffusely (fig. 1). The effect improved modestly over the next hour, and the patient was sent home with the reassurance that the air infiltration would continue to dissipate. The swelling resolved within the next 24 h. No complications were observed. Based on the nature of the swellings, the published cryotherapy complications, and the rapid resolution of the swellings without any specific treatment, a clinical diagnosis of subcutaneous emphysema was made. Subcutaneous emphysema is a rare and seldom reported complication associated with the use of liquid nitrogen spray. Any type of compressed gas has the potential to insufflate tissue. The association between compressed nitrogen gas causing tissue insufflation has been reported as a complication of cutaneous cryotherapy, but it is rare. Contrary to other causes of subcutaneous emphysema, this cryotherapy complication is benign and self-resolving, although startling to the physician and patient. The literature states that a benign course is expected despite the risk of infection due to microbes carried along with the injection of compressed air into the subcutaneous tissue. Subcutaneous emphysema has been described in connection with the use of sprayed liquid nitrogen for treating actinic keratosis on fragile elderly skin. Our case illustrates this rare complication of cryotherapy in a patient with frail and atrophic skin due to chronic topical corticosteroid application. In coincidence with Lambert et al., we believe that patients with atrophic skin are at particular risk for this cryotherapy complication. Therefore, topical corticosteroid application should be ill advised weeks before cryotherapy. Photodynamic therapy has emerged as a novel and versatile method of treating actinic keratosis and might be a better option than cryotherapy in those patients with important corticosteroid-induced skin atrophy.
