“Operative” use of Steroids in Head and Neck Surgery
Although controlled, double-blind studies of the operative use of steroids in head and neck surgery are few, there are numerous reported uses of steroids in head and neck surgery. In this article we will review the scant literature on the use of steroids in intraoral, nasal, laryngeal, and cutaneous surgery. Steroids have been used (with conflicting results) alone or in combination with other drugs to reduce postoperative tonsillectomy pain. Smith et a1 . I reported that a tonsillar fossa injection of a mixture of a steroidpenicillinlocal anesthetic immediately after tonsillectomy relieved pain and reduced both immediate and long-term inflammation. Rundle,' however, concluded in a separate report that intramuscular injection of penicillin G procaine (Depo-penicillin) was just as efficacious in the reduction of pain or morbidity. Papangelou" reported on 480 tonsillectomy patients (ages 13 to 56). 323 of whom were given steroids (betamethasone16~-methyl-9a-fluoroprednisolone) and analgesics, while 157 were treated with analgesics alone. He administered 0.5 mg betamethasone every 6 hours on the first postoperative day, every 8 hours on the second postoperative day, every 12 hours on the third day, and one tablet on the fourth day. The patients treated with betamethasone and analgesics showed significant pain reduction on days 1 through 3 when compared with those receiving only analgesics. There was no difference in hemorrhage or infection between the two groups. However, in a double-blind study Anderson et al .4 used methylprednisolone acetate (DepoMedrol) as an injection (20 mg) into each tonsillar fossa. No significant difference was found in any parameter studied (pain, difficulty swallowing, resumption of solid food, or day of most pain). Steroids may be used in the therapy of problems in the upper aerodigestive and respiratory tract. Their use in laryngeal edema (croup and epiglottitis) will be reviewed by another author. However, in caustic burns of the esophagus they are thought to be beneficial .,i.'i Ashcraft and Holder' found that intralesional injections of triamcinolone in experimentally induced esophageal strictures in mongrel dogs resulted in marked reduction in the amount of scar tissue formation. They emphasize that very short strictures are the only ones amenable to this therapy. These same authors also report good results with such injections in clinical trials.',# In trauma to the larynx and subglottic trachea there is strong disagreement as to the benefit of systemically administered steroids in the prevention of subglottic stenosis. Skolnick and Tardys believe that steroids have not been proved effective in the prevention of the stenosis, whereas Hawkins"' uses them routinely. Miles et al. , I ' in an experimental study of the therapy of acute laryngeal fractures, found that their data (in a small series) favored the steroid-treated group in the reduction of granulation tissue. Their conclusion was that steroid administration is effective in open-reduction surgery when given in the first 24 hours after injury. Croft et a1 . I y used a steroid-antibiotic (prednisonepenicillin) regimen to successfully prevent subglottic chondritis and its resultant stenosis both in the laboratory (beagle dogs) and in two patients. Thus it would appear from experimental work that steroids may be useful in the prevention of posttraumatic sequelae in the larynx. Although there has been much discussion about the use of steroids as an adjunct to rhinoplastic surgery, most written material is based on personal experience and clinical impressions.
