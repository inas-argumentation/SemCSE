Practical implications of choice of test in National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE) guidance for the prevention of Type 2 diabetes
Recently published guidelines from the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence recommend using a two-stage approach to identify those at high risk of developing Type 2 diabetes: screening using a validated self-assessment questionnaire or computer-based risk assessment tool, followed (in those with a high risk score) by a blood test for either HbA1c or fasting glucose [1]. The presence of a fasting glucose level of 5.5–6.9 mmol/l or HbA1c 42–47 mmol/mol categorizes a person as high risk for progression to Type 2 diabetes. We assessed the practical impact of this screening strategy in a cohort of 2253 participants aged over 40 years, and not known to have diabetes, recruited from the Exeter 10000 study. Participants were predominantly identified through primary care records. Fasting blood was taken for glucose and HbA1c. Two suggested risk scores were calculated in all participants—the Leicester Risk Assessment [2] and the Cambridge Risk Assessment [3]. Recommended cut-offs of 16 for the Leicester Risk Assessment and 0.128 for the Cambridge Risk Assessment were used [2,3]. Participant characteristics were median (interquartile range) age 59 (50–66) years, BMI 26.0 (23.7–28.9) kg/m, HbA1c 39 (37–41) mmol/mol, fasting glucose 5.0 (4.7–5.3) mmol/l, 36% male, 99% Caucasian. Undiagnosed diabetes was present in 68 (3.0%) by HbA1c criteria and 32 (1.4%) by fasting glucose, and prevalence of ‘high risk’ of Type 2 diabetes was 18.2% by HbA1c and 18.4% by fasting glucose. Both risk scores performed equally well and identified similar people (Fig. 1). Fifty-two per cent of participants were positive on one or both tests (Leicester 38%, Cambridge 48%). Ninety-two per cent of participants positive on the Leicester Risk Assessment were also positive on the Cambridge Risk Assessment. While both tests had similar performance for detecting those with diabetes or at high risk [area under receiver operating characteristic curves 0.66 (Leicester) and 0.65 (Cambridge) for HbA1c defined risk], the Cambridge Risk Assessment was more sensitive and less specific (68.6 and 56.9% vs. 62.1 and 69.1% for the Leicester Risk Assessment). Using the Leicester Risk Assessment initial risk assessment, followed by HbA1c in those with a Leicester Risk Assessment score 16 would mean that, of the 38% of screened patients undergoing blood testing, 29% would be ‘high risk’ (HbA1c 42–47 mmol/mol) and 7% would have Type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 48 mmol/mol). Of those with a Leicester Risk Assessment score < 16, 12% had HbA1c in the range 42–47 mmol/mol and 0.9% would have Type 2 diabetes. Overall, 59% of participants with HbA1c in the range 42–47 mmol/mol and 82% of those with undiagnosed Type 2 diabetes would be detected using this approach. Although prevalence of biochemical ‘high risk’ in those with high risk score was similar, whether defined by HbA1c (30%) or fasting glucose (28%), only half of those classified as high risk by one test would have received this classification had the other test been chosen (Fig. 1). It is unlikely that both tests will perform similarly in predicting future risk if they identify different populations. A recent publication by Heianza and colleagues in this journal has analysed the relationship between similar HbA1c and fasting glucose cutoffs and the development of Type 2 diabetes (defined by HbA1c or fasting glucose or self reported) in a large Japanese cohort and found that HbA1c in the range 42–46 mmol/mol has far lower sensitivity (30%) but higher specificity (98%) than a fasting glucose cut-off of 5.6–6.9 mmol/l (sensitivity 78%, specificity 76%) [4].
