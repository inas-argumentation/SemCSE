Septic Thrombophlebitis of the Superior Mesenteric Vein: An Unusual Complication of Appendicitis
A 19-year-old black man presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of right lower quadrant pain with associated fevers, anorexia, and diarrhea consistent with acute appendicitis. At presentation, the patient was afebrile with a white cell count (WBC) elevated to 14,800/mL (25% bandemia) and a total bilirubin of 6.4 mg/dL with a predominant indirect component of 4.9 mg/dL. Serum haptoglobin was 225 mg/dL. Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a thick-walled appendix with surrounding soft tissue stranding and a small focus of extraluminal air, consistent with appendicitis with concern for small perforation. The patient was taken to the operating room where he underwent an uncomplicated laparoscopic appendectomy. Postoperatively, he was brought to the ward where he was recovering well and had a decrease in his WBC to 8700/mL by postoperative day (POD) 1. Later that day, approximately 16 hours postoperatively, he became acutely febrile to 39.8°C, hypoxic (PaO2 of 43 mmHg, O2 saturation 82%), tachypneic, and tachycardic to 140 beats/min. The patient was emergently transferred to the intensive care unit for impending respiratory failure. His chest radiograph revealed bilateral pulmonary edema and an arterial blood gas with a PaO2/FiO2 of 118. Lower extremity ultrasound and a V/Q scan were negative for deep venous thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, respectively. The patient was placed on broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungal coverage with Zosyn, vancomycin, metronidazole, and micafungin. Persistent fevers to above 40°C and ongoing sepsis without source control prompted a repeat computed tomography scan on POD 2. This revealed trace fluid in the right paracolic gutter with concern for thrombus in the superior mesenteric vein (SMV). Gas seen in the SMV was suggestive of septic thrombophlebitis. Blood cultures subsequently grew pan-sensitive Escherichia coli and the antibiotics were tailored accordingly. The presence of SMV thrombosis in this otherwise healthy man raised our concern for an underlying hypercoagulable state. The patient was tested for Jak2 (evaluate for myeloproliferative disorders associated with SMV thrombosis), anticardiolipin antibodies, homocysteinemia, and for possible deficiency of Protein C and S. His workup revealed homocysteine elevated to 19 mmol/L (reference, 0–13 mmol/L), but otherwise the laboratory values were within normal parameters. Antithrombin III was 61 per cent (reference 73 to 132%), which was low but not enough to have been clinically significant. Nonetheless, the patient showed evidence of marked heparin resistance with partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 29 seconds despite heparin infusion exceeding 2000 units/hr. Antithrombin III was administered, which assisted in achieving PTT of 60 to 90 seconds. The true etiology of heparin resistance remained unclear. The patient’s fevers resolved on POD 7 and he was transitioned to Coumadin and was discharged home on Coumadin along with 3 weeks of Augmentin. Appendectomy is one of the most common emergent surgical procedures performed with over a quarter million operative cases per year in the United States alone.1 Early operative intervention has long been favored by surgeons to minimize postoperative morbidity and mortality. Although postoperative morbidity and mortality after appendectomy are rare, serious complications can occur and must be considered whenever the postoperative course does not progress as planned. The majority of postoperative surgical morbidity is infectious in nature, including surgical site infections as well as intra-abdominal fluid collections and abscesses. Postoperative bowel obstruction is rare but always remains a potential concern after any abdominal operation.
