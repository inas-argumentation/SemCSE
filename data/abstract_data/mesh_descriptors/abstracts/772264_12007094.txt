The challenge of obesity during pregnancy: When to intervene and what could work?
Over the last few years the number of papers submitted to the journal presenting findings from studies of women who were overweight or obese in pregnancy has steadily increased. Findings have been presented from quantitative studies of lifestyle interventions and qualitative studies which explored the views and experiences of women and health care professionals. More recently, attention has turned to the impacts of excessive gestational weight gain (GWG) among women who had a normal Body Mass Index (BMI) at pregnancy commencement. Although studies have been undertaken in different countries and health care settings, perhaps not unsurprisingly most studies to date have been undertaken in high income countries, where up to half of the women of reproductive age are overweight or obese (Ogden et al., 2006; Thangaratinam et al., 2012). From a public health perspective, the burden of obesity for the woman and her family, the impact on health care resources and complexity of factors associated with lifestyle choices means that an effective and sustainable intervention may not be easy to identify. However, the need to tackle weight management during and after pregnancy is clear. Women who are overweight or obese have an increased risk of common pregnancy disorders including gestational diabetes, are more likely to require birth interventions, to have heavier birthweight babies and are less likely to breast feed. Their infants are at risk of higher BMI and blood pressure in childhood and young adulthood (Poston et al., 2011). Excessive GWG is linked with increased susceptibility to retain and gain weight postnatally, with longer-term health implications for the woman. We now know that even relatively modest increases in BMI can impact on perinatal outcomes in subsequent pregnancies (Villamor and Cnattingius, 2006). Failure to lose weight within 6 months of giving birth is an important predictor of future weight gain and obesity, hypertension, diabetes and degenerative joint disease (Renjilian et al., 2001; Rooney et al., 2005; Stubbs et al., 2011; van der Pligt et al., 2013) and poorer health behaviours including smoking, dietary choices and lack of regular exercise (George et al., 2005; Hilson et al., 2006; Poston et al., 2011). How best to support women of reproductive age to manage their weight, and the optimal time to support them to do this, remains a challenge. In the UK, current guidance is that pregnant women should only be weighed at their first antenatal consultation unless there is concern about their clinical condition or nutritional status (NICE, 2010). Adherence to the Institute of Medicine (IoM) guidance for pregnancy weight gain (Rasmussen and Yaktine, 2009) is not recommended in the UK given the lack of evidence from large intervention studies underpinning IoM guidance and lack of evidence of appropriate weight-gain ranges in pregnancy for a UK population (NICE, 2010). Trials to date have mainly considered outcomes of pregnancy interventions and few have found significant differences, although some differences in GWG have been reported (Thangaratinam et al., 2012). The LIMIT trial from Australia (Dodd et al., 2014b) which recruited over 2000 overweight or obese women found that a lifestyle intervention which focused on diet and physical activity was associated with a reduction in number of macrosomic babies born to women allocated to the intervention, but as this was a prespecified secondary outcome, a chance finding cannot be ruled out. Reasons postulated for this difference included greater physical activity in the intervention arm and differences in dietary intake including a reduction in energy intake from saturated fat and an increase in dietary fibre (Dodd et al., 2014a). The results of recently completed trials of pregnancy weight management interventions in the UK are currently awaited. The three papers relevant to pregnancy weight management in this issue add to our current knowledge, with the caveat that they are relatively small studies and none are based on findings from a randomised controlled trial. Merkx et al. (2015) from the Netherlands explored GWG in healthy women in relation to prepregnancy BMI, diet and physical activity in a cross-sectional study of womenwho received antenatal care from an independent midwife. Of the 455 women surveyed, 42% gained weight in pregnancy in line with IOM guidance (Rasmussen and Yaktine, 2009), 14% below the guidance and 44% above recommended guidance. After considering a range of variables relevant to maternal characteristics, diet, and activity levels, decline in physical activity was the only modifiable factor associated with weight gain above recommended IOM guidance. That all women, regardless of BMI at pregnancy commencement, should be offered information on how to achieve a healthy GWG is an important message. Haby et al from Sweden (Haby et al., 2015) present data from an antenatal pilot study which compared outcomes of an intervention delivered by midwives which aimed to limit weight gain in obese pregnant women (BMIZ30). The first 50 enrolled pregnant women who met inclusion criteria received the intervention and were matched with 50 pregnant women who formed the controls (based on age, parity and BMI). The intervention comprised 60 minutes of additional time with the midwife, opportunity to attend food discussion groups or receive individualised dietary advice, and prescribed physical activity.
