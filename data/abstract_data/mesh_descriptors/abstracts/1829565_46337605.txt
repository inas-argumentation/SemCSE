Is vaccine therapy a renewed strategic approach for non-small-cell lung cancer therapy?
Lung cancer, accounting for approximately 1.6 million new diagnoses, is the most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with 1.3 million deaths worldwide in 2008 [101]. Non-smallcell lung cancer (NSCLC) represents more than 80% of lung cancer cases. Despite progress in NSCLC research, there is still no cure. Currently, based on disease stage at diagnosis, primary treatment of NSCLC involves surgery, radiotherapy, cytotoxic and targeted drugs. Since most patients have advanced disease at diagnosis, systemic therapy is the mainstay of management. Despite these treatments and apart from the discovery of markers to be used in vivo for the therapy of a small percentage of selected advanced NSCLC patients [1,2], no major advances have been observed for decades. Immunotherapy, an old concept, is now giving new insights into the treatment of many cancers, including NSCLC, because of the identification of antigens and cytokines, the molecular events that trigger oncogenesis and the improved understanding of the immune system. Despite NSCLC not being considered to be immunogenic, identifying lung tumor antigens and presenting them in the optimal context may enable the immune system to generate anti-lung-tumor effector cells, which are usually absent. To date, no vaccine has been demonstrated to be useful for NSCLC treatment. In fact, although several vaccines have already been investigated in this disease, the promising results coming from small Phase II trials have not translated into successful Phase III trials [3]. Several explanations for these unsuccessful attempts have been suggested. The methodologies used for testing chemotherapeutics may not be appropriate in evaluating the effects of vaccine therapy due to the lack of technologies able to evaluate these outcomes. When vaccines are investigated in combination with chemotherapy, we should evaluate not only the typical end points of standard therapy, such as response rate and survival, but also those related to vaccine treatment, such as the immunologic response. In this way, based on this last specific outcome, we could better address the treatments in vivo. The availability of tumor tissue allows for the selection and the verification of the exact antigens to target, because the administration of a vaccine therapy simply on a theoretical basis is difficult. Thus, resected patients are the ideal candidates. However, the recent need for adequate amounts of tumor sample tissue also from advanced stage disease, to perform biomarker analysis to address the most appropriate treatment [2,3], should also help for an adequate analysis of the vaccination approaches.
