Antibiotic trials for community-acquired pneumonia.
The Comment by Kim Mulholland and colleagues about methodological challenges with the design of antibiotic trials for community-acquired pneumonia brings up some interesting points that have been raised in the past. However, we are concerned about the conclusions they draw on the basis of selective examples they cite. First, they report that the trials of treatment for pneumonia with chest indrawing but no danger signs that were used to change WHO casemanagement policy were based on data that are not globally representative. We disagree. The change was based on a systematic literature review that included trials from many countries worldwide such as the cited NO-SHOTS trial (in Pakistan), but also the noncited APPIS trial (in eight countries in Africa, Asia, and Latin America), and the PIVOT trial (in the UK), among others. The other studies the authors cite (in Hala and in Haripur, Pakistan) were not considered for the guidelines change, since they were not published at the time of the expert review, and these were trials of community (not facility) case management. Second, they describe issues with antibiotic efficacy studies that rely on non-specifi c criteria (ie, chest-wall indrawing) for bacterial pneumonia diagnosis without addressing the debate on how best to diagnose bacterial pneumonia. They suggest chest radiology as a more specific alternative, without addressing its well described limitations. To achieve consensus readings of childhood chest radiographs is diffi cult and inconsistent. Third, they assert the pneumonia trials excluded the sickest patients, as shown by low mortality rates (<5%) compared with 9–11% reported elsewhere. However, the cited studies with increased mortality include cases with very severe disease and danger signs, a level of severity excluded from our studies because it always requires hospital admission. In the Yemen study, more than 50% of children had baseline cyanosis whereas in the Indian study, mortality in patients with very severe disease was nearly 50%. The South African study was done in a setting with high HIV prevalence before widespread HIV prevention and treatment access, in which high mortality is expected. Nonetheless, mortality was only 1·2% in children non-infected with HIV. Fourth, they invoke the well known Pollyanna Phenomenon, which asserts that antibiotic trials can be biased towards the null when including viral cases, erroneously making two antibiotics seem equivalent even if the standard of care is superior. Although we acknowledge this issue, a previous report by Fox and Fox provided an indepth description of why these trials are less susceptible to this phenomenon. By focusing on this phenomenon the authors miss the point of these trials and the WHO policy. The phenomenon applies when the question is whether two drugs are equivalent for treatment of a bacterial disorder. This question has no relevance to the treatment of chest indrawing pneumonia in outpatient settings because it is not possible, in view of the scarcity of microbiology facilities, to distinguish between bacterial and viral pneumonia. Rather, the somewhat poor specifi city of the WHO diagnostic algorithm is accepted to permit use of lifesaving antibiotic treatment for true bacterial cases with the acknowledged risk of overtreatment. Moreover, referral for care (and parenteral antibiotics) is often times not accepted. Thus, the appropriate question is a policy question—ie, how to treat children diagnosed with pneumonia in the context of poor-resource settings in which laboratory diagnostic facilities are generally not available. We feel that the authors have used selective medical literature biased towards their conclusions and have ignored more relevant studies. It is disappointing that the authors, while criticising the neonatal sepsis studies that assessed treatment that could be given in clinics or outpatient settings for babies who cannot access in-patient care, they do not suggest any alternatives for these babies. Mulholland and colleagues suggest that the only valid studies would recruit babies with a high probability of having bacterial infection (for which currently there is no standard approach) and have an endpoint of mortality. However, such studies would require tens of thousands of babies to be studied, would take years, and the results would have no practical relevance to caregivers and, more importantly, to the children who cannot access hospital care.
