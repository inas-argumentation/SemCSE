Adjuvant mitomycin C in functional endoscopic sinus surgery
I read with interest the recent article from Venkatraman et al. [1] regarding the use of mitomycin C(MMC) in functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) for the treatment of chronic rhinosinusitis. As seen in other specialities, these off-label antimetabolites were employed in this study to ascertain whether the success rate of the procedure would have improved. In fact, MMC has been demonstrated that it has an antiproliferative effect on the mucosal fibroblast and decrease product secretion for scar formation [2][3]. While the authors correctly stated that a common reason for failure in FESS is the development of adhesions obliterating the middle meatus, it should also be stressed that a further important reason is represented by the antrostomy size [4], whose effect was not investigated in this study. Although the authors achieved a commendably high surgical success rate (100 %) at 6 months with the MMC, it must be noted that they also found no difference between the procedures without MMC and the cases that were treated with MMC. I praise the authors for their efforts in limiting confounding factors by adopting strict inclusion criteria, limiting the study to primary FESS, and studying a single group of patients with bilateral and equal chronic rhinosinusitis, treated with the same surgical technique which employed the adjunctive MMC in the treated arm only.Hence, the authors stated that their ‘‘study is comprehensive and representative of the results of mitomycin C in FESS’’. In truth, it should be highlighted that the extension of the disease was not graded on the basis of the CT scan, as previously described and commonly performed [5]. In addition, the authors failed to provide information regarding the underlying causes of the chronic rhino-sinusitis cases included: allergies, asthma, nasal polyps, hereditary tendencies, and environmental factors (such as smoking) influence the prognosis in different ways [6]. It would also be useful for the authors to provide details regarding the antrostomy size they routinely adopt or other technical or therapeutical details they believe determinant in achieving a such high success rate, which was independent from the usage of MMC. Although the authors concluded that their study showed that ‘‘the use of topical Mitomycin C can help reduce the incidence of synechiae in the early post-operative period’’, given that all the treatments resulted in absence of symptoms at the last follow-up examination independently of the adoption of MMC and that this consists in an off-label treatment, I would challenge the authors to agree that the study findings would clearly advice the reader against the mitomycin C adoption in FESS for chronic rhinosinusitis.
