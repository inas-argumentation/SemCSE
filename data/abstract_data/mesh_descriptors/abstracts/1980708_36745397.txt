More functionally isolable subsystems but fewer “modules”?
For nearly thirty years information-processing models have been used by psychologists. For as long, with some rare exceptions (e.g., Morton, 1981; Newell and Simon, 1972) there has been remarkably little concern, about the assumptions implicit in the conceptual framework. Theorists have used a sir.lilar box-and-arrow notation to express theoretical positions of very different sorts. Fodor’s book The Modularity of Mind (1983) is likely to bring some improvement in this muddled state of affairs. For the concept of the “module” has been articulated by Fodor in a powerful and plausible fashion, much as ‘paradigm was by Kuhn (1962) in the Structure of Scientific Revolutions. It is likely to become a standard part of the vocabulary of cognitive science. Any information-processing theory which is stated in terms of “boxes” corn-patible with Fodor’s modules will probably be interpreted with the boxes having the properties of modules unless this interpretation is explicitly rejected. Fodor, therefore, has at the very least done cognitive psychology a major service by forcing it to consider much more seriously what the ‘boxes’ of ‘box-and-arrow’ notation represent. The appeal of his account stems from the plausibility of some of his central concepts such as his version of ‘module’ itself and ‘vertical faculty’. However it also rests on his taking a whole series of strong positions which fit very neatly with each other; this makes the argument very elegant. In addition the book is very short. Fodor’s use of this type of approach may, however, worry many readers, particularly those that have worked experimentally in some of the areas to which he briefly refers. If one is brief, many things of theoretical interest can only be said if one i’s not too concerned about facts, or at least about presenting detailed arguments about the empirical findings in any given field.
