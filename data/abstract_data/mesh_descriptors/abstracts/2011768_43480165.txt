HIV antibodies and athletes
The viruses that persist in humans despite our immune responses do so either by hiding or by running from the immune system [1]. The longstanding pessimism about the prospects for these viruses being controlled by vaccines has been relieved somewhat by recent results, with a focus on achieving the main prize, control of AIDS. Many immunological responses have been put forward as candidates for providing protection against persistent viruses. My perspective is that this represents hubris on behalf of the advocates, because the truth is that we lack the scientific information required to propose pharmacokinetic studies of any particular immune response. Instead, we should use the ability of an immunogen to control viral replication as a pharmacodynamic read-out of its potency and thereby identify the nature of the protective response without making prior assumptions. There have been some encouraging results for my favourite virus CMV as well as for HIV, implicating humoral immunity in both cases, with the possibility that mechanisms other than neutralisation, such as antibody dependent cellular cytotoxicity, may be important [2–6]. There is thus the possibility of identifying common features between a virus that hides and one that runs from the immune system, together with a focus on the function of B lymphocytes despite the affected patients having overt disease onlywhen the function of T lymphocytes is profoundly impaired. Although additional studies on mechanisms such as antibody dependent cellular cytotoxicity are awaited [7–10], the recent focus has been on neutralisation of HIV. Broadly neutralising antibodies are found in few people with HIV infection and take years to appear [11,12]. Their genetic lineage can now be retraced to observe serial rounds of recombination and somatic mutation of the complementarity determining regions responsible for interacting with the target epitope [13–15].
