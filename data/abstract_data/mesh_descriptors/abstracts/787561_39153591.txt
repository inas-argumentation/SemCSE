Epidemiologic findings on body mass and blood pressure in adults.
Univariate Cross-sectional Data Data have been accumulating for decades on the positive relationship between body weight (controlled for height) and blood pressure (BP) in adults. A comprehensive review was published in 1969 (1). It noted 31 papers in the literature on this matter, published between 1923 and 1967. Data from analyses of the univariate cross-sectional type have been reported in extenso, including by our group, e.g., for middle-aged men of the People’s Gas Company Study in Chicago (2-5). In 1973-75, the Community Hypertension Evaluation Clinic (CHEC) survey, initiated in cooperation with the National High Blood Pressure Education Program, screening about one million Americans all over the country, produced typical findings (Table 1) (6). In this case the independent variable was the screenee’s self-assessment of his/her weight status. This massive undertaking yielded concordant data for both men and women in two adult age ranges, 20 to 39 and 40 to 64 years. In the past it was argued by some that these findings could be artifactual because of bias in BP measurement due to fatness and consequent large circumference of the arm to which the blood pressure cuff was applied. However, additional data acquired in subsequent years-as methodology became more sophisticated-yielded similar findings with use of cuffs of various sizes to control for this problem. Further, it was also shown that indices of relative weight (RW) were associated with electrocardiography (ECG)-demonstrated left ventricular hypertrophy, one of the well-known consequences of sustained high BP (HBP). Th us, the association between RW and BP and HAP was independently validated. Since the 1969 review, many additional data sets have accumulated, yielding further confirmation of the relationship.
