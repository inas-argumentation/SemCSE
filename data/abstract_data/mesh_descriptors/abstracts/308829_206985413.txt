A commentary on “Brain vessels mummification in an individual of ancient Egypt” by Isidro et al., 2014
Isidro and colleagues present a case of solidified resin in the cranium of an anthropogenic Egyptian mummy, wherein the resin has produced a cast of the meningeal grooves e impressions of the meningeal arteries present in nearly every human endocranial surface. A detailed search of the literature would have revealed that such a finding is neither unusual nor unexpected. The authors refer to “little evidences of meningeal coverings” (p. 218) in the literature, and demonstrate a naı̈ve perception of rarity that may stem from the unfortunate lack of full, detailed description of mummified remains in many studies. Alternatively, it may simply be the case that little evidence of preserved meninges had been located prior to 1986, the date of the most recent publication that the authors provide to cite that scarcity of evidence. However, the authors' own list of published accounts of mummified meninges (Appenzeller & Aufderheide, 1999; Kim et al., 2008; Papageorgopoulou et al., 2010) and whole mummified brains (Lamb, 1901; Smith, 1902 e referring to hundreds of naturally mummified brains; Papageorgopoulou et al., 2010; Raven & Taconis, 2005; Riddle, Ho, & Chason, 1975; Scott, Horne, Hart, & Savage, 1977) demonstrates that the presence of meninges and intact mummified brains in natural and anthropogenic mummies is far from few or seldom reported. Our own recent work (Wade, Garvin, & Nelson, 2011) provides a detailed treatment of this topic, a reading beyond line seven of the third paragraph of which cites accounts of dozens of anthropogenic mummies with intact brains and four primary imaging studies that demonstrate dural remnants in both excerebrated and unexcerebrated anthropogenic mummies. It is not surprising, then, that the authors could have located a case of intact neural or meningeal tissue in an anthropogenic mummy, or, as was the case here, a resin ‘endocast’ of the meningeal vessels. The mummy to which Isidro and colleagues refer in their Letter was dated to between the Late and Ptolemaic Periods, “based on the characteristics of the mummification” (p. 217). Excerebration in anthropogenic mummies was common by the Late Period and resin poured into the crania of excerebrated individuals appears to have become increasingly voluminous by the Ptolemaic Period, likely owing to increased trade and access to imported resins (seeWade&Nelson, 2013). Therefore, it is not surprising that they should have located solidified resin in the cranium of this anthropogenic mummy. Rather than considering the implications of the position of the head at the time of solidification (cf. multiple non-parallel solidified fluid levels in Tutankhamun e Boyer, Rodin, Grey, & Connolly, 2003), Isidro and colleagues speculate vaguely about “something special” (p. 218) that occurred when the resin was poured into this cranial cavity. From the photos provided, too, the resin appears to be a fragment of a larger piece, which should show the original fluid level(s) and provide information about the volume of resin employed in this individual's mummification. Unfortunately, none of this information is provided and no new anatomical detail has been captured in this resin cast that would not have been captured in a physical or virtual endocast (cf. Charlier, Froesch, Balzeau, & HuynhCharlier, 2014: Figure 1; Wade & Nelson, 2013: Figure 2) of any human cranium.
