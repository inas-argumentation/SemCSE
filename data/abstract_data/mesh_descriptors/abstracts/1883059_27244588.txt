Hormonal control of breast cancer growth in women and rats.
Publisher Summary This chapter primarily reviews the studies carried out on the hormonal control of breast cancer growth in women and the experimental dimethylbenz(a)anthracene (DMBA)-induced mammary cancer in the rat. Although initially the DMBA-induced mammary cancer in the rat appeared to be a good model system for study of the hormonal control of mammary cancer in women, differences have emerged suggesting that they are not alike. Extensive studies in the rat model have indicated that prolactin is the predominant hormone involved in stimulating tumor growth. Despite the presence of cytosol estrogen receptors in the rat mammary tumors and the fact that estrogens can induce the synthesis of progesterone receptors blocking the estrogen receptors with an antiestrogen drug in these tumors does not interfere with prolactin-induced stimulation of tumor growth. Estrogens are probably not directly involved in stimulating tumor growth in the rat and that their effects are probably indirect by influencing the secretion of prolactin. In contrast, the administration of potent antiprolactin drugs to women with breast cancer has yielded only minor effects on tumor growth, whereas administration of antiestrogen drugs has yielded remissions approaching those of surgical hypophysectomy. The observation that the antiestrogen drug, Tamoxifen, can induce objective remissions in patients who have undergone complete hypophysectomy as documented by the absence of detectable levels of prolactin and growth hormone in the serum provides evidence that estrogens directly stimulate the growth of some human breast cancers. Estrogens have a predominant role in maintaining the growth of hormone-responsive breast cancer in women. However, in a study described in the chapter, the hypophysectomy-induced remissions in one patient who failed to benefit from antiestrogen treatment and in another who had initially responded to Tamoxifen therapy suggest that pituitary hormones may play a role in maintaining the growth of some human breast cancers.
