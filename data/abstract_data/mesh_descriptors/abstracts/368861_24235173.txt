THE PRESENT STATUS AND BACKGROUND OF ANTIBIOTICS IN THE FEEDING OF DOMESTIC ANIMALS
My topic differs from the title and main scope of this conference. Yet, in the many fascinating papers on the episomal transfer of resistance, there have been repeated references to the use of antibiotics for feeding farm animals. Therefore, it seems appropriate to give an account of the introduction and rationale of this practice which, twenty years ago, suddenly took antibiotics out of the clinic and placed them on the farm. Late in 1948, we found that crude residues from the production of chlortetracycline contained vitamin B12 and a second, unknown substance that promoted the growth of chickens. This substance was, of course, chlortetracycline itself (discussed in Reference 1). From the first, it was evident that the feeding of antibiotics produced resistant microorganisms in the digestive tract; indeed, this process takes place almost by definition because the surviving organisms have to tolerate antibiotics. Some typical experiments showed a temporary drop in numbers of intestinal bacteria for the first day or two, followed by a return to levels higher than before. What we were not prepared for was the fact that the changed and resistant flora were in some way beneficial, due either to the presence of certain new bacterial strains or the absence of certain former strains or species. It took us some time to adjust to such a concept, accustomed as everyone was to the idea of resistant pathogens. Our surprise was heightened by what we knew about the effect of sulfonamides on nutrition. Sulfonamides, when added to a purified diet for rats, can produce vitamin deficiencies when the vitamins are omitted from the diet. This is because the rat obtains certain vitamins from its intestinal flora and sulfonamides depress the biological synthesis of these vitamins. Our experiments showed that sulfonamides did not produce the growth response in normal chickens that we obtained with chlortetracycline.’ Furthermore, it was shown in many investigations that penicillin and the tetracyclines had an effect on nutrition opposite to that of the sulfonamides. One or another of these antibiotics was found to lessen the requirements of rats and other experimental animals for several vitamins and minerals. The blood levels of riboflavin and calcium in chicks were increased by feeding chl~rtetracycline.~~ Rats could actually be protected against thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency by adding penicillin or chlortetracycline to a purified deficient diet. Sulfamerazine and streptomycin had the opposite effect.73 These findings, however, did not explain the “antibiotic growth effect,” which we found took place with diets that contained more than sufficient quantities of all the known vitamins.
