FasL expressed on tumor cells may engage the Fas receptors expressed on the surfaces of antitumor immune cells, causing them to undergo apoptosis. Strand et al.5 previously reported that HepG2 cells, expressing FasL after treatment with cytostatic drugs, killed Fas-positive Jurkat lymphocytes, providing the first evidence that FasL is functional on HCC. Although the idea that tumor cells counterattack immune cells and escape from antitumor immunity has been brought to popular attention, several investigators have recently reported that the expression of FasL does not automatically confer immune privilege on a tissue; rather, under some circumstances, the presence of surface FasL has been shown to evoke an inflammatory response, resulting in tumor rejection. The function of FasL on tumor cells is the subject of hot debate today.9,10 Although experimental data derived from cell cultures and animals have revealed a variety of possibilities regarding FasL function in tumor cells, the precise assessment of Fas/FasL expression in human HCC tissues could indicate the relevance of this molecule in vivo. Fukuzawa et al.,11 in their report in this issue of the Journal of Gastroenterology, have shed light on this theme. They examined the expression of Fas and FasL in human HCC tissues, and found that Fas/FasL expression decreased in proportion to the malignancy of tumor cells. According to the concept that tumor cells become resistant to apoptosis during disease progression, their observation implies that FasL expressed on HCC may be involved in a self-regulatory mechanism of apoptosis (suicide/fratricide), rather than being involved in a counterattack against immune cells or infiltration into the liver. Recently, Ito et al.12 reported conflicting results, namely, that FasL-expressing HCCs were moderately or poorly differentiated, but not well differentiated, carcinomas. The Fas/ FasL system is one of the mechanisms by which cytotoxic T cells, as well as natural killer (NK) cells, kill target cells, including tumor cells. In addition, activated immune cells co-express Fas and FasL and thereby undergo apoptosis in an autocrine or paracrine manner, maintaining homeostasis in immune cells. Hepatocytes constitutively express Fas and are highly susceptible to Fas ligation.2 Because transformed hepatocytes also express Fas at various levels,3,4 it is possible that antitumor T and NK cells could eliminate hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells by inducing Fas-mediated apoptosis. Accumulating evidence indicates that HCC cells also express FasL, as is observed in diverse types of human cancer.5 What is the target of this ligand? In other words, what is the biological relevance of FasL expressed on HCC? There are several hypotheses that may not be mutually exclusive. First, because HCC cells often co-express Fas and FasL, they may undergo apoptosis not only induced by activated FasL-positive lymphocytes but also induced by their own FasL in an autocrine or paracrine manner.
