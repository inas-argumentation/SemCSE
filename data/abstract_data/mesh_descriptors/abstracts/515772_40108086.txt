Aberrant leukocyte common antigen expression in metastatic small cell lung carcinoma: a rare finding and a potential diagnostic pitfall.
To the Editor: We would like to draw the attention of practicing pathologists to a rare but significant aberrant expression of the immunohistochemical marker, leukocyte common antigen (LCA), in a case of metastatic small cell carcinoma. The patient was a previously healthy 75-year-old man with a long history of cigarette smoking, who presented with a nontender swelling in the right supraclavicular fossa. A computed tomography scan showed a mass in the hilum of the right lung with extensive lymphadenopathy extending from the mediastinum to the root of the neck at the same side. Although radiologic investigation revealed no lesions in the lung, it is recognized that the imaging studies often fail to detect primary small cell lung cancer. Hence, the clinical and radiologic studies of the patient favored the lung as the primary site. The patient underwent a supraclavicular lymph node biopsy. He died 12 months after the initial presentation. No autopsy was performed. Histologic appearances were of a small-blue-round-cell tumor. There was no evidence of normal residual lymph node tissue. The tumor cells were arranged in sheets with nesting in places and with a loose fibroedematous stroma (Fig. 1A). There were areas of necrosis. Cells showed mild pleomorphism, were round to ovoid with scanty, faintly eosinophilic or clear cytoplasm and indistinct cell borders. The nuclei were hyperchromatic and contained finely granular chromatin without discernible nucleoli (Fig. 1A). However, a minor population of cells had 1 or 2 small but conspicuous nucleoli. Spindling of tumor cells, peripheral palisading, molding, and crush artifact were focally present. Apoptotic debris and mitotic activity were prominent. There was an intermingling of small normal-looking lymphocytes. Tumor cells showed patchy but strong membranous reactivity for LCA (cocktail of PD7+2B11 monoclonal antibodies) (Fig. 1B), strong, sometimes dotlike staining with Cam 5.2, patchy positivity for Ber EP4 and MNF116 (Fig. 1C), and CD117 (KIT). Neuroendocrine markers (chromogranin, synaptophysin, CD56), T and B-cell markers (CD3, CD20, CD79a), and the following miscellaneous markers, AE1/AE3, EMA, 34 bE12, CD43, myeloperoxidase, TdT, BerH2, TTF1 and PLAP, were negative. Tumor cells were examined by electron microscopy following deparaffinization of the wax block. They had a rather nondescript cytoplasm, containing a few cisternae of rough endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, and sparse rounded dense granules of uncertain nature. No free cytoplasmic tonofibrils were seen, but moderate numbers of small but unambiguous demosomes, each with a plaque, an intermediate line and tonofibril tails, were present (Fig. 2). A histologic diagnosis of metastatic small cell carcinoma was made. The patient received chemotherapy for a small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) and radiotherapy. He responded well in that significant reduction in size and number of lymph nodes was noted on a repeat scan but there was residual disease in the right hilum. Although the immunohistochemical and the overall histologic features were highly suggestive of small cell carcinoma, the LCA positivity initially posed some diagnostic confusion. Carcinomas are typically LCA negative, a finding which distinguishes them from lymphomas, the latter frequently being included as a major differential diagnosis, particularly in small biopsies. Carcinomas and lymphomas are treated and followed up differently, so it is essential for histopathologists to provide an accurate diagnosis. Careful interpretation of the immunohistochemical stains, especially when unexpected results are encountered, is essential. With regard to cytokeratins, we recommend the use of both AE1/AE3 and MNF116 to detect carcinomas, as an intradepartmental audit in our laboratory indicated that some carcinomas show positivity for 1 of these markers but not the other. Moreover, some lymphomas, especially when extranodal, occasionally show cytokeratin positivity with weak or no expression of LCA, which may complicate the histologic diagnosis. With a limited panel of antibodies and without use of the multiple cytokeratins mentioned above, this case could have been misdiagnosed as a lymphoma. As for other markers, TTF-1 is usually positive in SCLC; however, up to 29% of these tumors can be negative. Consequently, TTF-1 negativity does not preclude a primary SCLC. Neuroendocrine markers are variably expressed: CD56, which is nonspecific, is usually positive, whereas up to 25% of SCLC can be negative for markers such as chromogranin A, synaptophysin, NSE, and Leu-7. Therefore, despite the negative results for the immunohistochemical markers usually expressed by SCLC, the clinicopathologic correlation indicated that the lung was the most likely primary site. Although LCA is widely used for the diagnosis of hematolymphoid neoplasms,11,12 a number of nonlymphoid tumors occasionally show immunoreactivity for this marker. These include fibroadenoma, in which the epithelial component was shown to be positive, aggressive pituitary adenoma, intestinal poorly differentiated carcinoma, seminoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, breast carcinoma, and metastatic undifferentiated carcinoma.
