Fludarabine combinations for patients with advanced marginal zone lymphomas – best treatment option or too toxic?
We read with interest the recent report of the phase 2 study of fludarabine (F) and rituximab (R) for the treatment of marginal-zone lymphomas (MZL) (Brown et al, 2009). This included 26 patients (median age 63Æ7 years (range; 31Æ4–84), five pretreated), the majority of whom had advanced disease (88% stage III/IV). Despite impressive efficacy with an overall response-rate (ORR) of 85%, 54% complete remissions (CR), progression-free survival (PFS) at 3Æ1 years of 79Æ5% and overall survival (OS) 87Æ4%, the authors conclude that FR as used in this study was unsuitable for patients with MZL due to excessive toxicity. The main toxicity was myelosuppression, with 57% of patients experiencing grade 3/4 neutropenia, however only two patients (8%) developed febrile neutropenia. Such rates of acute toxicities are generally considered acceptable with a treatment that results in high response rates and prolonged remissions. The main concern of Brown et al (2009), however, was delayed haematological toxicity. Two patients developed refractory anaemia with multilineage dysplasia (RCMD) associated with adverse cytogenetics. Two other patients developed ‘bone marrow aplasia’ 4 and 9 months after completion of FR treatment. All four died from infections. To add perspective to these data, we describe the outcomes of eight patients with MZL treated at our institution with fludarabine-based combination therapy. Patient and disease characteristics were similar to the study of Brown et al (2009): All had stage IV disease, median age 60Æ5 years (range; 47–81), four patients had previously been treated with 1–4 prior regimens. All eight patients received F (25 mg/m i.v.) and cyclophosphamide (C) (250 mg/m i.v.) days 1–3 of a 28-d cycle, seven patients additionally received R (375 mg/m i.v.) on day 1. There was no routine use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF); two patients received primary prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. The efficacy results confirmed the favourable data of fludarabine-based treatment for MZL presented by Brown et al (2009) and other groups, with an ORR of 100% and CR rate of 75%. With a median follow-up of 46 months, the median PFS was 51 months. The estimated OS rates after 3 and 5 years were 86% with only one death at 13 months after starting treatment, again demonstrating the durability of responses obtained with fludarabine combinations in MZL. In contrast to Brown et al (2009), our patients tolerated treatment very well with only one uncomplicated infusionrelated reaction during the first R-administration and minimal acute toxicity: of 31 treatment cycles administered, six caused grade 1 nausea, and there were only one grade 3 and two grade 4 episodes of neutropenia, none of which were complicated by fever. These data are in line with two other published phase-2 studies. Zinzani et al (2004) treated 31 patients with either C (400 mg/m i.v. days 1–5), vincristine (V, 1Æ4 mg/m day 1), and prednisone (P, 100 mg p.o. days 1–5) (CVP, 11 patients) or F and mitoxantrone (FM). All achieved a CR, but four patients treated with CVP relapsed versus none treated with FM. All four relapsed patients achieved a second CR with FM with no further relapse indicating superior efficacy of FM compared to CVP. Toxicities were mild with either regimen and no cumulative myelosuppression was observed (Zinzani et al, 2004). Economopoulos et al reported a phase-2 study of the same FM regimen for patients with low-grade lymphomas (26 MZL). Responding patients received R consolidation (375 mg/m weekly for 4 weeks, 1 and 3 months after completion of FM). The OR and CR rates were 91% and 38%, respectively, and disease-free survival at 39 months was 74%. The FM regimen was generally well tolerated, the main toxicity was myelosuppression, with 29% of patients developing grade 3/4 neutropenia, which was febrile in 9%. There were two deaths, one due to sepsis during treatment and one from hepatitis-B reactivation (Economopoulos et al, 2008). Neither of these two studies reported increased rates of lymphoma transformation, bone marrow hypoplasia or secondary myeloid malignancies. Of our cohort, five patients experienced disease transformation. However, in one case this diagnosis was made 46 months prior to initiation of FCR treatment and the patient had manifest disease recurrence with indolent marginal-zone disease, and in two further patients, transformed disease was diagnosed 2 days and 2Æ5 months after FCR initiation, suggesting this was likely to have been present at the time of treatment. The two other patients developed transformed disease 40 and 52 months after initiation of FCR. Despite small numbers, our data raise the question whether the observed disease transformations to aggressive lymphoma may, at least in part, reflect the natural course of MZLs. As a result of its rarity, this has not been particularly well addressed in MZL, but data on follicular lymphoma show a transformation rate of 3% per year with a significantly higher rate in patients with advanced disease and those treated with alkylatoror purine-based chemotherapy, rather than anthracyclines (Gine et al, 2006; Al-Tourah et al, 2008).
