A health policy paradox: the mind-body disconnect in primary mental healthcare policy. Part I.
Failure to diagnose patients with mental illness (MI) and substance abuse (SA) in the primary care setting is a significant problem. Policy researchers estimate the incidence of MI/SA at 29% of the U.S. population a year (WHO International Consortium in Psychiatric Epidemiology, 2000). Andrews, Sanderson, Slade, and Issakidis (2000) report that 58% of depressed patients in the United States are undiagnosed. Primary care providers also fail to diagnose SA or make necessary referrals (McQuade et al., 2000). Affective disorders are increasing in association with the growing prevalence of chronic illness (De Boer, Wijker, & De Haes, 1997), and primary care providers will increasingly be called upon to recognize and refer individuals with these disorders. The National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey reports that prescriptions for antidepressant drugs have increased by 48% since 1995. Office visits for MI numbered 40 million and emergency department visits 2 million during the year 2002 (Woodwell & Cherry, 2004). Early identification of MI/SA and access to primary care and treatment intervention are needed to prevent further morbidity. Research points to an association between MI in youth and teenage parenthood, in both boys and girls (Kessler et al., 1997). Low-income status increases one’s risk for depression, panic, and anxiety disorders (Eibner, Sturm, & Gresenz, 2004). Access to a mental health specialist in the primary care setting is associated with response rates for depression similar to treatment responses seen in specialty practices (Rost, Nutting, Smith, & Werner, 2000). Nurse practitioners (NPs) often see the consequences of MI/SA disorders in their clinical practices, including major life crises, loss of employment, family dissolution, homelessness, and poor quality of life. Although NPs are ideally placed in the healthcare system to initiate recognition and intervention strategies, there may be significant barriers that preclude successful outcomes. Health policy analysis is a structured process that begins with defining the problem within a sociopolitical context and providing research or epidemiological data that demonstrate the impact of the problem on the health of a social group. Once this is accomplished, the challenge confronting the policy analyst lies in formulating a rational solution to the problem that is politically feasible and has the potential to demonstrate measurable outcomes that benefit the target population. An influential policy analyst will provide clear, compelling testimony appealing to those on both sides of the policy table and those of all political persuasions. NPs have long been advocates for policies that enhance access to care for vulnerable populations. Policy analysis is an initial step in the advocacy process. This two-part column will consider the problems associated with delivering primary mental health care from a policy analysis perspective. Part I presents an analysis of current care for MI/SA; Part II will review policy solutions and strategies NPs can use to influence policy change at various levels of the policy process. Meehan (1994) has noted that both inquiry and the knowledge that is produced are capable of advancing the human condition in some way. Policy analysis is one aspect of policy research that helps one to determine what is the ‘‘best policy choice to a particular policy problem’’ (Jennings, 2004, p. 229).
