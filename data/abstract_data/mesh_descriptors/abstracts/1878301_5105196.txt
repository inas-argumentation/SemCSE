A glimmer of hope?
One of the consistent findings during the 1990s was the high rate of impairment and disability we observed among children born at borderline viability (1), a consistent feature of population based studies (2), hospital based cohorts (3) from North America, UK and Australia. With the dawn of the new century, are we beginning to see a glimmer of hope that these disability rates are reducing? In this issue, the article by Horsch et al. (4) seems to indicate a low rate of MRI diagnosed white and grey matter injury which we would predict might translate into lower impairment rates at follow-up. This is in contrast to the other large MRI study by Inder et al. (5) where most babies born less than 26 weeks of gestation had brain abnormalities at term equivalent age. Poor growth of the brain after extremely preterm birth has been observed to lead to less complex and smaller brains at term (6,7) related to very poor cognitive outcomes (2) and a linear relationship between IQ and gestation up to around 32 weeks (8) that can be inferred across a range of studies (9). Any progress in improving outcomes for tiny babies will have a great impact on their lives during the school years when educational needs (10) and costs (11) are high. Comparing studies across populations is fraught with difficulty. Social, economic and demographic factors result in significant differences between Scandinavian countries and the UK, for example, the great difference in the rate of very preterm birth (12). Differences in philosophy of care may lead to great outcome differences (13,14). Nonetheless, the frequency of brain injury in the Stockholm cohort does seem remarkably low and it is encouraging that the team is in the process of following up the children to determine long-term outcomes. In a report from Denmark, low rates of poor outcome were identified among a population exposed to a similar philosophy of care (15). There are encouraging signs from other papers.
