My father’s madness
The issue of what is a psychiatric disorder and what is just a moral fault or personal quirk is not entirely in the hands of psychiatrists.A public view, as expressed by the media and through the courts, has to be taken into consideration even though it has no scientific validity. Because psychiatric disorders remain largely intangible, it is possible to express an opinion about them without the recondite knowledge necessary to have opinions about, say, the management of necrotising enterocolitis or yaws. It is particularly true when it comes to the definition of those disorders that imply diminished responsibility. In the UK, there was the prominent instance of the Yorkshire Ripper. All the psychiatrists agreed about his mental state, but the judge pronounced him sane. The killers of the Bulger child were not afforded the protection of their youth and were tried in the manner of adults. Harold Shipman, found guilty of many murders and perhaps the author of many more was considered sane.However,his social behaviour was very odd and his murderous acts were pointless and extraordinary. He is thought to have brought the profession into disrepute (deliberately) because he is regarded as capable of acting as a free moral agent. He has not sought or been awarded a psychiatric diagnosis.What Shipman keeps to himself makes him sane. In each instance, the courts have responded to public pressure to avoid the perpetrator being thought to be escaping retributive justice and avoiding moral responsibility by slipping into the asylum of sickness. Psychiatric disorders are still defined in terms of a set of observed behaviours that conform to agreed formulae. Similarly, “fevers” were once evidenced by sweating and rigors rather than by thermometry. The gap in knowledge between behavioural deviance and functional, or even structural, changes still looms large. Provided psychiatrists conform to the schedules, it is argued, we will at least all know what we are referring to. The universal recipe book is the DSM manual. It is argued that unless we proceed in this manner it will not be possible to research the biology of these disorders. “Fevers”,however,was not a category from which to gain insight into the biological basis of “fevers”; disorder of the febrile mechanism is not the cause of feverish illnesses. One reliable principle is that basic sciences will eventually engulf psychiatric disorders and move them to the level called “diseases”. Like other recipes, even the carefully named disorders can be fanciful. Another important element in psychiatric disorder is whether the affected individuals are aware of the nature of their own acts. Often, that awareness differs markedly from that of the vast majority of people. Their acts are often not understandable to other people. Although it is not of much practical value in psychiatry the concept of “psychosis”,“madness” covers the important area where reduced responsibility is conceded. One way of exploring difficult concepts is to state them in unusual ways, ad absurdum for example, or through satire or parody. When this essay by a young prodigy came into our hands, it clearly deserved publication because it touches on these issues in a fresh manner.
