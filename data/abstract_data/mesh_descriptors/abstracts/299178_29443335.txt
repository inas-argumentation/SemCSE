While antibodies against DNA were first reported in 1957 (6,41,43,34) it was over 12 years before their known specificity for SLE became fully exploited as a diagnostic and therapeutic aid. The methods used to identify the antibodies included gel diffusion (4), complement fixation (?%, haemagglutination and radioimmunoelectrophoresis. These studies, while agreeing on the heterogeneity of DNA antibodies and their apparent specificity for SLE, were influenced by the limited sensitivity or selectivity of the techniques used to detect antigen-antibody reactions. In 1968. Wold et al. (52) applied the ammonium sulphate precipitation technique devised by Farr (16) to the detection of DNA antibodies. and the following year, Pincus et al. (39) published the results of DNA-binding assays in patients with SLE and other diseases. In a collaborative study with Pincus, the specificity of the test for SLE was confirmed, as was the reproducibility; sera tested in the two centres gave binding values within 5 % (38). In this study (Fig. I). 120 sera from children with a variety of diseases were tested. Twenty of 22 SLE sera showed greater than 20% binding. while all sera of other children showed values of less than 12%.
