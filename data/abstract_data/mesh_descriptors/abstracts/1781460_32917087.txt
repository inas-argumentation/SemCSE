The cytology of HIV‐induced immunosuppression. Changing pattern of disease in the era of highly active antiretroviral therapy
In recent years, the clinical course of HIV infection in Europe, North America and Australasia has changed. Several factors are responsible for this, including an improved understanding of disease pathogenesis, the availability of assays to measure HIV RNA `load' in peripheral blood, the increasing use of prophylaxis against opportunistic infections and of combination antiretroviral therapy. The last two mentioned factors have brought about a signi®cant reduction in AIDS-de®ning events, hospital admissions and HIV-associated mortality. Patients who are able to tolerate antiretroviral therapy are living longer, but because of their underlying immunode®ciency, they are at high risk of malignancy, in particular non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. HIV-infected patients also have a higher incidence of non-AIDS-de®ning malignancies. Despite combination antiretroviral therapy, the incidence of HIV-associated malignancies has not decreased. Opportunistic infections continue to be a common presentation of HIV-associated immunosupression in those unaware of their HIV infection; such patients typically present with advanced HIV disease.
