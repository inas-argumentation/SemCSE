Medicine, philosophy, and the medical humanities.
T phrase ‘medical humanities’ has recently begun to find a place in the wider vocabulary of medical and health care, perhaps especially — though not exclusively — within medical education and within academic medicine. Perhaps it is no coincidence that this phrase, and the ideas to which it refers, have come along after the emergence of the phrase ‘evidence-based medicine’ and the ideas to which it refers, for there is something about the idea of a focus on a highly scientific conception of ‘evidence’ which prompts many people to remind themselves that medicine is above all a science of the human and that many kinds of evidence will be important to such a science. Such evidence, it is now being recognised, includes the insights and enquiries made available by the social sciences and the humanities disciplines themselves, as well as those of the natural and life sciences. In many ways ‘medical humanities’ is the name for a conscious habit of thought, a willingness to see medicine as being painted upon a very wide canvas — the canvas of human experience in all its complexity, diversity, and unpredictability. As a philosopher, I welcome this attitude and the recognitions that it embodies. It reminds me, too, that the study of philosophy can also benefit from being painted over a wider canvas than the rather narrow concerns with language, which at times dominated academic philosophy in parts of the English-speaking world in the 20th century. More specifically, the study of ‘medical humanities’ invites philosophers to recognise the wide range of philosophical questions to which modern medicine’s understanding of the human body inevitably gives rise. Moreover, ‘medical humanities’ prompts a renewed conversation between medicine and philosophy, since they have as much to talk about now as they ever did; indeed, I believe that medicine and philosophy can be thought of as complementary enquiries into human nature. That is perhaps a rather mysterioussounding claim, and I will try to say more about it. One place to begin might be with the idea of medical ethics, which has brought medical matters forcibly to the attention of philosophy and prompted many doctors to notice philosophical questions arising in their clinical practice. However, I will not consider ethical questions as such but, rather, will jump over them to the much larger questions that lie behind them. These are the more ‘meaning-of-life’ type of puzzles which philosophers call ‘metaphysical’: questions about the nature of human existence and of our experience of it. Daunting though the name ‘metaphysical’ sounds, and daunting though ‘meaning-of-life’ questions undoubtedly are, I do think they claim our attention, especially once we recognise this larger canvas over which both medicine and philosophy clamour to be considered, and the wider conversation between them. As such, the study of medical humanities is an invitation to consider precisely these ‘meaning-of-life’ questions; however, it is an invitation with some very practical purposes, and not just a diversion from the business of treating patients. One of the most prominent and central of all metaphysical questions in medicine concerns the relation between our physical state and our conscious experience: in simple terms, the relation between mind and body. (The inescapable importance of this is obvious enough, but strangely overlooked in the rapid development of technological medicine.) People consult their doctors when their states of health intrude into their daily lives and experience. If the problem is an essentially physical one, nevertheless its importance is experiential and the patient seeks relief from pain, discomfort, breathlessness or other unpleasant and intrusive symptoms; and if the problem is essentially a psychological one, nevertheless its management may well involve physical interventions, such as relaxation techniques (not to mention the prescription of drugs). In either case, medicine is asked to intervene in the relation between our embodied state and our experience of that state, either by changing the underlying state itself or by changing our experiential response to it. In one sense this is transparently obvious, and scarcely merits our noticing it. In another sense it is profoundly mysterious. For the truth H M Evans, BA, PhD, senior lecturer, Centre for Philosophy and Health Care, University of Wales Swansea.
