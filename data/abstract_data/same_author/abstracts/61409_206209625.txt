A response to Gary Rolfe's 'Cardinal John Henry Newman' and 'the ideal state and purpose of a university'.
It is a pleasure to respond to the latest episode in the continuing saga of our exchanges with Gary Rolfe. As usual, his critique of our work is detailed, incisive and challenging and, since we stand accused of some literary and intellectual misdemeanours, we feel obliged to reply. This time we are taken to task for invoking Cardinal John Henry Newman and the liberal university and our selective reading of Newman’s work. We plead ‘guilty as charged’ to both counts. First, on the issue of selective use of Newman’s work, we make no apology as quoting the work of others in defence of your own is inevitably selective. One alternative is to cite or append the whole work, or insist that everyone reads it before they address our arguments. This would be tedious for readers and would not serve the purpose of adding our own interpretation of the facts—as we see them—contained in the words actually used, towards whatever end, by Newman. Another alternative is to qualify each statement with so many ‘ifs’ and ‘buts’ that, in addition to diluting our own rhetoric (in the proper sense of the art of discourse), the line of argument in which Newman’s words are woven into our own argument becomes, again, tedious for the reader. We do not patronise our readers, or our critics; however, we both have a good idea of what gets published. Rolfe admits that our ‘somewhat cursory’ summary of Newman’s position on the fundamental role of universities is ‘broadly accurate’ but then goes on to say that Newman was not supportive of the Enlightenment ideal of secular rationality and attributes to us the notions that Newman was a champion of the Enlightenment university of the nineteenth century, that he was an early advocate of the pursuit of knowledge through scientific research and that he would have welcomed the discipline of nursing into the university. We deny trying to make any connection between Newman, the Enlightenment and the Enlightenment university. We are aware that the tenets of David Hume and his followers were diametrically opposed to those held by Newman. We are also in no doubt that nursing would not have been accepted into a university as proposed by Newman; it would not have been accepted into an Enlightenment university either. Newman was a conservative Christian, a High Anglican who ‘defected’ to the Roman Catholic Church. Therefore, we are unsure what Rolfe means, or to whom he attributes the statement: ‘whilst Newman’s book tends to be held up by certain writers as the touchstone of liberal freethinking nurse education and research...’ (99). The observation that Newman gave lectures at Dublin Catholic University on the establishment of its medical school merely suggests to us that he could be a pragmatist. It is entirely logical, nevertheless, that Newman would teach that medicine and curiosity within it should be subservient to the teachings of The Church; this has been a consistent line to this day with regard to all lines of enquiry—philosophical and scientific—of the Roman Catholic Church. This has no bearing on our argument or, we suggest, on Rolfe’s. We have no problem with Rolfe’s proposal that we create a space for nursing in the academy on our own terms. Nor do we disagree with the view that nurse academics engage with practice-based colleagues to work together on projects that are of direct concern to nursing practice and to the benefit of patients. Our position has been to defend non-practice development oriented research within nursing and in university nursing schools. We have pointed to examples outside nursing which support the case for such research and an approach to scholarship that does not necessarily sit within the current business model of the modern university.
