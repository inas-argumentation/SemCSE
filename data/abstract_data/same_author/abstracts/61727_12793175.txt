Objective Data Rather Than Opinion in the Interpretation of the Legitimate Goals of Phase 1 Oncology Trials
In his OP-ED piece describing “Ethical issues of phase 1 clinical trials in cancer patients,” Dr. Grunwald makes a number of statements, and draws conclusions, that must be challenged based on both an objective evaluation of the definition of phase 1 trials and available data in the peer-reviewed medical literature. Dr. Grunwald describes phase 1 trials as being “designed to find the best tolerated dose for a drug not previously used in humans” and further notes the “major ethical problems” associated with “offering such patients an unproven therapy with low probability of benefit.” He also declares, “since the probability of benefit to the participant is extremely low, and thus the altruistic motivation of helping humanity and the enhancement of medical knowledge ought to be the primary driving force in participation.” Finally, he offers the opinion that “such trials should only be offered at a time when no other effective treatment is available.” Although phase 1 cancer trials do include “first in man/women” studies, the actual definition of such studies is far more inclusive than described by Dr. Grunwald. As stated in the Code of Federal Regulations, phase 1 studies “are designed to determine the metabolism and pharmacologic actions of the drug in humans, the side effects associated with increasing doses, and if possible to gain early evidence on effectiveness” (1). Thus, although Phase 1 trials will certainly include the examination of a truly novel agent (“not previously used in humans”), these studies will also encompass initial attempts to combine, for the first time, two well-established and highly effective drugs in a particular malignancy.
