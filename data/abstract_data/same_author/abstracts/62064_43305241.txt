Dermatitis induced by estrogen and progesterone: dual positive results on the intradermal skin test.
To the Editor: A 25-year-old woman presented with a 5-year history of persistent cutaneous eruptions that exhibited periodic exacerbations regularly 3 to 5 days prior to every menstrual cycle and then improved after the end of the menstrual cycle. Clinically, the lesions looked like atopic dermatitis and were pruritic erythematous lichenified scaly patches on both flexural areas of the extremities and areolas (Fig 1). The patient did not have an individual history or family history of atopy. Other aspects of her individual and family histories were unremarkable. She had her first menarche at the age of 13 years, and her menstruation showed a normal cyclic pattern of 28 days. She reported no history of the use of oral birth control pills. All routine laboratory test results were negative or within normal limits. As her clinical features strongly suggested hypersensitivity to female sex hormones, an intradermal skin test was performed. The patient received 12 mg of oral triamcinolone (Ledercort, SK Chemicals, Seoul, Korea) per day for 1 month, with a subsequent slight improvement in symptoms. Thereafter, she received daily topical applications of clobetasol propionate (Clobeson, Dongkook, Seoul, Korea) and a daily oral dose of 2 mg of azelastine HCL (Azeptin, Bukwang, Seoul, Korea) and 10 mg of olopatadine (Allelock, Kyowa Hakko, Tokyo, Japan) for symptom control. Intradermal skin test with progesterone and estrogen was performed on the patient’s right forearm with progesterone (Taiyu Progesterone, Tai Yu, Hsinchu, Taiwan) diluted with mineral oil (1 mg/mL, 0.02 mL) and with estradiol valerate (Estradiol-Depot, Bayer Schering, Berlin, Germany) diluted with mineral oil (1 mg/mL, 0.02 mL). A corticosteroid (triamcinolone acetonide, 10 mg/mL, 0.02 mL) and mineral oil (0.02 mL) were injected as a control. Because the results of the intradermal skin test suggested the possibility of positive reactions to both progesterone and estrogen, the test was repeated after one menstrual cycle (with estrogen on one arm and progesterone on the other) to confirm the results. The tests were conducted in the early follicular phase of the menstrual cycle. Skin reactions were read 30 minutes after application to confirm immediate reactions, and 24 and 48 hours later to confirm delayed reactions. The intradermal skin test result was positive with both progesterone and estrogen. The control site showed no reaction. In particular, the injection site of triamcinolone acetonide showed no reactions (such as edge effect, vasoconstriction, or vasodilatation) for more than 7 days. The patient had no hypersensitivity reactions to topical or systemic corticosteroid therapy for more than a year. These results are shown in Table 1, Figure 2, and Figure 3. Autoimmune progesterone dermatitis and estrogen dermatitis are rare disorders characterized by cyclic skin eruptions influenced by hormonal fluctuations during the menstrual cycle. The cutaneous manifestations of autoimmune progesterone dermatitis and estrogen dermatitis are variable and include urticaria, eczematous eruptions, vesicopustular eruptions, fixed drug eruptions, stomatitis, erythema multiforme, and anaphylaxis. The diagnosis is suspected on the basis of clinical features, and an intradermal skin test generally helps to establish the diagnosis.
