Hypersensitivity Reactions: An Increasingly Recognized Toxicity of Platinum Agents
Antineoplastic agent-induced hypersensitivity has been observed with multiple classes of drugs (including both cytotoxics and biologics). Several underlying immunologic mechanisms have been proposed to be involved in the pathogenesis of the process (for example, direct mast cell degranulation, IgE and IgG mediated), and the incidence, features and severity of reactions can vary widely, based on factors such as the rate of infusion, the number of previous treatment cycles with the agent and likely genetic predisposition [2] . Hypersensitivity reactions associated with platinum agents are particularly problematic, due principally to the major role played by this class of drugs in multiple tumor types. Allergic episodes are a well-recognized occupational hazard among platinum miners [6–8] , and it has been speculated that the slow development of such events in susceptible patients receiving a platinum drug relates to the presence (or more correctly stated, contamination) of the infused treatment solution with minute (nonmeasurable) concentrations of unbound (‘free’) metallic platinum. The report by Kim et al. [9] in this issue of Oncology supports and extends the experience with hypersensitivity to platinum, specifically oxaliplatin. In their series the investigators note a median number of 7 total platinum treatment cycles delivered before a reaction is observed, similar to that previously reported for carboplatin (or cisplatin plus carboplatin) [4] . First, as the treatment of malignant disease has improved and survival has been prolonged, the cancer research community has appropriately focused greater attention on both shortand longer-term quality-of-life issues. There can be little doubt that the development of significant hypotension, respiratory distress, diffuse rashes and associated intense anxiety negatively impacts a patient’s immediate quality of life, even if the events are completely reversible [1–4] . Second, as more than a single therapeutic option is available in multiple cancers, it is increasingly relevant to inquire if therapy should be changed if a hypersensitivity reaction is observed, versus continuing current management and possibly employing some form of ‘desensitization’ [5] . One might even inquire, in the setting of 2 regimens in a malignancy, if the program that is not associated with the development of symptomatic hypersensitivity reactions should be the treatment of choice (of course, assuming other toxic effects are essentially clinically equivalent). Finally, treatment-related deaths which are due to chemotherapy-associated hypersensitivity reactions, while fortunately rare, have been reported [1] .
