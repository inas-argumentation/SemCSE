Provider Impact on Survival Outcomes in the Management of Malignant Disease
In our revolutionary information technology era where “data” regarding a bewildering number of subjects is quickly available to anyone with an internet connection, it should come as no surprise that patients and their families, and others with an intense interest in the area (e.g., employers, third party payers, etc.) would have an ever increasing desire to learn more about individual providers and health care teams or institutions before deciding where indicated medical care will be delivered. Considering the seriousness of a diagnosis of a malignant condition, it is surely understood why questions of provider experience and documented outcomes (for both an individual provider and institution) would be highly appropriate. Further, there will be little (if any) disagreement with the statement that from a cancer patient’s perspective, the primary (although certainly not only) goal of cancer treatment will be to cure the illness (if possible), or at least to extend survival to a meaningful degree (an endpoint, in the opinion of this commentator, that should be defined by the patient). As a result, it is again not surprising that one of the most commonly asked questions of providers and their institutions by prospective cancer patients and their families is the “survival outcomes” in the particular clinical setting. The specific issue to be addressed in this commentary is the actual role individual oncologists can realistically play in favorably impacting survival. And a critical extension of this question is how can that impact be objectively measured and reported?
