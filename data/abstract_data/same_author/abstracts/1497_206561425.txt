Biomedical Image Analysis
The papers assembled in this special issue of Computer Vision and Image Understanding represent a selection from among those presented at the First IEEE/ SIAM Joint Workshop on Mathematical Methods in Biomedical Image Analysis (MMBIA). This meeting represented a blending of the talents and interests of individuals who attended the IEEE Workshop on Biomedical Image Analysis held in Seattle in 1994 and the Mathematical Methods in Medical Imaging Conferences previously held under SPIE sponsorship in San Diego during 1992–1994. The fulllength papers submitted to the workshop were reviewed by at least two members of the program committee and the results were compiled to produce 32 papers presented in a one-track forum on June 21–22, 1996, in San Francisco. In addition, the meeting included two plenary talks by Fred Bookstein and Demetri Terzopoulos. The primary focus of the research presented at the meeting was on the development of new methods for segmentation, registration, shape representation, and motion tracking for biomedical image analysis. The international flavor of these efforts is indicated by the fact that the individuals making the presentations represented France, Japan, Netherlands, Spain, United Kingdom, and the United States. The papers presented in this issue of Computer Vision and Image Understanding focus primarily on the task of extracting information from medical images acquired from X-ray devices, MR images, nuclear medicine scanners, and ultrasound. These are to be distinguished from images of biological structures acquired through light or confocal microscopy or the scanning electron microscope. Analysis of this type of image has expanded rapidly in scope in recent years, driven in part by advances in the parent disciplines of image formation and reconstruction as well as computer vision and shape modeling. However, the expansion owes in part to the steadily larger availability of 3D image data, which provide a playground for volumetric modeling of tissues, without dealing with projection operators encountered in optics. In order to extract information in images in a more automated, accurate, and reproducible way, methods are needed that combine the underlying image physics with explicit or implicit data structures that effectively account for data originating from living tissues. Also, since the objects under investigation in biomedical images represent the large anatomical and biological variability inherent in nature, the researcher is forced to produce methods which can process the degrees of variability in the data. The issue begins with Bookstein’s summary of developments in morphometrics (the multivariate statistics of shape) that are relevant for medical image analysis and image understanding. The second paper, by Okatani and Deguchi, offers a novel approach to the analysis of the endoscopic data increasingly arising in the course of optically guided surgery. The third and fourth papers approach the classic problem of ridge (or skeleton) extraction from medical images. They represent two distinct approaches: one more traditionally algorithmic and one set instead in the context of image evolutions.
