Efforts of institutions and organizations in the developed world designed to assist their colleagues in the developing world to improve clinical care and establish research agendas focused on the unique issues in this complex environment have the potential to exert a major favorable impact on individual programs and global activities designed to enhance the delivery of essential care [3] . In this issue of Oncology , Strother et al. [2] describe an experience dealing with the management of HIV/ AIDS-related Kaposi’s sarcoma in Western Kenya that developed from a relationship between academic institutions in the United States and the Moi Teaching Hospital in Eldoret, Kenya. A retrospective review of patients managed with single agent gemcitabine (itself a gift from the pharmaceutical company Eli Lilly) in previously treated Kaposi’s sarcoma revealed both objective activity for the agent and critically important data that the observed responses were able to be translated into measurable evidence of direct clinical benefit for those receiving the drug (reduction in pain and requirement for pain medications, increase in weight, improvement in performance status) [2] . It has been estimated that by the year 2020, 70% of the 17 million new cancer cases discovered each year will be from countries of the developing world [1] . Based on current projections related to the known and anticipated health care expenditures of these countries, it is unfortunately appropriate to firmly state that malignant disease will almost certainly exert a staggering toll in these regions, where medical staff and facilities (e.g. radiation therapy units) are often seriously inadequate to provide even the minimal level of care established to be effective in either curing, or at least relieving, profoundly distressing disease-related symptoms (e.g. narcotic analgesia). The administration of cytotoxic chemotherapy in both the curative (e.g. lymphomas) and palliative (e.g. metastatic solid tumors) settings is particularly problematic considering the costs associated with the drugs themselves and the infrastructure required to safely deliver the agents, as well as effectively monitor usage (e.g. trained nursing staffs, blood tests, effective use of anti-emetics, antibiotics).
