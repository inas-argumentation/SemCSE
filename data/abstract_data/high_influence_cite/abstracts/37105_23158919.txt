S100B Serves as a Ca2+ Sensor for ROS-GC1 Guanylate Cyclase in Cones but not in Rods of the Murine Retina
Rod outer segment membrane guanylate cyclase (ROS-GC1) is a bimodal Ca<sup>2+</sup> signal transduction switch. Lowering [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<sub>i</sub> from 200 to 20 nM progressively turns it “ON” as does raising [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<sub>i</sub> from 500 to 5000 nM. The mode operating at lower [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<sub>i</sub> plays a vital role in phototransduction in both rods and cones. The physiological function of the mode operating at elevated [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<sub>i</sub> is not known. Through comprehensive studies on mice involving gene deletions, biochemistry, immunohistochemistry, electroretinograms and single cell recordings, the present study demonstrates that the Ca<sup>2+</sup>-sensor S100B coexists with and is physiologically linked to ROS-GC1 in cones but not in rods. It up-regulates ROS-GC1 activity with a K<sub>1/2</sub> for Ca<sup>2+</sup> greater than 500 nM and modulates the transmission of neural signals to cone ON-bipolar cells. Furthermore, a possibility is raised that under pathological conditions where [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<sub>i</sub> levels rise to and perhaps even enter the micromolar range, the S100B signaling switch will be turned “ON” causing an explosive production of CNG channel opening and further rise in [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<sub>i</sub> in cone outer segments. The findings define a new cone-specific Ca<sup>2+</sup>-dependent feature of photoreceptors and expand our understanding of the operational principles of phototransduction machinery.
