Manufacturing over the Internet and into Your Living Room: Perspectives from the AARIA Project
Consumer demand and current computational capabiliti es are driving the manufacturing complex from mass production to mass customization. Current barriers to mass customization have less to do with manufacturing machinery and more to do with the manual and computerized information systems currently used to control that machinery. On line commerce offers potential benefits and functionality far beyond the automated catalogs that characterize today's cybermarkets. The AARIA project (Autonomous Agents for Rock Island Arsenal) demonstrates how agent technologies and Internet communications can support this expanded vision. This paper outlines new directions that we expect trade on the Internet to take, and shows how AARIA's architecture, scheduling approach, and simulation capabiliti es support these new directions. 1. New Directions for On Line Commerce Most products are manufactured: appliances, automobiles, electronic equipment, packaged foods, paper products, furniture, clothing. Many consumers have an oversimpli fied view of how these products come to our homes or businesses. The casual perception is that someone goes to a store and purchases them. In reality for every product purchased at a store, there is often a deep network of suppliers for all the components that go into that product. Adam Smith referred to the mechanisms that establish the complex interrelationships between each of these suppliers as the " invisible hand" [22] of the capitalistic system. This network of interrelationships is even deeper if we consider the internal chains of production at each manufacturer in this supply chain. Consumer demand and current computational capabiliti es are driving the manufacturing complex from mass production (where the manufacturer tells consumers what they can buy) to mass customization [19] (where the customer tells the manufacturing complex what to manufacture) Agent technology and the Internet provide powerful new tools for creating agile [9] networks of suppliers that are truly responsive to individual customer desires. Internet based home shopping as casually conceived oversimpli fies the capabiliti es that can be provided to the consumer. Many think of home shopping as the abilit y to electronically order available products from a catalog. Yet it is technically feasible for home shopping to be a dialog between the consumer and the distributed manufacturing complex. Current catalogs do not show the customer the dynamics of product availabilit y. Some times a product can be delivered the next day at a low cost, on other days only a close substitute for the product is immediately available, and at other times the customer may have to wait a number of months for the exact product listed in the catalog. In such cases, commitments made by the catalog may not be reliable, as they depend on the dynamic availability of manufacturing capabilities. Current barriers to mass customization have less to do with manufacturing machinery and more to do with the manual and computerized information systems currently used to control that machinery. In general, the installed base of manufacturing equipment can be used to produce a much wider variety of products than it currently does [2] The problem is more one of logistics and coordination than of basic machinery. Furthermore, even though current information systems throughout the manufacturing complex are not responsive to customers, they add a great deal of cost to the customer. Retail pricing of many products is often many times the base manufacturing costs for those products. So, not only do these systems distance the customer from actual capabilities, they add substantial costs to the final product. Current trends are redefining consumers' expectations from the manufacturing complex. Leading edge manufacturers who provide broad customization options will be substantially more competitive than manufacturers who maintain the status quo. Manufacturers who are not willi ng to tailor their products to the individual desires of their customers will shrink or disappear. Ford Motor Company used to be the largest automotive manufacturer in the world. To reduce costs, Henry Ford insisted on only producing black cars. His decision was consistent with his other principles of mass production, but underestimated his customers' individuality. Ford's decision opened a niche for other automobile manufacturers. We use a simple protocol whereby a customer requests a specific product, the manufacturing system responds with a bid of cost versus delivery time and the customer chooses a delivery time and a cost that satisfy him. Each agent in the internal supply chain that emerges then makes and maintains a commitment to perform his part of the job. Agents modify their plans to meet their commitments in light of any problems such as machine failures, scrap, power outages, sickness, or emergency jobs. They also perform background computations attempting to reduce the costs of their commitments by moving jobs around or swapping resources. In this scenario, later stages of production become customers to earlier stages of production. This structure provides a self configuring manufacturing system where any capabilit y can become another capabilit y's customer or supplier. The final consumer is just viewed as another customer in this supply chain.
