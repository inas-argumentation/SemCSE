Reframing international conflict after 9/11
One of the obvious questions emerging after September 11 was if and how the 'war on terror' would influence the framing of political conflicts in the world, particularly those involving groups targeting civilians and/or involving Muslims. The political geographers O'Loughlin, O Tuathail and Kolossov (2004) took up the question with a study on Russian political 'storylines' in which the events in New York are pictured as an act of aggression of international terrorism, 'the plague of the 21st century' that threatens the US just as much as it does Russia (Chechen insurgence) This 'official storyline' had to compete with other interpretations, the 'sceptical' storyline and the 'American imperialism' storyline. The influence of 9/11 on framing has inspired many studies. Framing started already with the live coverage of the events by C N N The use of words such as 'Pearl Harbor' 'war' 'God' 'pray' and 'America' in comments by TV reporters evokes the image of a people involved in a struggle of cosmic dimension (Reynolds Barnett 2003) A n article on 'Framing of 11 September in the Turkish media' reveals that the media emphasised the Arab (non Turkish) background of the attackers (liter 2003) In an article about attitudes to the US in the wake of 9/11, the author showed that media in Quebec feel the need to distance themselves from the US (Adams 2004) What is the common denominator among such different studies? The most obvious conclusion is that events such as 9/11 shed a new light on a framer's national identity or on local problems. This implies either a call for defence in the face of an ominous international event or an opportunity for political action and self justification. C N N reporters invoked the sacrosanct mission of America; Russian politicians wanted to imagine Russia as involved in the respectable war of the civilised world with terror; Turkish media emphasised the innocence of a particular Islamic society
