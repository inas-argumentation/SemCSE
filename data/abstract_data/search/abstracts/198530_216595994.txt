Minimally invasive pancreatoduodenectomy
Minimally invasive access has become standard for many surgical procedures. Transient increases in specific complications with new laparoscopic procedures, such as bile duct injuries seen after introduction of laparoscopic cholecystectomy, emphasize the learning curve and the importance of training in innovative procedures. Open pancreatoduodenectomy (PD) is considered complex surgery but, when attempted laparoscopically, it represents a true technical challenge requiring difficult dissection, control of major vessels and complex reconstruction. Yet, it must be done with acceptable morbidity while maintaining oncological principles. Laparoscopic PD has seen much slower adoption than other laparoscopic procedures. A reason may be the lack of training of established hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) surgeons in advanced laparoscopic skills. Mastering simple laparoscopic procedures, such as cholecystectomy, is clearly insufficient and a whole new skill set has to be learned by the open HPB surgeon. Conversely, being an expert laparoscopic surgeon without a background in pancreatic surgery is equally insufficient. Despite the challenges, there are theoretical advantages to minimally invasive PD. First, improved recovery time and shorter hospital stay might allow earlier initiation of adjuvant therapy. Second, the availability of a minimally invasive approach might modify the risk benefit ratio of pancreatectomy in favour of acceptance of more prophylactic surgery (including both PD and distal pancreatectomy) for premalignant lesions such as intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm or pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia. Several retrospective cohort studies from expert centres have described good results with laparoscopic PD. However, there has been concern regarding the long learning curve (more than 40 procedures)1, the potential for higher morbidity and mortality rates, and the need for a high volume to achieve outcomes equivalent to those of the open procedure. Since 2017, three trials2 4 and a meta analysis5 have been published comparing the short term outcome of laparoscopic versus open PD. There were no differences between procedures except for blood loss, which was lower in laparoscopic groups, and operating time, which was shorter for open surgery. Although two trials originally described a shorter length of hospital stay after laparoscopic PD, the multicentre LEOPARD 2 trial4 reported longer stay in the laparoscopic group. Overall 90 day mortality and severe complication rates were similar. The LEOPARD 2 trial was stopped prematurely because of safety concerns, with a higher mortality rate after laparoscopic PD4. In the light of these results, the obvious conclusion is that laparoscopic PD currently seems to be a step too far technically. In this setting, the French Health Agency has recently both promoted the laparoscopic approach for distal pancreatectomy, and banned it for PD. There is a high probability that several national health agencies will take a similar decision if no series invalidates these disappointing results. Therefore, the principal question is whether laparoscopic PD should be abandoned forever. The answer is not simple because several parameters should be taken into consideration. The challenge for the next few years will be to define the best indications (surgical training, centre volume, surgeon expertise, tumour and pancreas characteristics, technology) for a minimally invasive approach in such difficult situations. As is the case with all surgical procedures, the surgeon's performance, based on level of training and experience, has a major impact on both intraoperative and postoperative outcomes. The concept of a learning curve is well recognized, but this more accurately represents the course towards proficiency as opposed to learning. Obtaining proficiency is an endeavour to minimize error and maximize performance. A recent large multicentre analysis1 of laparoscopic oesophagectomy showed that over 100 procedures were necessary to reach the learning curve plateau, suggesting that the learning curve for laparoscopic PD might be much longer than presumed previously. The current methods of teaching and disseminating minimally invasive pancreatectomy are inadequate in an era of quality based reimbursement and outcome scrutiny. With the current training schedules, most centres will not have enough procedures to progress trainees and new adopters of this approach in a feasible time interval.
