Smith, D. M. 1977: Human geography: a welfare approach. London: Edward Arnold
With the publication in 1969 of David Harvey's Explanation in geography, the so called quantitative revolution in geography had consolidated its hold on the subject or so it seemed. However, the trouble with formal theory, model building and quantitative analysis is that human beings, whether as individuals or as groups, are apt to disappear from view. As is well known, numerous counter revolutionaries were gathering, many of them intent on challenging the Popperian logical positivism that lies at the heart of the approach adopted by the quantifiers. Thus it is that the epistemological debate has continued, with realism, structuralism and other 'isms' competing for our attention. In one sense, Smith's book is part of that counter revolution. By the time the book was published in 1977, there was a considerable body of literature, including his own work, which in effect was saying 'hold it, there are real people out there' In Human geography: a welfare approach. Smith seeks to put people back as the central concern of human geography, asking us to abandon the assumption of homogeneity which had become so popular. However, whereas some sought to deny the validity of analytical techniques associated with the so called quantitative revolution, for example, looking to the metaphysics of Marxism, the cardinal feature of Smith's book is the way in which he calls these techniques to his aid in pursuing the agenda of social research in a spatial framework. In that sense, the book is very conservative. Shining through the text is a passionate conviction that, at all geographical scales, the degree of inequality is unacceptably great. Internationally, the poverty of Bangladesh compared with the wealth of Britain was a matter of grave concern. The injustice of apartheid in South Africa and of social segregation in the cities of Europe and the USA touched him to the quick. The agenda which Smith set for us amounts to the following. Given that there are wide and unacceptable spatial inequalities, how can these be described and measured, and how can they then be explained? Only when we can answer these questions will we be in a position to ask the further, crucial question: 'how may these outcomes be altered to achieve a fairer distribution of welfare?
