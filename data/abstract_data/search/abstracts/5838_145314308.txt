'It's Crunch Time' The 'Lost' Geographies of the Crisis
The intimate relationship between finance and the `real' economy has become the subject of increased study by scholars in geography and the social sciences of late, through their study of financialisation and the impact of financial markets on everyday' life. Financialisation can be used as a tool to explain why businesses in the `real' economy have suffered so deeply from the failures of the US subprime mortgage industry. Whilst this provides an initial insight into how the crisis has spread through the international circuits of capital, it does not provide much insight into the spaces that have become (re)shaped by the current crisis. Sidaway (2008) has suggested that earlier financial crises have been profoundly uneven, and that the framing and naming of crises such as `The Asian Crisis' obscure the myriad of geographies that constitute the crisis. As such, the spatial understandings of the current `subprime crisis' are limited, having been interpreted through a series of geographically insensitive narratives, where the absence of space within these contemporary accounts have, in effect, hidden a series of `lost' geographies. Governments, journalists, and academics alike have been consistent in emphasising the global nature of the financial crisis and the forces that are active beyond the territorial reach of governments. In this sense, accounts of the credit crunch have become subject to the hyperbole associated with earlier conceptualisations of globalisation and its allegedly homogenising forces. Geographers have attempted to move beyond these early theorisations by highlighting the heterogeneity of globalisation (Taylor et al, 2002) which in doing so, places them in an ideal position to contribute to debates on the crisis, through spatially nuanced narratives that are sensitive to both space and scale. Early geographical contributions on the crisis have explored its global nature (Sassen, 2009) and its local impact (Hernandez, 2009) but three years since the credit crunch began there are still geographically and politically significant spaces and scales, which are yet to be thoroughly examined. In this commentary I seek to briefly outline some of the spaces and scales that still require attention. Aalbers (2009) provides a useful review that outlines some of the existing research that has been conducted on the geographies of the crisis, and reminds us that in contrast to the `global' nature of the crisis, where the rights to national mortgage portfolios are circulated in global markets to North American, European, and Asian investors, the property on which mortgages are based remains physically fixed in local neighbourhoods and regional economies. French et al (2009) have also highlighted the different networks, spaces, and scales of the credit crunch, which reminds us that it is important to acknowledge that the credit crunch has unravelled unevenly across the globe in different ways, a consequence of historical legacies, emerging from political regulation, financial innovation, and cultural differences.
