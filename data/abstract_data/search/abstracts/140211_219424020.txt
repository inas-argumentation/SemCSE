One Hundred Years of Solitude: Meditating on Tarik Amar's Book on Lviv
SlavojZizek once commented that he prizedAlexei Yurchak's bookEverythingWas Forever, Until It Was NoMore for bringing philosophical eternity into history. In The Paradox of Ukrainian Lviv: A Borderland City between Stalinists, Nazis, and Nationalists, Tarik Cyril Amar borrows Lviv's "paradox" not from philosophy but from the work of the Swedish historian Martin Aberg, who stressed that Soviet Lviv is paradoxical as a result of an unintended consequence of Soviet modernization, which brought "the laying of the socio cultural and demographic foundation for Lviv to become the 'secret capital' of Ukraine and amajor center of ethno nationalist politics during the first years after independence" (Aberg 2000, 296) The changes brought about by the economic plans, while providing a basis for new forms of ethno linguistic differentiation in civil society, were so modest that they allowed for established social structures and cultural forms to continue to exist below the surface (Aberg 2000, 296) A few years after Aberg's observations and following Roman Szporluk's identification of this situation as "strange" (Szporluk 1992, 104) the Ukrainian historian Yaroslav Hrytsak was still wondering whether the twenty interwar years in Lviv's history "were so crucial in shaping attitudes that proved to be so persistent some sixty to eighty years later or, are we dealing with some longue duree history of nationalism" (Hrytsak 2003, 103) Hrytsak took urban point while claiming that Lwow/Lemberg/Lviv was special due to itsmetropolitan character, since it was "the largest metropolis in that region," playing the role of nationalizing city, instead of nationalizing empire or state (Hrytsak 2003, 103) Amar's book contradicts these accounts, and he does not agree that the Ukrainization of Lviv under Soviet rule happened unintentionally or due to the special character of the city and its long history. Amar believes that Soviet rule left behind the most Ukrainian version of the city in its history. Thus, unlike scholars who look at pre Soviet history in order to explain Lviv's paradox, Amar argues that Lviv's national character was established mainly by the Soviets (321) To prove this argument, he produced an extensive, well written work rich in resources that highlights contradictions among Ukrainian historians. Amar employs a critical voice, which has been noted by several reviewers. He is criticizing and ironizing nationalists, communists, or Nazis, and this attitude, in my opinion, does not lead, as some historians claim, to biased and wrong conclusions. Amar points out that the city is placed on the border, making it not only marginal but also importantly subject to external forces. According to Amar, Lviv's recent and paradoxical history and character was molded into a modern and national Ukrainian center by two highly authoritarian and immensely ferocious regimes: Nazi Germany and the Stalinist Soviet Union (1 2) However, in other sections of the book, Amar asserts that Lviv and the region was transformed by Sovietization andUkrainization, as well as rapid Soviet industrialization and modernization, which interacted with both of these.
