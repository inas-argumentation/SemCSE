A Review of: "Selekman, M. D. (2010) Collaborative Brief Therapy with Children."
Matthew Selekman, a social worker specializing in family therapy and addictions counseling, is co director of an international family therapy training and consultation firm. His numerous articles and books on family therapy emphasize a collaborative, strengths based brief therapy approach. Prior to 1997 there was no book on brief therapy with children and their families. Selekman filled this gap with his 1997 book Solution Focused Therapy with Children: Harnessing Family Strengths for Systemic Change. This current book is an expanded and revised remake of that original work, featuring new culturally diverse case examples and includes six full length case examples, including what Selekman terms the 'blueprint for change' plan. This plan invites clients to be in charge of deciding what their goals are, helps determine key strengths and resources, determines who will attend sessions, and specifies frequency of visits. This book's 12 chapters start with an explanation of the collaborative strength based brief therapy approach. The book then moves on to a prototypical assessment session, providing an interesting chapter on motivational enhancement strategies leading to assessment in order to increase the likelihood of change. Therapeutic strategies and use of therapeutic experiments and design are detailed including using various modalities such as family play and art therapy. His solutions oriented parenting approach, developed from Solution Focused Therapy, is followed by a problem solving chapter on optimizing therapeutic cooperation. His Working with larger social systems, including schools and working with primary care physicians and pediatricians, is followed by another problem solving chapter dealing with complex or 'stuck' families. The book ends with a chapter reviewing the major themes presented in the book and future implications. Selekman begins with an interesting quote from John F. Kennedy who said: 'the greatest enemy of the truth is not the lie deliberate, contrived, and dishonest but the myth persistent, pervasive and unrealistic. Similarly, Selekman believes that there are many 'sacred cow' beliefs about child psychotherapy which he then attempts to dispel, the first of which is the notion that traumatized children must grow up to be emotionally flawed adults, especially citing the belief that sexual abuse must inevitably lead to severe emotional problems.
