TOWARDS IMPROVED VISUAL AIDS FOR THE PARTIALLY SIGHTED
With an ageing population in most western countries, the proportion of low vision patients is likely to show a steady increase for many years to come. There is, moreover, an increasing emphasis on integrating younger partially sighted individuals into the community. It may be expected that these factors will lead to a continuing demand for improvements in both the provision and effectiveness of low vision aids. Unfortunately, although many such patients can be helped by various designs of low vision aid, from optical magnifiers and telescopes to closed circuit television (CCTV) devices, only a small fraction of those in need actually receive this help. While improvement in the availability of aids hinges partly upon political and organizational decisions, it also inevitably depends upon the cost of the devices prescribed, Has there, then, been any progress in recent years in the design of aids that are both cheaper and more effective in use? If we consider aids for reading, it is evident that three main sets of parameters are likely to influence the reading speeds achievable. The first of these corresponds, of course, to the visual characteristics of the partially sighted person. The second is the optical or electro optical design of the device itself and the third is the combination of manual dexterity and mechanical design involved in the manipulation of the relative positions of the aid and the text to be read. Legge and his colleagues at the University of Minnesota have recently carried out an interesting study into the significance of the first two of these (Legge el al. 1985a, 1985b) Using a CCTV display, they systematically investigated the effects of changing the display parameters on reading speeds for normal (Legge el al. 1985a) and low vision (Legge el al. 1985b) subjects. The latter had a variety of central and peripheral defects and Snellen acuities as low as 6/288. The reading material was automatically scanned at controllable speed across the screen, so that "mechanical" factors did not play a role. Surprisingly, for both normaland low vision subjects, only four characters were required to be visible at any instant in order that maximal reading rates should be achieved. Less surprisingly, whereas normal subjects needed character subtenses between 0.3 and 2" for their maximal reading rates of about 250 words min I (the reading speed being lower for both smaller and larger characters) most #low vision patients demanded much higher levels of magnification to achieve their peak rates of between about 20 and ux) words mir l with character subtenses up to 24" being necessary in some instances. The highest levels were, in general, required by those patients with central field loss. As has been found by other investigators, low vision patients with cloudy media, associated with such conditions as cataract or corneal scarring, benefitted most from using displays with reversed contrast, i.e. white letters on a black ground, whereas this produced little advantage for those with clear media. The results of varying the resolution of the display were particularly striking. It was found that for "continuous" displays, involving letters with the normal printed format, a resolution of only two cycles per character was required for maximal reading rates to be reached. This, combined with the requirement that four characters be visible, means that a continuous display with a resolution as low as 8 x 8 cycles would satisfy the visual requirements for fast reading. When the text was presented in "dot matrix" form, it appeared that only 80 x 80 samples across the full screen would be necessary (Pelli et al. 1985) In considering which characteristics of the low vision patient might affect reading speed, Legge and his coworkers noted particularly that those with central scotomas always tended to read very slowly, raising interesting questions about the possibly unique importance of central vision in this type of visual task. Knowledge of near acuities measured with Sloan M reading cards (Sloan and Brown, 1963) the state of the central field and the cloudiness of the media was found to enable reasonable predictions of likely reading speed to be made. Distance Snellen acuity was of much less relevance, a similar conclusion having been reached earlier by Brown (1981) who suggested that measures of contrast sensitivity performance with Arden plates (Arden, 1978) gave useful predictions of likely reading performance with CCTV devices. The picture that emerges, then, from the Minnesota group's work is that a relatively low resolution magnifier might allow many low vision patients to achieve reasonable reading speeds, provided that characters could be magnified to subtend up to about 24" The group has put forward its own design for such a magnifier (Pelli et al. 1985) based on a thick (12 mm diameter) lowresolution fibre optics bundle containing about 125 X 125 plastic fibres. The text to be read is imaged by an appropriate objective onto one end of the 60 cm long bundle, the other end being viewed by an eyepiece, which may be spectacle mounted. A plastic stand supports the objective at the appropriate distance from the text, which can be illustrated by either ambient illumination or by built in focused bulbs. Overall magnifications between x 5 and x 40 are obtained by varying the combination of objective and eyepiece. Among the merits of the device are portability, since the bundle can be coiled to go in the pocket or handbag, and the flexibility of reading posture that it permits. Cost is less than U.S. $lOOO.
