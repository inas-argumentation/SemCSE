Primary Care, Generalism, Public Good: Dj vu? Again!
Developing the supplement on the future of generalism in medicine in this issue caused us to have a collective sense of dj vu (1 5) Many of the ideas and arguments presented in this supplement echo the calls for revitalization in recent special reports by groups in family medicine (6) pediatrics (7, 8) and internal medicine (9, 10) and harken back to the 1970s (11 13) During the reviewing and editing process, we felt like we have been through all this before. And, in fact, we have. Admittedly, there are important differences between the mid 1970s, when the primary care movement emerged (11) and today. Costs are far greater than anyone would have anticipated. Science and technology and the acceptance of evidence based principles are much further developed. Medical care is even more complex and occurs in more sites, with more exchanges, than we would have imagined possible 30 years ago. And, of course, advances in information systems have transformed modern society and are transforming medicine in ever more powerful ways. Indeed, the very notion that resources for medical care are finite is being called into question. However, medicine in the United States has still not developed into anything resembling a systemmuch less a system based on the model that seemed so promising 30 years ago, a model organized around a generalist principle or primary care physician. For most Americans, this ideal of patients receiving most of their care based on a relationship with a personal physician or personal team of clinicians located in the patient's community remains just thatan ideal. Given that conventional wisdom and good evidence indicate that a primary care physician is essential to good medical care (14) and a primary carebased system is the ideal system, why then hasn't the United States developed one? Why haven't we changed? One simple answer might be, It's the money, stupid. Despite ongoing attempts to reform physician payment and payments for medical services in general, the current payment system is widely regarded as dysfunctional (15) In particular, prices paid for medical services in the United States have made it increasingly difficult for a career in primary care to be economically attractive or, in some instances, even viable compared with most other specialtiesespecially those providing procedural services (15 18) Because of increasingly market driven economic forces, students and trainees who may enter medicine with idealism and a commitment to public service are increasingly unlikely to enter primary care disciplines (19, 20) Perhaps more important, the investment capital that drives service and program development in medicine is generally not being used to develop primary or generalist medical services. Instead, as in every market economy, capital is being invested to expand the more lucrative services. There have been times in recent history when social forces seemed to nudge the health care system toward organization around a generalist model. The recognition of the value of primary care in the late 1970s is one example. Organizational changes, such as the managed care movement of the late 1980s and early 1990, are another (21) But there has always remained a relatively small margin between the low prices paid for generalist and primary physician services and the relatively high cost of producing such services, placing providers of these services at a disadvantage. In this supplement, Schwartz and colleagues address what can be done to make primary care more attractive to students short of a major overhaul of economic incentives in favor of generalist careers (5) However, it may in fact be the case that until the economic disparity is resolved, it is very unlikely that Americans will see a flourishing primary care physician cadre (18, 22, 23) And without major reform of health care financing and payment, we believe it is even less likely that the United States will develop a system of care organized around a primary care model, except for large integrated delivery systems such as Kaiser Permanente and Group Health Cooperative (24, 25) Even these systems will be challenged because of prevailing styles of practice, usually driven by our medical schools (which themselves exist in a market driven health care system) and prevailing community standards. Since the standard of practice is such an overarching consideration in our litigious society, it has a powerful effect on practitioners, patients, and the way care is organized, such that even integrated delivery systems cannot afford to be too different from prevailing practice. So what might lead to a major beneficial change? One perspective is that market forces will determine what primary care will look like. Will health care eventually go the way of the airlines industrya journey so artfully described in the title of a recent Wall Street Journal article: Long Flight: How Airlines Resisted Change for 25 Years, and Finally Lost (26) Will the market simply drive out providers who are unprofitable? Will it leave just a few types of primary care physicianshighly compensated boutique practitioners providing primary, ongoing care to those who can afford it; employed primary care physicians in large integrated systems; and a poorly compensated, disorganized group of safety net providers? To date, the evidence indicates that market forces are not truly efficient in medicine because, if anything, they tend to promote more care, often with unintended adverse consequences (27, 28) In fact, more care can be worse, especially at the extremes when it is based on the proliferation of specialty care (29, 30) More care, when poorly organized, seems to produce results that are worse from both an economic and social perspective, actually leading to inferior outcomes (29, 30) Instead, we need to build on the principles that good, generalist based primary care offers as an alternative to wasteful and inflationary systems. Rather than uncoordinated, episodic care, we need to offer care that is well organized (2) coordinated (3) integrated (2, 3) characterized by effective communication (4) and based on continuous healing relationships (15, 31) If market forces of today are unlikely to support such change to a primary carebased system, what might? How might the public good prevail? What forces might result in development of a health care system organized around primary care and, we believe, a system that will not be afflicted with the problems many Americans receiving health care know all too well? Are there lessons not only from the failure of the airline industry's resistance to change but also from success stories in health care where the public good prevailed? Can we hope for and, more important, work for success of the models envisioned and aspired to some 30 years ago (32) Isaacs and Schroeder (33) proposed an answer to that question by studying instancesstories, if you willof health care problems that have largely been solved or ameliorated. They write that such instances can help us learn how research, advocacy, public discussion and policy fit together in successful campaigns for change. They studied how our society has had success at removing lead from gasoline and paint, adding fluoride to community water supplies, reducing smoking rates, and increasing automobile safetyespecially through the manufacture of safer cars and the reduction of alcohol related crashes. On the basis of their study they concluded that these quiet victories shared 4 ingredients for success, ingredients that offer lessons for shaping public policy: Highly credible scientific evidence can persuade policymakers and withstand attack from those whose interests are threatened. Public health campaigns need advocates who are passionately committed to their cause, who have the inner resources to withstand the tremendous pressure applied by the industries whose practices they are criticizing, and who continue to fight even at the risk of their professional reputations. Public awareness and discussion depend on a partnership with the media. Advocates need the media to reach the public, and the media, looking for good stories, also need the advocates. Law and regulation, often at the federal level, have been critical elements in focusing Americans' attitudes on health concerns, providing policy direction, and setting standards that have led to improvement in the public's health. Which of these ingredients are present today with regard to the development of a U.S. health care system based on a primary care delivery system? Credible scientific evidence: There is a credible body of scientific evidence worldwide, but to date it has not been strong enough to overcome market forces and the constraints of a dysfunctional payment system that, while not actively attacking primary care per se, does ultimately resist change. Advocates who are passionately committed: To date, no advocacy group has emerged with leaders the likes of Ralph Nader (Public Citizen) or Candy Lightner (Mothers Against Drunk Driving) to advocate for primary care. It is conceivable that someday citizens groups such as AARP may embrace the need for care organized around primary care and emerge as leaders, but to date none has. The media: The media have been remarkably silent about the consequences of the absence of primary care. In fact, market forces largely rely on the popular media to preserve the status quo (the Harry and Louise ads were effective in turning the tide against the health care reform proposed by the Clinton administration, and the 2002 movie John Q, with Denzel Washington, was remarkably effective at demonizing managed care) Mass advertising has become increasingly evident not only as direct to consumer drug advertising but also as a means of promoting lucrative services, to the ultimate detriment of primary care.
