The importance of including the patient in the therapeutic management planning
Many skin diseases unfortunately have a tendency to turn into a chronic course after an initial phase of acute flare. This is especially true for the very common inflammatory skin diseases like eczema in all variants, psoriasis, chronic urticaria and many others. The phenomenon is frustrating for both patients and doctors, and this calls for a special commitment on the part of dermatologists. The three steps of 'diagnosis' 'therapy' and 'prevention' can be understood as a cycle since tertiary prevention includes all strategies to prolong the interval of remission preceding the next flare. The term 'diagnosis' not only includes the definition of the disease but also the evaluation of its severity, a crucial parameter concerning the choice of treatment. For many chronic skin diseases, there are instruments to measure severity by evaluating both signs and symptoms (e.g. itch, pain or sleeplessness) The impairment of quality of life can also be measured with a questionnaire such as the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) Attempts to measure not only the socioeconomic burden of a disease but also the actual intensity of individual suffering have been made in many chronic skin diseases. Therapy has become more and more complex and involves not only pharmacotherapy but also counselling activities, including explaining the principles of skincare in general, avoidance procedures (e.g. diets) information on effects and side effects and the very individual prognosis. This takes time much more time than usually available in a routine office consultation. It is absolutely mandatory to include the patient into considerations when planning a therapeutic management by giving him/her the necessary information and discussing the pros and cons before coming to a decision. Many seemingly trivial things are important. Below, I touch upon only some aspects of this process: 'Individualized treatment' is a magic word at the moment. It not only reflects therapeutic strategies for mutated genes in skin cancer one major progress in our specialty but also in everyday life from skincare to the selection of pharmacotherapy. Every patient is different; there is no uniform basic skincare prescription as every dermatologist knows. Often intra individual comparisons 'right versus left' with a new topical product allow a better choice in terms of efficacy but also in terms of acceptance. This holds true for emollients and topical medications, as well as hygiene procedures such as skin cleansing. Also the body area involved has to be taken into consideration. We all know that water in oil versus oil in water emulsions function differently according to different body areas. The decision for the right galenics is often more important than the choice of the actual pharmacologic active substance in the preparation. The search for individual trigger or provocation factors is central in the management of chronic inflammatory skin diseases. This can only be done by meticulous history taking, i.e. talking and listening to the patient. It has been proven helpful for the patient to keep a diary registering everyday events. The results of these observations have to be discussed between the doctor and the patient and sometimes supplemented by special tests such as allergy diagnostics. In this way, hitherto unknown elicitors can often be determined. In chronic inflammatory skin diseases, there are not only specific elicitors but also so called unspecific augmentation factors like acute infection, physical exercise (e.g. urticaria) psychologic stress (e.g. eczema) intake of certain drugs (e.g. beta blockers in psoriasis) intake of pharmacologically active substances like ethanol (e.g. rosacea) The individual evaluation of the relevance of these factors makes life much easier; instead of forbidding everything, one can concentrate on the truly relevant factors. Dietary recommendations have to be discussed in this background. Many of our patients undergo severe and sometimes even hazardous diets taken from magazines or witchcraft medicine. It makes no sense to generally disregard these efforts when the patients believe in them. Furthermore, there is an obvious interaction between the gastrointestinal tract and the skin, although at the moment this is pathophysiologically not well explained. The application of probiotics in order to restore a healthy gastrointestinal flora may have some effects. In many countries, there is a tendency to resort to so called unconventional methods when classical 'medical school' approaches do not lead to a satisfactory outcome. In such a discussion, the doctor should never take the position of 'ex cathedra' The patient should never have to feel guilty for doing something. Yet, we should not lie to our patients and when they ask tell the truth that the evidence for unconventional methods is low or close to zero, but we should not start an ideological debate with Weltanschauung.
