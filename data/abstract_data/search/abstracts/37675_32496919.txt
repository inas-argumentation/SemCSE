Twins with Williams Beuren Syndrome and Discordance for Wilms Tumor
To the Editor: Williams Beuren syndrome (WBS) is a genetic disorder mostly caused by deletion of a single copy gene region (WBSCR) on q11.23 of chromosome 7.[1] Patients with WBS have distinctive facial appearance, cardiovascular abnormalities, short stature, mental retardation, and overfriendly personality.[1] Predisposition to malignancy in WBS is not established; however, several authors have reported malignant disease in children with WBS, predominantly non Hodgkin lymphoma and leukemia.[2] We report a case of Wilms tumor (WT) in one of identical twins with WBS. We confirmed the WBS diagnosis in both twins by determination of ELN deletion with fluorescence in situ hybridization.[1] They were concordant for short stature, facial appearance, positional valgus deformity of soles at birth, hyperkalemia during infancy, and cognitive delay. Twin B had an inguinal hernia at birth. All are characteristic for WBS, but genotype phenotype correlation is complicated by variable penetrance and other genetic and epigenetic differences in twins.[3,4] Twin A was diagnosed with WT at the age of 4.5 years, presenting with acute abdominal pain and vomiting. CT scan revealed a 6.5 x 8 cm2 tumor originating from left kidney and numerous lesions in the liver. Fine needle aspiration revealed primary WT with only liver metastases. Treatment included six preoperative cycles of chemotherapy per SIOPWilms 2001 protocol, followed by resection of the left kidney and hilar lymph node, then postoperative chemotherapy (etoposide, carboplatin, cyclophosphamide, and doxorubicin) for stage IV WT. Because histopathology showed residual regressive type WT (local stage I) and no vital liver metastases, radiotherapy was not administered. The patient is disease free 5 years off therapy. Twin B has asymmetry in kidney size and a left renal parenchymal cyst. Renal malformations are common in WBS,[5] but there are few reports of kidney function deterioration and no studies indicating risk of malignant alteration.[6] Some genes in WBSCR are involved in regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation, which could predispose to neoplasia in WBS.[1,7] Case reports of malignancies in patients with WBS include lymphoid, myeloid, and nonepithelial cancers but no susceptibility loci have been identified.[2,7 9] Uncertain association of malignancies andWBS could be explained by its low prevalence (1/7,500 1/20,000 births) and possible unrecognized WBS cases.[10] WT is genetically heterogeneous, with mutations in WT1, WTX, CTNNB1, and TP53 in only one third of cases.[11] In a genetic study of 60 patients with WT, Corbin et al. identified possible Wnt pathway target genes in WT tumorogenesis, including FZD9, positioned onWBSCR.[1,12] BCL7B, another Wnt pathway tumor suppressor gene located onWBSCR, could also contribute to malignancy in patients with WBS.[13] Such genes, togetherwith others involved inDNArepair (e.g. RFC2) could have a role in WT tumorogenesis. In this regard and due to inconclusive evidence of concordance forWT in twins,[14] we follow both twins regularly.
