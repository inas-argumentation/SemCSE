One of the most interesting aspects of the analyses is how the teachers are 'structuring' 'the Muslim pupil' or 'the Muslim pupils' This is done differently at the two schools and the constructions are rather complex. Categories like gender, social class, and cultural capital are combined with religion in different ways. At one of the schools, 'the Muslim pupils' are produced as a coherent and particularly low performing 'underclass' Mette Buchardt shows how the often good intention of connecting the content of the teaching to the kind of pupils actually populating the classroom may have negative effects in constructing separating entities and limited identities (73) Making 'pupils' part of the curriculum puts an emphasis on their 'experience' and 'backgrounds' i.e. they contribute with some 'other' type of knowledge. The personal is being attached to Muslimness whereas to be Christian is attached to being Danish and something universal. Part 4 summarises the main findings and presents a concluding analysis. As stated at the end of the book, "'To be Muslim' and 'to be Universal ChristianDanish' are indeed things you can learn in school" (180) It is a pity that the complexity of the book may be a barrier to a wider audience receiving and understanding this message.
