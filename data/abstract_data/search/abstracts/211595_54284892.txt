The My Health Record debate: ethical and cultural issues
In recent months a debate has raged across Australia about the introduction of a universal electronic health record system called the 'My Health Record' While the system itself has been in development for nearly 10 years, current interest has been stimulated by a decision to reverse the process for including individual citizens, from 'opt in' which attracted only a relatively small number of takers to 'opt out' according to which records would be automatically created if citizens failed to take action within a short time frame. The initial impetus to establish My Health Record arose from a perception that a centralised, widely accessible system would generate major benefits by improving coordination of care, helping avoid drug errors, preventing duplication of investigations and services, enhancing patients' control over their care and limiting other inefficiencies and inaccuracies inherent in more traditional systems for collecting and storing health information. Indeed, it has been claimed that the availability of an integrated, centralised, coordinated medical record system would save the country $11.5 billion dollars in healthcare costs by 2025. The challenge for policy makers and researchers is that, while individuals may derive personal benefit from My Health Record if they sign up, most other stakeholders, including government, researchers, health professionals and insurers, will benefit only if large numbers of the population join. In this regard, the problem has been that despite surveys showing high levels of support for the concept, relatively few people have actually signed up voluntarily and when the government decided to break the deadlock by declaring that inclusion would be on an opt out basis, a public furore was incited. While it is likely that, at least in part, this reflected an inherent mistrust of government intrusion into private domains of life, most of the public debate has focused on concerns about privacy and the extent to which individual health data would be made available to public agencies, including the police. The level of concern has, quite understandably, been fuelled by welldocumented accounts of data security breaches, both internationally and in Australia, by revelations in the press of abuses of data privacy by Facebook and other social media platforms, and by reports of the failings of similar health data linkage systems. After representations from multiple parties with an interest in the areas, including medical professional organisations, the Commonwealth Government recently agreed to amend the relevant legislation to enhance the protection of data and to prohibit access by the police. It has also extended the opt out period by 1 month and increased public education about My Health Record. While it remains to be seen how many Australians will choose to opt out, these measures appear largely to have allayed the public and professional anxiety and dampened criticism. But while the debate about privacy is an important one, there are additional ethical issues that are raised by the move to the My Health Record system that should also be considered. These include the assumptions behind the entire project, including the particular concept of the medical record on which it is based, what it signifies about how medical care has come to be understood by the public and health professionals and the ethical implications of the internet for our lives. The medical record is as old as medicine itself, certainly extending back to ancient Egypt and occupying an important role in the Hippocratic texts. However, historically, its roles and functions have varied widely. Most commonly, records were employed to share the knowledge and experience of doctors, and to provide an archive of the phenomena on which the developing discipline was based. For Hippocrates, Rufus of Ephesos, Galen and other important contributors, data were collected under headings such as 'nosology' 'symptomatology' 'prognosis' and 'therapeutics' Records were rarely, if at all, used to compile personal summaries of health information or to guide the treatment of individual patients. In the medieval period, Islamic physicians such as Muhammad ibn Zakariya al Razi further extended the medical record to include case histories and accounts of their own personal experiences. However, even here, records did not occupy an established role in patient management. In European and American experience, right up to the modern period, the focus remained on didactic and archival purposes, including education, research, statistical reporting and public health reviews.
