It is an uncanny image, a mirror image of a standard analogue clock Fig. 3.12 MLM floor plan with drawings clusters 3 THE MUSEO LABORATORIO DELLA MENTE 43 display, where the number nine has been redacted, and three clock hands spin at the centre of the face, seemingly with no relation to each other, nor to the standard measurement of time. Diane Mulcahy (2016) after Sara Ahmed, writes of how museum objects (exhibits, displays, and concepts) will, through affective transfer, become sticky; they will engender effects, give rise to new capacities for thinking, doing, and being, at least for some learners. For our participants, the 'stickiness' of Baeri's clock suggested intensities passing between their sensing bodies, the projected image, and the spoken words and gestures of the tour guide (this cluster was made up exclusively of Group A drawings) When participants' drawn images of Baeri's clock were spoken about, these intensities became specified in terms associated with the fear of losing order or losing the ability to perceive (and perceive time, in particular) This is an interesting finding given the museum's near celebratory exhibition of the visual works of Baeri and fellow patient artist oreste Fernando Nannetti as 'inventors of worlds' (the name given to Stage 5) The isolation room, found in a rear corner of the MLM building, also proved to be a 'sticky object' drawn into repeated relation with the bodies of participants and productive of forceful intensities. As an object, the room was already multiple: comprising a single bed with a metal railing type bed head, made up with a blanket and pillow. The domestic and quotidian qualities of this bed were interrupted by long, pale coloured restraining straps, albeit passively folded over the top of the blanket. Above the bed head was a barred window the window bars echoing the vertical rails of the bedhead with a view to the garden beyond the asylum/ museum. Nine drawings (from both Groups A and B) referenced this room, either in a figurative sense or by narrated reference. This was the numerically largest cluster within the category of exhibit focussed drawings. In one such drawing, fascia (straps) with belt holes for tightening, criss cross like highways (Fig. 3.13) of this depiction, the participant shared: [I] was inspired by the isolation room in which the patients were restrained with straps on the bed. HUGHES Catharine Coleborne, reflecting as a curator on the disproportionate interest in a straitjacket she exhibited, suggests 'perhaps nothing [comes] as close to the body of the patient for many visitors, as the canvas lockedjacket' (Coleborne, 2001, p. 114) This statement is, for us, suggestive of two things: some visitors are interested in patients' bodies (Coleborne explores this as a kind of fascination with the grotesque) the jacket being almost like a body on display (like a 'mummy' who is so ancient as to be immaterial, being given form only by cloth wrappings) while other visitors are interested in body doubling i.e. in empathically substituting their body for the patients by virtue of their physical proximity to an object that has been used on patients' bodies. The asylum's prohibition on cutlery, especially forks, is explained in a wallmounted exhibit in the same room. The exhibit is made up of dozens of metal forks seemingly scattered across and suspended on the vertical plane of the wall, at one end of which is a small video screen. In the other drawing in Fig. 3.13 (B5) a participant has illustrated one of the stories narrated in this exhibit via the video screen. While the participant remembered the violence of one patient's injury by fork as an accident, she shared that she 'was thinking about how an everyday object can become dangerous for someone who has a mental disease' The prohibition on cutlery at the asylum, however, had aimed to reduce self harm on the part of patients. The violence of this drawing, with a repetition of injury (two hands appeared stabbed) suggests a significant affective encounter between the participant, the forks exhibit and the narration by video screen. Had we merely interviewed the participant about this exhibit and heard what she had 'been thinking' in the cognitive wake of encountering the exhibit, this violence of her feelings would likely not have been evident. Having identified 'clusters' of drawings in Stages 5 and 6, but mindful of the small numbers of participants and drawings in our study, we offer here some initial thoughts regarding the findings and the methods we employed. our first response was to consider whether these exhibits, even as they were emotionally challenging to encounter, might have been 'representable' in ways that the experiences demanded by the earlier exhibits the initial Stage 1 'Enter Without, Exit Within' and Stage 2 'Ways of Feeling' encounters were not? Were participants on more comfortable ground, in their subjective positions as museum visitors and students, when they approached 'recreations' rooms 'as they would have been' and stories of 'real people' from the former institution? Were the exhibits that were undeniably about 'other' people, places, and times less threatening matter for representation than the shock of one's own personal experience of an optical illusion (in the Ames Room) or the feeling of being watched that is characteristic of standing at the threshold of the MLM?
