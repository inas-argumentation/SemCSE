Introduction: Why it's (still) kicking off everywhere
O ne of the two CITY sessions held at this year's Annual Meeting of the American Association of Geographers in Los Angeles explored BBC economic journalist Paul Mason's original and updated version of his book (2012) Why It's Kicking Off Everywhere. The New Global Revolutions and Why It's Still Kicking Off Everywhere (2013) In his account, he argued that 'Greece is the modern case study of what happens when the political elite of a developed country allows its legitimacy to go up in flames' An economic crisis turned social crisis, he argues, led to unrest and revolution, involving 'people nobody ever expected to resist' (2012, 39) and to questioning existing social relations beyond the presumably stable ideological framework of capitalism: 'the age of capitalist realism was over' (ibid. Moving from Greece to the USA, he explored how this sense of an ending and the beginning of something new panned out, and from Cairo to Manila, investigated the hopeful alternatives as emerging faces of a larger movement, the global revolution. His work has the characteristic depth of historical investigation and the immediacy of street based observation and digital dialogue. In the session, editors of CITY, building on work in the journal and beyond, discussed Mason's overall approach and its relevance for analysis, action and outcome in the light of their own experience, both in terms of activism and work. Four papers presented by Andrea Gibbons, Mark Davidson, Antonis Vradis and Kurt Iveson are collected here to extend the ensuing debate beyond the panel discussion. Individually as well as taken together they demonstrate the ever more crucial attempt to bridge theory and practice, journalism and academicism, scholars and activists. The session's theme, Mason's account of 'the new global revolutions' caused by the 'near collapse of free market capitalism' (2013, 3) continues to be one of significant (non )attention and for many increasingly a question of survival. Both the book and the four pieces describe moments and interpretations of revolt and crisis, yet differ with respect to interpreting their dynamics. The collection presents the different perspectives through which 'the general mood of uprising' (Gibbons) is explored and challenged. Questioning the importance of technology and 'the existence a new sociological type, the graduates with no future' as main characteristics of recent uprisings, from the Arab Spring via Syntagma Square and from St Paul's Cathedral to Gezi Park, Andrea Gibbons reflects on the necessary immediacy of grassroots organizing in Los Angeles and London.
