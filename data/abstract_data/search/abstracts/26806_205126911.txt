Preface on air quality in China.
Recent Global Burden of Disease studies have provided quantitative evidence of the significant risk of ambient pollution to human health globally, but particularly in Asia. In the mega cities of emerging Asian economies, the rapidly growing fleet of vehicles not necessarily low emitters contribute to overall air pollution. On a continental scale, power generation, with billions of tonnes of coal burned annually, household emissions, trans boundary pollution, and resuspended desert and exposed soil dust, all are the constituents of the ambient air pollution which is particularly acute in cities. By 2050, the world population is expected to grow to 9.3 billion, with 6.3 billion living in urban areas. China is at the forefront of this trend with 89 cities in 2010 exceeding one million of habitants, out of 442 such cities worldwide. The economic burden of the impact of air pollution on human life is evident around the world by the cost of health care. In addition to human health, it has been conclusively established that the release of the products of fossil fuel combustion into the air, which has accelerated exponentially over the past decades, has significant and long term consequences on the entire Earth system. The emissions originate from industrial, transport and energy generation sources, and have been studied and quantified in terms of carbon dioxide (CO2) as well as other greenhouse gases, including methane, nitrogen oxides and ozone. Based on this, predictions have been made about the future climate, particularly in terms of temperature increase,which in turn drives the evolution of the Earth as a whole. While the pressing need for lowering the emissions and significant improvement of air quality has been identified, the complexity of the problem requires input from a diverse mix of science, technology, innovation, health disciplines and policy. Further, to accelerate progress in cleaning the air, cross disciplinary and applied research, and most importantly, international collaboration is of crucial significance. In this context, it is our pleasure to present the STOTEN Special Issue (SI) focused on: Air Quality in China: Current and Emerging Challenges. The SI includes 51 manuscripts addressing a broad spectrum of topics from five general areas: (1) Primary pollution sources and their impacts on air quality; (2) Dynamic processes: secondary formation and transformations; (3) Indoor air and its relationship with outdoor air; (4) Health and exposure; (5)Mitigation: New technologies, policies and strategies. 14 of themanuscripts are reviews, which were conducted by large multidisciplinary, multi organization teams, and 38 manuscripts report findings from new studies. Even a quick browse through the titles of the manuscripts reveals the complexity of the problem that the experts are collectively undertaking to understand and to fix, as an ultimate goal. The sources extensively discussed in the SI are transport (vehicle and ship emissions) as well as biomass and coal burning. Further, sources of specific pollutants, compounds or their groups (particulate matter, VOCs, HULIS, PAHs, bacteria and metals) are covered in depths.
