Morrison, Bambara, Silko fractured and reconstructed mythic patterns in Song of Solomon, The salt eaters, and Ceremony
This dissertation explains the development of Northrop Frye's myth critical theory and examines the extent to which it can be seen as a meaningful paradigm with which to analyze English literary conventional development and the communication of archetype in works of postmodern fiction. By examining three African American and Native American novels in light of biblical archetype, West African religious ritual, Native American folklore, and the Frygian monomyth of the loss and regaining of identity, I suggest that Frye's theory is as credibly applicable to Native American and African American postmodern works as it is known to be to works of the traditional canon. This study traces the history of the myth critical approach and elucidates the presence of biblical archetype and structure in African American and Native American postmodern fiction and of the communication of continental African archetype to diasporic African jAmerican culture through an intermingling of ancient African religion and biblical archetype and structure. As an application of Frye's theory in an original context, this dissertation enriches our understanding of literary conventional development and of the scope of Frye's theories, and it challenges our notions of African American and Native American literature.
