In the last instance, Nash played the role of Rowan himself. As an Irishman, Nash was attracted to Joyce's Irish origins and to the Irish setting of the play. Also, since Exiles might be considered "noncanonical" in spite of its debt to Ibsen, Nash felt free to take certain liberties with Joyce's precise and detailed stage directions. A notable example is that Nash used an offstage recoding to "play" the part of Rowan's and Bertha's young son rather than a live actor. Nash's directorial choices are particularly refreshing in light of the "freedom" Rowan and presumably Joyce encourages throughout the play. Given the opportunity I would surely attend another performance of Exiles now that I have been immersed in the erudite responses others have taken away from the play. As for the intention of the editors to "give readers a renewed sense of the value of Exiles as an individual work and as part of Joyce's canon," it is admirably realized. Their work is indeed a fitting tribute to the 100th anniversary of Exiles. Even so, I am still no admirer of Richard Rowan.
