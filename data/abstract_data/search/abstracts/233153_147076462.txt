However, many of Breyer's experiences are so particular to her Episcopalian and metropolitan context that immediate relevance to diverse seminary experiences is less evident. Moreover, the underlying personal theme is one that may resonate with younger seminarians while seeming less relevant or even immature to the more typical, nowadays, mid life seminarian. The life stage issue Breyer wrestles with throughout her first year of seminary, and from pages xi to 256 in her memoirs, is that of special destiny versus ordinary life. This is particularly acute perhaps for a child of privilege and a Harvard "first class education." (Marketers, not the author, tell us also that she is the daughter of Supreme Court Justice Stephen Breyer. Breyer's favored biblical character is Queen Esther, whose role she had played in a Sunday school program as a child. The author clearly sees her own life situation in the rhetorical query put to Esther, "Who knows whether you have come into the kingdom for such a time as this?" (p. xviii) Yet the youthful notions and perhaps familial expectations of special. destiny run counter to her expenences of "ordinary time," liturgically and clerically. Surely one has been born for something of greater significance than the plethora of mundane tasks characterizing seminary and clergy life?
