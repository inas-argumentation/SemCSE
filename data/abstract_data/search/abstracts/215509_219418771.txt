Exemplary Textual Criticism
Recent years have witnessed a resurgence in the field of New Testament textual criticism. One of the stimuli for this is undoubtedly the increasing provision of high quality digital images of manuscripts, many freely available online. Another is the way in which computers have made it more straightforward to create, analyse and compare large datasets, such as evidence for the transmission of the biblical text. Accordingly, there has been an increase in doctoral projects such as the one behind this substantial volume which undertake a detailed examination of one or more of the thousands of surviving New Testament manuscripts, shed light on how these witnesses relate to the broader textual tradition and offer a fresh appreciation of their historical significance. The focus of Hixson's study is the text of Matthew in a group of three magnificent but now fragmentary manuscripts copied in the sixth century. All of them were written using gold ink on parchment which had been dyed purple, not from ground mollusc shells but by the use of different types of lichen. Despite their pages currently being dispersed across locations from New York to St Petersburg, Hixson proposes that these books were produced at the same scriptorium, probably in Constantinople, as gifts to churches built by Justinian I. It has long been known that their texts are very closely related, yet because they correspond largely to the standard Byzantine tradition scholars have dismissed them as 'quite irrelevant for textual criticism' (in the words of Kurt and Barbara Aland) Hixson's masterstroke is to realise that the relationship of these manuscripts means that they can be used to examine how a group of sixth century scribes copied books and what changes they introduced to the text. Interventions by copyists are usually a matter of conjecture, since most New Testament manuscripts cannot be compared with the documents from which they were produced as these exemplars have long been lost. The so called 'singular readings' method, developed by Hort, Colwell and Royse, identifies the readings unique to a particular manuscript and takes these as a guide to the 'scribal habits' of its copyist. Because these three manuscripts of Matthew were all copied from the same exemplar, by reconstructing this text Hixson can determine the ways in which each copy differs from its model. Comparing this information with the results which would have been produced by the 'singular readings' approach enables him to evaluate that method based on an actual historical scenario. This painstaking process results in a fascinating account of how each copyist worked. Hixson characterises Codex Purpureus Petropolitanus (majuscule N, GA 022) as the product of a younger scribe, who copied with care but struggled with the spelling of certain combinations of vowels, particularly at the end of words.
