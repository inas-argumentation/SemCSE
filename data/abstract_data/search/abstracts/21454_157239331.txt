Unraveling Sanitation and Sewerage Concerns in a Developing Country Metropolis: The Case of Metro Manila, Philippines
1. IntroductionUsed water or wastewater from family dwellings, commercial and industrial establishments eventually winds up in creeks, rivers and the aquifer. If not adequately treated, wastewater will pollute these water bodies which provide water supply, livelihood and recreation. Further, untreated wastewater causes diseases such as diarrhea, cholera, typhoid, dysentery, and hepatitis (WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme 2000 and 2012; Petri et al 2000; Thielman and Guerrant 1996; Bern et al 1992) A lack of sanitary sewerage systems is also associated with gastric cancer and peptic ulcer disease (Travis et al 2010).Cairncross (2003) noted that more than a third of the world's population did not have access to adequate excreta disposal facilities, and of these unserved populations 80% were in fast growing Asian economies. He also noted that even in large Asian cities less than half of the households with excreta disposal facilities such as a watersealed toilet were connected to sewerage systems. In the Philippines, sewerage systems consisting of sewer lines connecting households and establishments to sewage treatment plants covered only about 3.3% of the population (World Bank 2008a) Even in the foremost urban center of Metro Manila, sewer line system coverage covers only about 8% (Palanca Tan 2013).Metro Manila, the study site for this article, is the national capital region of the Philippines. It is the political, economic, social and cultural center of the Philippines. It is one of the more modern metropolises in Southeast Asia and is the world's 11th most populous. Covering an area of only 638 km2, Metro Manila is the smallest of the 17 regions but is the most populous (11.9 million in 2010, 13% of the entire Philippine population) and the most densely populated (18,113 per km2) according to the Philippine Census of Population and Housing (National Statistics Office 2010) Up to the present, planned sewerage expansion programs in Metro Manila have not advanced substantially. The Manila Waterworks and Sewerage Systems (MWSS) concessionaires attribute their inability to meet their sewerage and sanitation expansion commitments to financial and physical constraints.Kimura (2007) noted that sewerage was one area of public works that was farthest from being self supporting, giving as an example the case of Japan where interest and principal payments on loans for sewerage projects were more than twice the revenues from sewerage user charges. Hutton (2013) however, argued that improved sanitation can deliver significant economic returns to society. Using cost and benefit data from reviewed literature, he arrived at a global benefit cost ratio (BCR) of 5.5 (dollar return per dollar invested) for sanitation programs. A World Bank study (2008b) on the economic impact of poor sanitation in four Southeast Asian countries, namely, Cambodia, Indonesia, Philippines and Vietnam, yielded a combined loss of US$9 billion a year, approximately equivalent to 2% of the four countries' combined Gross Domestic Product (GDP) The economic impact figures for each of the four countries were US$6.3 billion (2.3% of GDP) in Indonesia, US$1.4 billion (1.5% of GDP) in the Philippines, US$780 million (1.3% of GDP) in Vietnam, and US$450 million (7.2% of GDP) in Cambodia. These estimates pointed to substantial gains that could be realized with improved sanitation. On a sub national level, Cronin et al (2014) arrived at a benefit cost ratio of about 5.7 or a net benefit of at least 1.6% of the state GDP from achieving full sanitation coverage for the state of Odisha in India.Whittington et al (2012) challenged the conventional wisdom that water and sanitation improvements would always be a wise economic investment and disputed the use of global wide estimates of benefits and costs in the decision making process. Along the same line of thought, Naughes and Whittington (2009) argued that decision makers should give high priority to information about how specific regions or groups of people respond to different service options and pricing schemes.
