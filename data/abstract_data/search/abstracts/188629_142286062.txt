Exemplary Teacher Voices on Their Own Development: Three Factors Influenced the Development of Exemplary Teachers: Specific Kinds of Professional Development, Collegial Support, and a Sense of Engaged Autonomy
American schools have adopted an array of methods to develop effective teachers. There are databases on student achievement, value added formulas, merit scales, professional portfolios, and massive mentoring, induction, and recruitment schemes. The Bill Melinda Gates Foundation has begun a $43 million project to identify ways to measure effective teaching. What is missing are the voices of exemplary teachers themselves. In a nationwide study, we asked 30 exemplary 4th grade teachers working in high poverty elementary schools to describe factors that influenced their development (Allington and Johnston 2002; Allington, Johnston, and Day 2002) The teachers from New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Texas, California, and Wisconsin were selected based on recommendations from their local teachers, administrators, and university professors. We asked local educators to recommend teachers with reputations for effectively teaching students who were considered "at risk" or below grade level, and these recommendations were supported by a pattern of high student achievement across state and other standardized tests (Allington and Johnston 2002) Though these teachers espouse different education philosophies, teach in different states, and use different methods, materials, and programs, they cite the same three influences on their development: specific kinds of professional development, collegial support, and a sense of engaged autonomy. PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT The exemplary teachers in our study all mentioned certain professional development programs as influential in their development as teachers. Since the teachers had such a wide range of experience, they had not all taken a single program or training, but there was a clear pattern to the methods and outcomes of professional development that teachers valued. Teachers valued programs that provided a systematic way to observe and interpret students' work and actions. One example of a valued professional development program was training for Reading Recovery, which teaches a specific framework for observing and designing instructional responses for struggling readers. Jannelle (all names are pseudonyms) from New York explained that Reading Recovery helped her "understand reading strategies, organize [her] own hierarchy of how a child learns to read, what the strategies look like, how to support those strategies and push them to the next level, how to keep doing that constant stretching that helps a child become an independent problem solving type of a reader." Another teacher mentioned a course about writing IEPs, which likewise provided a structure and set of guidelines for collecting meaningful information through informal, ongoing observations. Similarly, Elizabeth from New York highlighted a workshop that introduced her to cooperative learning strategies, noting that they freed her from always being at the front of the classroom. She explained that the course empowered her to observe students more closely as she circulated to coach them while they worked. She told us, "My second year [of teaching] they sent me away for a weeklong workshop on hands on learning, and I came back a changed woman. I was so much more student sensitive. I just remember realizing then that the children were little humans rather than something that we just directed. I started giving the children more of a say in the classroom and becoming more involved instead of being the person in front of the room." Regardless of the advertised focus of the professional development program, teachers valued those who offered them a new, systematic way to observe and learn about their students and ideas of how to respond to what they saw. Exemplary teachers did not mention professional development opportunities that focused on methods, materials, or strategies other than ways of observing and interpreting evidence of student learning as a source of growth.
