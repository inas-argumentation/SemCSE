Paradoxically, the Spanish migrants to the New World, who became the socially and politically dominant group, have received short shrift in Spanish and Spanish American history. This neglect has left a vacuum in our understanding of the transfer of institutions, and of the personal experiences of those who participated in what was one of the most significant emigration movements in human history. Taking the Spanish western province of Extremadura as her scenario, Altman studies the migration qualitatively by focusing on the lives ofthe people who left for "the Indies." She selected the towns ofCaceres and Trujillo for this monograph. The latter was the birthplace of the conqueror of Peru, Francisco Pizarro. Despite the starkness and relative poverty of the region, Extremadura produced two religious military orders, had an important and popular religious shrine, and providedinfluential political leadership in the sixteenth century. Those who achieved name and prosperity left Extremadura, either for Castileor for the Americas. Emigration was the key to their success. Altman focuses on the features of the communities left behind by the emigrant. After briefly outlining the history of Caceres and Trujillo, she examines the main socioeconomic features of the population following an hierarchical and occupational outline: nobles, commoners, the clergy and professionals. The lifestyle and the networks established through family and kinship are central elements of the analysis. Women, children, dowries and inheritance receive due attention and add considerable texture to the study. The process ofemigrationitselfcovers recruitment, financing, destinations and family structure of the emigrants. Altman also follows the emigrants in their diverse careers in the New World and their ties with their Spanish roots. In the chapters dealing with the physical movement ofthe population, Altman displays her ability to convert an anatomical dissection into a lively and dynamic picture of the human dimension of emigration, and the interconnectedness ofthe migrants in their old and their new scenarios. This is a solid work backed by intensive research in Spain, especially in the local archives of the towns under survey. The centrality oflocal data makes a good case for the need to make of such sources the core of any emigration study. One positive result of this study is to put the Spanish settlers in a context that makes them closer to other European emigrants, and less the stereotyped truculent conquistadors of simplistic narratives. Altman also succeeds in demonstrating her thesis that the history of both Spain and Spanish America in the sixteenth century will remain incomplete ifwe fail to recognize and underline the coherent line of development between the Spanish historical experience and that which began to take place in the New World after 1492.
