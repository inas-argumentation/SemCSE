Indigenous Cities: Urban Indian Fiction and the Histories of Relocation by Laura M. Furlan (review)
In Indigenous Cities: Urban Indian Fiction and the Histories of Relocation, Laura M. Furlan explores the aesthetics of urban Indian writing, understanding how its narratives might differ from those with a reservation orientation and appreciating how place may be experientially reconstructed. Her book centers on the contemporary texts of Janet Campbell Hale, Sherman Alexie, Louise Erdrich, and Susan Power. The writing of Gerald Vizenor informs much of her analysis of these writers; she applies Vizenor's concepts of post Indian survivance and Native presence while also adding the lens of transnationalism with its emphasis on frontiers, borders, and liminal zones. The introduction establishes some of her thematic concerns via the 1961 film The Exiles, which centers on a twelve hour period in the life of urban Indian transplants. The post relocation era is documented in much the same way that House Made of Dawn (1968) sets up the same time period in Los Angeles, but Furlan's discussion draws out the way identities are remade and the sense of intertribal community is established. The sum of these new senses of identity are manifest in Janet Campbell Hale's The Jailing of Cecelia Capture (1985) which Furlan sees as an anti homing novel that establishes its female protagonist in an unapologetically urban setting. Cecelia's disenfranchised identity is alienated from the reservation, yet the aura of estrangement permeates Cecelia's vision. For Furlan, this standpoint facilitates the creation of a new urban Indian womanhood. Furlan explores the way Hale works through issues of gender by turning to descriptions of body image, hair, color, skin, and other physical icons in order to show that Cecelia creates a nonstereotypical political effectiveness and, hence, a Native presence. The character's self determination leads to awareness of the necessity of intertribal activism. In her chapter on Alexie, Furlan traces a shift from an Indian ambivalence toward the city to a more urban stance, but here disaffiliation is figured in the presence of homeless Indians. Furlan explores the colonial understructure of this phenomenon as a way to set up a vision of Alexie's metaphorical use of the urban Ghost Dance and an analysis of three of his texts: Indian Killer (1996) "What You Pawn I Will Redeem" (2003) and Flight (2007) In Indian Killer, Alexie challenges any sense of belonging and questions intertribal unity as he turns the homeless into ghosts.
