Re: Honzik T, Adamovicova A, Smolka V, Magner M, Hruba E, Zeman J. Clinical presentation and metabolic consequences in 40 breastfed infants with nutritional vitamin B12 deficiency what have we learned? Eur Jour Ped Neurol 14 (2010) 488 495.
The observation that 40 breastfed infants and small toddlers out of approximately 5000 children referred to the participating centers with suspected metabolic disease had nutritional cobalamin deficiency, is highly interesting. The inclusion criteria were described as "low or borderline cobalamin level in serum in breastfed infants, causing high urinary methylmalonic acid excretion and/or high plasmahomocysteine concentration, reversible on enteral cobalamin substitution" They found that all but one infant had methylmalonic aciduria, and 80% had hyperhomocysteinemia. Unfortunately, they do not report the frequency of raised methylmalonic acid excretion in urine in the cohort of approximately 5000 children, nor whether cobalamin levels were determined in all infants with raised methylmalonic acid excretion, or whether enteral cobalamin substitutionwas performed in all. Similar observations have been published in numerous case reports and case series in the past, many of which they refer to. Methylmalonic excretion in urine has been shown to be a sensitive and reliable indicator of cobalamin deficiency (1) Therefore, we think that in their discussion on screening, preventive and diagnostic measures and pitfalls, Honzik et al. should have emphasized the role of methylmalonic acid excretion in urine as a sensitive test for nutritional vitamin B12 deficiency. This easy, non invasive test should be performed in any breastfed infant or toddler presenting with failure to thrive or developmental delay regardless of the maternal nutritional habits and can be used as a follow up parameter in those with cobalamin deficiency.
