Facial Recognition, Big Data, and Close Readings: Tracing the Asset in the Bourne and Snowden Trailers
Many scholars worry that we are accelerating into socioeconomic and technological configurations for which we are unprepared. Vectors with steep trajectories include the information sciences: artificial intelligence, robotics, big data, psychometrics, and biogenetics. Predictions include a 40% unemployment rate thanks to robots; CRISPR editing that will create pressures for engineered babies; corporations that can bypass resumes because Big Data and artificial intelligence (AI) will have already quantified us; advertisers that will more aggressively direct our attention; privacy that steadily diminishes and an environment that deteriorates; dark money that will increasingly corrupt politics; and a resistance that will end up broken. With such dire forecasting, our imagining dystopian and utopian futures more closely may prove helpful. Negative potential outcomes, for example, may feel conceptual, but consider, in 2019, that out of the box, inexpensive drones powered by machine learning can hunt people. Small, prosumer wireless cameras linked to the Cloud and powered by AI can choose how and when to videotape subjects whether a child or a spouse's lover. Dog like robots can open doors and fend off whoever blocks their way. Facebook requested we use their facial recognition software, and then suffered a breach reaching potentially more than 90 million accounts to unknown sources. Barbra Streisand cloned her dog. Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) has developed a headset that can "hear" internalized voices and speak to its user through a bone conduction system. Advertising publications encouraged Fortune 500 companies to mine old data, seeking moments when clients became frustrated or enraged during phone calls or email exchanges.
