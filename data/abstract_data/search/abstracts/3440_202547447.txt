CPSC 490 Final Report Visualizing Convolutional Neural Networks Trained on Neural Data Recorded from Mouse Visual Cortex
Understanding human vision is interesting both for its own sake and for the potentially revolutionary applications, such as improving vision systems for self driving cars. Mice are a good proxy for studying the human visual system and have long been used to this end. This project explores what might be learned about the visual system of mice by analyzing a neural network model trained to simulate it. While past work has shown that convolutional neural networks can accurately predict responses to visual stimuli of cells in the retina, this project aims to assess whether deep convolutional networks are a good model for cells in primary visual cortex. A video of Gaussian noise and the neural activity of mice watching the video were, respectively, used as the input and output data for training a convolutional neural network. The network was not trained with the goal of maximizing performance on some test data set, but rather gaining insight into what the model is doing, accomplished by applying visualization techniques to the trained model. Training the network revealed the difficulty of reproducing research publication results, the abundance of trivial minima in the optimization space, and the importance of Poisson negative log likelihood loss in avoiding such trivial minima. Visualization of the convolutional layer units demonstrated that the network indeed learned frequency and orientation selectivity, but also suggested that mere noise videos are not enough to train a robust model to simulate the behavior of primary visual cortex. Rather, patterns and natural scenes may be required. Introduction and Background Previous assessments of the mouse visual system seemed to show that mice have poor visual capacity, not responding to visual stimuli at spatial frequencies greater than 0.5 cycles per degree. However, new classes of high frequency flow stimuli were shown to elicit strong responses from mice, demonstrating that their visual system is more complex than previously thought.1 Neural networks, originally inspired by the brain, have been used to study sensory systems in the brain2 and model the retina and visual cortex in mice. Maheswaranathan et al. demonstrated that convolutional neural networks trained on natural scenes were able to accurately predict neural responses to visual stimuli, and moreover, that internal units of the model exhibited behaviors characteristic of the visual system despite not being trained on them (e.g. motion anticipation and fast contrast adaption).3
