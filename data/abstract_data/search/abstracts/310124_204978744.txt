Lessons from Massachusetts: cost remains a hurdle for those with public insurance.
A S AN EMERGENCY DEPARTMENT physicianatMasschusetts'second largest "safety net" hospital in Cambridge, Danny McCormick, MD, MPH,seespatientshavingahardtimeaccessing health care despite the adoption of state health reform in 2006. Although many assumed that improved access to health coverage would also improve access to health care, McCormick said, patients who have obtained insurance through the state often find that cost remains a barrier to care. McCormick, an assistant professor at Harvard Medical School, and his colleagues surveyed 431 patients presenting to the emergency department at Cambridge Hospital in July 2009 and March 2010 (McCormick D et al. J Gen Intern Med. doi:10.1007/s11606 012 2173 7[published online July 24, 2012] The good news was that patients insured by Medicaid or statesubsidized Commonwealth Care reported having a primary care clinician or other nonemergency source of regular care at about the same rate as those who were privately insured. Uninsured individuals were far less likely to report having a regular care provider. Both groups reported access to preventive care, which does not require any cost sharing. Those with public insurance, however, reported more difficulty finding a physician who would accept their insurance. "At least in this setting, [health reform] allowed patients to get in the door," said McCormick. Still, cost remained a barrier to getting needed care for many individuals covered by public insurance. For example, 42.2% of those covered by Medicaid or Commonwealth Care type 1, which has the lowest co payments of the state subsidized options, reported delaying or forgoing dental care because of cost, compared with only 27.1% of privately insured individuals, while 30% reported forgoing medication, compared with only 7% of privately insured individuals. Those with the higher cost sharing options (such as Commonwealth Care 2 or 3, which are available to individuals making 150% 300% of the national poverty level) reported even greater difficulty with medical costs, with 45% reporting cost as a barrier to obtaining health care in general (compared with 16% of those privately insured) 25% reporting cost as a barrier to accessing primary care (vs 6% of those privately insured) and 58.3% reporting difficulty accessing a dentist because of cost (vs 27% of those privately insured) Thesedifficultiesoccurreddespitevery modestcost sharing for thosewithCommonwealth Care, which has no deductible,co payments forofficevisits ranging from $0 to $22 (depending on income levels) and medication co payments rangingfrom$1to$50,accordingtoMass Resources, a nonprofit providing information about the program (http:/www .massresources.org/commonwealth care.html#whatis) Maximum out ofpocket costs are $1500 or less, depending on income. McCormick explained, however, that even small co payments can be problematic. For example, an individual who is making 150% of the poverty level makes just under $1400 per month, according to MassResources. But McCormick noted that a 1 bedroom apartment in Boston would cost $1500 a month. Harold Pollack, PhD, the Helen Ross Professor at the University of Chicago's School of Social Services and faculty chair of the Center for Health Administration Studies, said the findings add to a large body of literature suggesting that low income individuals are surprisingly sensitive to price and that they may not always respond in ways that administrators expect.
