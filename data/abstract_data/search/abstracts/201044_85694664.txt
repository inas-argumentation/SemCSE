Polygyny Leads to Disproportionate Recruitment in Urban Cooper's Hawks (Accipiter cooperii)
Excluding harriers (Circus spp. and the Boreal Owl (Aegolius funereus) polygyny is rare in most species of raptors and more often occurs under conditions of food abundance and in rodent eating birds of prey (Korpimaki 1988, Korpimaki and Hakkarainen 2012, Simmons et al. 1986, Rosenfield et al. 2007a) We genetically confirmed paternity of a male Cooper's Hawk (Accipiter cooperii) in successful polygyny in a high density population in the city of Grand Forks, North Dakota, where the predominant prey for this species is birds (Rosenfield et al. 2007a, T. Driscoll unpubl. data) To our knowledge, ours was the first study to report polygyny in Cooper's Hawks, and the first documentation of successful polygyny in Accipiter; that is, all three young fledged in each of two simultaneous nests in 2006. The male's reproductive output may have been enhanced in this urban setting, whereas that of the females may have been compromised as each female's output was below the average number of young fledged in successful nests in our urban population. The male's collective production of six young from both nests exceeded by one the maximum brood size of all but one monogamous pair of Cooper's Hawks in Grand Forks (Rosenfield et al. 2007a, T. Driscoll unpubl. data) Production of six young in one nest is rare among Cooper's Hawk populations (Rosenfield et al. 2007a, Millsap et al. 2013) We here update and provide further novel ecological events across 11 yr in our Grand Forks study area regarding this episode of polygyny. Conceivably, this unusual behavior was individually adaptive as all three adults produced recruits (i.e. breeders) and that a proportionately large number of total detected recruits in our study area occurred from this one instance of polygyny. We highlight that it is rare for investigators to report (or have) data on recruits from episodes of polygyny, especially in a comparative, longterm manner. Instead, most avian studies index fitness of polygyny (as with monogamy) via production of nestlings and/or fledglings, which typically are not studied beyond fledging age (e.g. Simmons et al. 1986, Marks et al. 1989, Rosenfield et al. 2000, Korpimaki and Hakkarainen 2012) Our results are relevant to the biology of avian mating systems and to the documentation and understanding of the ecology of urban breeding Cooper's Hawks (Estes and Mannan 2003, Nicewander and Rosenfield 2006, Stout et al. 2007, Rosenfield et al. 2007a, Rosenfield and Sobolik 2014) We monitored a breeding population of Cooper's Hawks within the adjoining cites of Grand Forks and East Grand Forks (hereafter Grand Forks) North Dakota and Minnesota, respectively, with a collective human population of approximately 60 000 (Rosenfield et al. 2007a, 2010) We found nests opportunistically in these cities during 2004 2005, but beginning in 2006 we annually canvassed by vehicle and on foot a 3669 ha study area in Grand Forks during the pre incubation and early incubation periods (April late May) for presence of breeding pairs and/or partially constructed nests. We found about 15 nests each year in this study area. Breeding adult Cooper's Hawks were trapped, aged, and sexed following methods in Rosenfield and Bielefeldt (1993, 1997) Adults and nestlings about 2 3 wk of age were sexed (males noticeably smaller [Rosenfield et al. 1996] and banded with U.S.G.S. lock on aluminum leg bands as well as colored, alphanumerically coded leg bands. We sometimes refer to the polygynist birds by their color marks, N/N the male and 8/C and P/2 his mates. We measured body mass of adults to the nearest 1 g with a balance beam or digital scale. We used body mass at initial capture of an individual during the nestling stage as an index to body size. Body mass at the nestling stage is a reliable index to the size of breeding adults in Grand Forks and elsewhere across northern portions of this species' breeding range in North America (Rosenfield et al. 2010, Sonsthagen et al. 2012) Data on recruits were derived from hawks we banded as nestlings and captured as breeding birds in later years.
