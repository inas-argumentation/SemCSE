Critical Care Medicine www.ccmjournal.org e343 guidelines, Surviving Sepsis Campaign: International Guidelines for Management of Sepsis and Septic Shock 2016 (3) have recommendations based on the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation system, which emphasize randomized controlled trials that tend to enroll robust patients. This suggests that treatments for sepsis in frail patients, including those in the author's study, may have been inadequate. One example of this is nutrition. Nutritional support is very important for frail patients because frailty is usually related to sarcopenia. The current guideline suggests early hypocaloric feeding as well as full feeding (3) but the safety of hypocaloric feeding in patients with sarcopenia has not been well evaluated. An observational study found that adequate energy intake was associated with better outcomes with low body mass index (4) Thus, before giving up on treating sepsis in frailty, we should look for treatments that are optimized for them. Second, as the authors themselves noted, their study may have included patients with treatment limitations. In a previous study, do not attempt resuscitation was independently associated with mortality in ICU patients (5) Furthermore, treatment limitations make sepsis treatment difficult. For example, patients with limited use of mechanical ventilation or blood purification therapy may not be given adequate fluid resuscitation. Third is the question of cost. The authors reported that frailty was associated with increased medical costs. However, most of the increased costs were for services from nonphysician health professionals and nursing care. Although this may depend on labor costs and the insurance system, most of these costs may be necessary even if patients were admitted to a general ward. Also, although the duration of mechanical ventilation was longer in frail patients, the cost of respiratory therapy did not differ significantly between frail and nonfrail patients. Therefore, regardless of whether the patient is admitted to the ICU, the higher medical costs of frail patients may be unavoidable. Cost effectiveness should be assessed not by whether the patient is frail or not but by whether the frail patient is hospitalized in the ICU or a general ward.
