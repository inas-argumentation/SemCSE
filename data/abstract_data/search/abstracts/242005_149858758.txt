Why We Have to Talk About Race When We Talk About Religion
The question, "Why did the professor make us do x when we were there to do y?" is familiar to any experienced professor. It's shown up most commonly on my student evaluations as: "This wasn't an English class! Why did we have to write so much?" and, "I thought I was going to learn about what Christians believe, and now I'm just confused by all the questions." The questions reveal misalignment between student and teacher. Students expect one kind of learning agenda and the professor offers another or offers the agenda in a way students find disagreeable. Sometimes students are upset at having to venture into unexpected territory and sometimes they are angry, feeling that the professor has pulled a "bait and switch" on them. In my experience and admittedly, my experience is limited these feelings are the rawest, and most visceral when it comes to talking about racism and feminist theory. A particular version of the students question has stuck with me over the years. Its appearance in a class conversation years ago helped shaped my scholarly agenda, and form my thoughts on teaching about race more broadly. For these reasons it will frame my discussion on "Creating Brave Spaces" a discussion I undertake from the social location of White, (cisgender) female, Roman Catholic educator. In an undergraduate course on world religions, a young White student raised his hand during class discussion and asked, "Why do you want us to talk about race when we're supposed to be learning about religion?" His tone was not aggressive or disdainful, but the nature of the question implied challenge, even defiance. I will elaborate the context for his question below, but for now it's important to note my straightforward response: "Because examining racial diversity helps us get clarity about religious diversity." We had just begun our final section of the course, exploring interreligious dialogue after having spent the semester learning about the so called "major" world religions Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, and Islam while also learning about Christianity. (My school is Roman Catholic, and I approached the course through the framework of comparative theology. The aim of studying dialogue rather than just doctrine was to ground what the students learned. I hoped to deliver my students to a place where they could think about how they might practically encounter non Christian religious traditions in "real life." Students read the final chapter of Diana Eck's spiritual autobiography, Encountering God.
