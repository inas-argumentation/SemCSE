Such an advance would provoke challenging questions about our notions of liberty and personal autonomy, with potentially chilling ramifi cations. To be sure, neuroimaging is in its early stages, despite the hype about it and the spike in the number of neurologic studies using fMRI. Many challenges remain in the quest to derive accurate inferences from neuroimaging. A major one is that the measurement of blood fl ow is merely a correlate for neural activity, under the assumption that the more often neurons are fi ring, the more they depend on energy rich, oxygenated blood. But the link between neural activity and blood fl ow isn't always proportional, as one study at Columbia found with monkeys. Another problem is the complexity of the statistical analysis that must be sorted through after a brain is scanned; false results are easy to come by, according to neuroscientist Craig Bennett, who published a study (in the Journal of Serendipitous and Unexpected Results) showing the misleadingly active regions of a dead salmon's brain in an fMRI machine. Even if the science advances far enough that the combination of neuroimaging and existing clinical tools is effective for predicting criminal behavior, great care would still be needed in using it. It would be a double edged sword. On the up side, it could help society in a number of ways. It might lead to support for research into ways to rehabilitate known criminals work that is underfunded today. A clear brain picture of psychopathy might also help prevent violent offenders from being released into society too soon, given a sound way to rate their likelihood of recidivism. But important distinctions exist in the ways society might react to such predictions. Protecting the public from convicted criminals would be a noble application, yet what about using the predictions to penalize people whose scans simply look dangerous? The possibility looms that the data might be used in other ways by the government, by schools, by employers. What uses would be misuses? Would it be reasonable to act on knowledge that a person had been deemed likely to commit a crime because of low activity in the anterior cingulate cortex, the brain region associated with decision making and impulse control, even if the person had not committed any illicit acts? If such information became public knowledge, it seems a slippery slope to go from potentially being a productive member of society to a scorned one denied admission to college, fi red at work, even stripped of the right to raise one's own children. It does not seem a stretch to imagine a scenario in which the government would mandate that everyone with a high enough likelihood of criminal activity must be monitored or even removed from society altogether for the sake of the greater good even if no crimes were previously committed. For anyone who values individual rights, this possibility is alarming. Acting on biological predictions alone would undermine the Fourteenth Amendment's commitment to equal protection for all. "Innocent until proven guilty" is not just a proverb; it's a fundamental principle of justice that must withstand assault from biological determinists if our country is to remain free. That defense rests on moral, not scientifi c, warrants. If and as brain imaging improves and biomarker correlates emerge for criminal behavior, it is imperative to remember that people commit crimes not brains. A scan does not necessarily imply a lack of autonomy. People deserve to be treated and judged on the basis of their actions, not on their perceived biological defects.
