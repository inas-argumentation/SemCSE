Freud 100 years ago. On beginning the treatment further recommendations on the technique of psychoanalysis (I) The theme of the three caskets
To begin with the scope of these two writings, they would appear as quite distinct. The first, a piece of straightforward technical writing, dealing in detail with the analytical situation, the second aims far beyond that. The latter can duly be categorized as "applied psychoanalysis" If placed side by side, they may, however, bear witness to the breadth of Freud's oeuvre. In this way, we are introduced to two different fields. And we should take note that, even if Freud must have felt more at home when writing On beginning the treatment, carrying it through to an end was postponed again and again. As is implied by the subtitle, this work is part of a series of what later was to be called Freud's technical papers. Altogether they constitute 6 papers published between 1911 and 1915, the one which we focus on here being the third in the series. What was it that made Freud doubt, causing him to put it aside on several occasions? This question stands out on the background of the relatively few technical papers that were to be published later on in Freud's authorship. It appears even more odd when we note the range of important topics Freud actually covers in the course of this article. Various considerations concerning the beginning of treatment are presented. Why one must start with a trial period, and furthermore, how preliminary discussions before beginning the treatment, can prove disadvantageous. The historical background for the psychoanalytic session, the use of the couch, is depicted as well as some reasons for maintaining it "I cannot put up with being stared at by other people for 8 hours a day." On the matter of what the patient is expected to do, Freud recounts the famous image of a traveller sitting next to the window of a railway carriage and describing to someone inside the carriage the changing views which he sees outside. What if we ourselves were made the object of an analytic investigation? Nothing would be liable to provoke more intense resistance, Freud tells us, and adds that few experiences can be more persuasive when it comes to making us appreciate "the dimension of depth in the mind" Other delicate issues concerning time and money, where Freud's attitude, warning against prudishness and inconsistency, may at first strike us as rigorous. However, taking into consideration his awareness of which issues may surface in these respects as to money, "powerful sexual factors are involved" his way of dealing with them turns out as admirably matterof fact. As to the issue of time, the fact that Freud saw his patients 6 times a week is well known for most of us. We know the "Monday crust" was of special concern. The considerably shorter length of time which was necessary for psychoanalytic treatment is also evident. Psychoanalysis, Freud says, arguing against those who see it as a great expenditure of time, "is always a matter of long periods of time, of half a year or whole years " Nevertheless, just a few lines further on, Freud sees attempts at shortening analytic treatment as justified. How differently these matters are viewed today! But still, when faced with Freud's considerations, we are doomed to rethink what appear as well established facts concerning the duration of treatment. To what extent are we simply lead by undisputable findings, for instance from research within developmental psychology of which Freud could not possibly have known? Or are the viewpoints we firmly endorse today more due to a change of culture or paradigm? To read Freud today is not only to be informed of what the founder of psychoanalysis might have thought about these matters, it may also stir reflections upon what is indeed the foundation or reasoning on how we do things clinically in modern times. Freud's viewpoints on ending treatment are remarkable. He tells us that he does not "bind" his patients to continue treatment for a certain length of time "I allow each one to break off whenever he likes" adding, however, that he does not hesitate to tell the patient that the unfinished operation may leave him dissatisfied. Surprisingly, he sums up this section by indicating the situation at present is that he has to induce patients to give up analysis. As the title indicates, however, this article is primarily about the beginning of treatment. The fundamental rule is presented more fully here than previously, as Freud is preoccupied with how we can let the patient understand from the start that he or she should do the talking.
