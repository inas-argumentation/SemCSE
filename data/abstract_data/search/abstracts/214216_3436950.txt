Low volume, high intensity: Time efficient exercise for the treatment of hypertension
High blood pressure is one of the leading global risks for mortality and is responsible for 13% of deaths globally. Global trends during the last 40 years show an increase in raised blood pressure worldwide, mainly in low income and middle income countries due to population growth and ageing, while the prevalence of raised blood pressure in high income countries has decreased. However, with the new 2017 ACC/AHA Guidelines changing the definition of high blood pressure (blood pressure >130/80mmHg instead of >140/90mmHg) the proportion of adults in the United States labelled as having hypertension has suddenly increased from 32% to 46% Nonpharmacological interventions of hypertension are therefore more important than ever and should be offered to all individuals. According to the guidelines DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet can reduce systolic blood pressure by 11mmHg, reduced intake of dietary sodium can reduce systolic blood pressure by 5 6mmHg and aerobic exercise (90 150min/week) can reduce systolic blood pressure by 5 8mmHg. The guidelines recommend exercise for the prevention, treatment and control of hypertension. The blood pressure reduction by exercise matches the magnitude of that obtained with first line antihypertensive medications. In a recent issue of EJPC, Gorostegi Anduaga and colleagues evaluate the effectiveness on blood pressure of 16 week intervention in 175 overweight/obese, sedentary participants with hypertension (EXERDIETHTA study) The participants were randomized to attention control (DASH dietth physical activity recommendations) or one of three groups with supervised aerobic exercise programs twice per week and DASH diet: high volume of moderate intensity continuous training (HV MICT, 45min) high volume of highintensity interval training (HV HIIT, 45min) and low volume of high intensity interval training (LV HIIT, 20min) Contrary to their hypothesis, there was no between group differences after intervention and all groups including the attention control reduced systolic blood pressure by 7 9mmHg. At baseline, 87% of the participants were using antihypertensive drugs, and the medication could be withdrawn in 8% and reduced in 38% of the participants. The dropout rate was only 7% which is impressive since the intervention was in overweight/obese and sedentary individuals. Physical activity is believed to be important especially in the early stage of hypertension, since the increase in blood pressure is mainly mediated through increased cardiac output and more manifest vascular changes with increased peripheral resistance have not yet developed. Unfortunately, there was no available information of how long the participants had been on antihypertensive medication. In the ENCORE Study the participants were not on antihypertensive medication and the reduction in blood pressure after intervention was greater compared with EXERDIET HTA study. DASH diet only (DASH A) group achieved significant blood pressure reductions and adding an exercise and weight loss program (DASH WM) led to an even greater decrease in blood pressure. At 16 weeks, systolic blood pressure was reduced by 16mmHg in the DASH WM group, 11mmHg in the DASH A group and 3mmHg in the usual care group.
