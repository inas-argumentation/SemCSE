Predicting the future for recurrent respiratory symptoms in young children: applying a dash of science to the art of medicine.
At the medical school entry interview, students in the United Kingdom are highly likely to be asked the following question: 'Why do you want to be a doctor? Many will respond, 'because I am good at science and want to help people, andmedicine seems to be the right way to do this. Those who are awarded a place at medical school experience an epiphany during their first year when they discover that there is little science behindmuch clinical decision making. Asthma is one of a number of conditions that might awakens a student's consciousness to the art of medicine because the diagnosis is commonly made despite there being no unifying definition or diagnostic test. The diagnosis of asthma in children aged less than 5 years can present a particular challenge. The problems are that young children (1) are more likely to have respiratory tract infections that cause cough, shortness of breath, and respiratory noises that might be reported as 'wheeze' (2) make genuine wheezing sounds with lower respiratory tract infections; and (3) are more likely to have alternative causes of recurrent wheeze, including acute episodic (viral) wheeze, undiagnosed cystic fibrosis, ciliary dyskinesia, and congenital airway abnormalities, when compared with older children. There have been 3 phases in the evolution of the diagnosis of asthma in young children. Initially, there was the 'denial' phase, in which there was debate as to the lower age limit at which an asthma diagnosis could be made, with the consensus being somewhere between 7 and 10 years of age. In an entirely non Darwinian process, the second phase occurred in the early 1980s, in which, after a 'meteorite' of an article that described improved asthma symptoms in young children treated with inhaled corticosteroids, asthma became a diagnosis of exclusion. The liberation of diagnostic criteria from 'none' to 'all' perhaps not unsurprisingly coincided with a worldwide increase in asthma prevalence and brought us to the present third (and hopefully final) phase, in which asthma is a diagnosis that has to be made.
