Update in Infectious Diseases
This year's Update in Infectious Diseases focuses on the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) hepatitis B, respiratory viruses, antibiotic resistance, and vaccination. The past year held both alarming findings about emerging infectious diseases and drug resistant bacteria as well as exciting findings about new vaccines and more effective therapy for infectious diseases. Respiratory Disease SARS Tsang KW, Ho PL, Ooi GC, et al. A cluster of cases of severe acute respiratory syndrome in Hong Kong. N Engl J Med. 2003;348:1977 85. [PMID: 12671062] As this Update was being prepared for Annual Session in April 2003, cases of SARS were emerging in several sites worldwide; all were eventually linked to one person who had traveled from Guangdong Province in China to Hong Kong in early February 2003. Physicians were just learning how to diagnose and manage the infection. The following symptoms suggest the diagnosis of SARS: fever, chills, severe malaise and myalgia, headache, a dry and persistent cough, shortness of breath, and hypoxemia. Initially, about half of the case patients were suspected of having pneumonia, which was severe enough in 20% to require intensive care and intubation. In retrospect, it was determined that most proven SARS case patients (as determined by a significant increase in serum antibody) also had clinical and radiographic evidence of pneumonia. Patients with proven cases of SARS nearly always had lymphopenia, liver enzymes were elevated (with serum levels usually less than twice the upper limit of normal) in about 66% of cases, and creatine kinase levels were elevated in about 50% of cases. Direct fluorescent antibody testing for influenza and respiratory syncytial virus in suspected cases of SARS can help rule out such infections. A critical aspect of the patients' history was recent travel to a high risk area or contact with a SARS patient. A person who presents with symptoms of SARS and who may have had contact with the virus should be admitted and placed in respiratory isolation. The extent to which the virus is contagious is still unclear, but 95% of SARS cases in Taiwan occurred among health care workers. Health care workers caring for patients suspected of having SARS must use stringent infection control measures against aerosol spread, including N95 respiratory masks, gowns, goggles, and gloves. They must also take special care during such procedures as intubation and bronchoscopy. The etiologic agent, a coronavirus, was rapidly identified by a team headed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in April 2003, and diagnostic tests were developed soon thereafter. No specific treatment is known but has primarily comprised effective intensive and respiratory care. Antibiotics are ineffective. Many of the patients in Hong Kong received ribavirin and steroids, but it is too early to determine whether these agents are effective. Two case series of patients with SARS in Canada and Hong Kong helped provide the basis for its diagnosis and prognosis (1, 2) The epidemic was finally controlled in July 2003 after nearly 8000 cases had been identified and approximately 800 deaths had occurred worldwide. Two suspected cases, however, appeared in late 2003 in Southeast Asia (one in a laboratory worker studying the virus) which suggests that the SARS virus is likely to reappear in the human population. Hepatitis B Hepatitis B is an important disease globally. Although immunization strategies are working well according to recent reports from Taiwan and Hong Kong, the disease infects 350 million individuals worldwide. Hepatitis Brelated liver cancer and liver failure due to cirrhosis reduce life expectancy by 15 to 20 years. Interferon alone is effective only in one third of infected individuals. Lamivudine initially suppresses the virus in most cases, but resistance develops within a year in about one third of patients. The long term effectiveness of these drugs is unknown. Hepatitis B e Antigen Increased Hepatocellular Carcinoma Risk Yang HI, Lu SN, Liaw YF, et al. Hepatitis B e antigen and the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma. N Engl J Med. 2002;347:168 74. [PMID: 12124405] The investigators determined the relationship between positive results on tests for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) and hepatocellular carcinoma. A total of 11893 Taiwanese men (30 to 65 years of age) without evidence of hepatocellular carcinoma were tested for HBsAg and HBeAg by radioimmunoassay and tracked for evidence of hepatocellular carcinoma. The investigators performed a multiple regression analysis to determine the relative risk for hepatocellular carcinoma according to HBsAg and HBeAg status at enrollment. During 92359 person years of follow up, hepatocellular carcinoma occurred in 111 persons. The incidence rate of hepatocellular carcinoma per 100000 person years was 1169 cases among men who were positive for both HBsAg and HBeAg, 324 cases in those who were positive for HBsAg only, and 39 cases in those who were negative for both. After adjustment for baseline differences in age, sex, antibodies against hepatitis C virus, cigarette smoking, and use of alcohol, compared with men who were negative for both markers, the relative risk for hepatocellular carcinoma was 9.6 (95% CI, 6.0 to 15.2) for men who were only HBsAg positive and 60.2 (CI, 35.5 to 102.1) for men who were positive for both HBsAg and HBeAg. In Europe and the Middle East, at least two thirds of hepatitis B patients are positive only for HBsAg and not HBeAg. However, they still face substantial risk for cirrhosis and liver cancer. HBeAg Positive Patients Benefited from Adefovir Dipivoxil Treatment Marcellin P, Chang TT, Lim SG, et al. Adefovir dipivoxil for the treatment of hepatitis B e antigen positive chronic hepatitis B. N Engl J Med. 2003;348:808 16. [PMID: 12606735] Early studies showed that adefovir dipivoxil was effective against hepatitis B virus (HBV) including lamivudine resistant strains. The investigators randomly assigned 515 patients with chronic HBV infection who were positive for HBeAg to receive 10 mg of adefovir dipivoxil (n 172) 30 mg of adefovir dipivoxil (n 173) or placebo (n 170) daily for 48 weeks. Treatment was effective by several measures. At the end of the treatment period, more patients who received 10 mg or 30 mg of adefovir dipivoxil had histologic improvement (53% [P 0.001] and 59% [P 0.001] respectively) compared with the placebo group (25% Serum HBV DNA levels decreased by a median of 3.52 (P 0.001) 4.76 (P 0.001) and 0.55 copies/mL in the 2 treatment groups and the control group, respectively, and the percentages of patients with undetectable levels of serum HBV DNA were 21% (P 0.001) 39% (P 0.001) and 0% respectively. The alanine aminotransferase levels became normal in 48% (P 0.001) 55% (P 0.001) and 16% of patients, respectively, and 12% (P 0.049) 14% (P 0.01) and 6% experienced HBeAg seroconversion. The authors checked for adefovir associated resistance mutations in the HBV DNA polymerase gene and found none. Adverse effects were similar between the 10 mg adefovir dipivoxil group and the placebo group. Adverse effects and renal laboratory abnormalities were more common in the 30 mg adefovir dipivoxil group. In summary, a 10 mg daily dose of adefovir dipivoxil is effective for patients with HBV infection who are positive for HBeAg. HBeAg Negative Patients Benefited from Adefovir Dipivoxil Treatment Hadziyannis SJ, Tassopoulos NC, Heathcote EJ, et al. Adefovir dipivoxil for the treatment of hepatitis B e antigen negative chronic hepatitis B. N Engl J Med. 2003;348:800 7. [PMID: 12606734] This clinical trial, published at the same time as the preceding study, tested the effectiveness of adefovir dipivoxil against HBV in infected HBeAg negative patients. The investigators randomly assigned 185 patients with chronic HBV infection who were negative for HBeAg to receive either 10 mg of adefovir dipivoxil or placebo once daily for 48 weeks. Among patients who had liver biopsy results at baseline, 64% in the adefovir dipivoxil group had reduced histologic liver abnormalities (77 of 121) compared with 33% in the placebo group (19 of 57) (P 0.001) Serum HBV DNA was undetectable in 51% of patients in the adefovir dipivoxil group (63 of 123) but was detectable in all 61 patients in the placebo group (P 0.001) The median decrease in log transformed HBV DNA levels was greater with adefovir dipivoxil treatment than with placebo (3.91 vs. 1.35 copies/mL [P 0.001] and alanine aminotransferase levels had normalized in 72% of adefovir dipivoxil patients (84 of 116) compared with 29% of placebo patients (17 of 59) (P 0.001) The authors looked for HBV polymerase mutations associated with resistance to adefovir and found none. As in the previous study, the safety profile of the 10 mg daily dose of adefovir dipivoxil was similar to that of placebo. In summary, a daily regimen of adefovir dipivoxil, 10 mg, seems to benefit patients with HBV infection who are negative for HBeAg. The 48 week period of follow up is too short to know how often adefovir dipivoxil alters the natural history of the disease. One current treatment strategy for HBV infection is to use lamivudine until resistance develops and then switch to adefovir dipivoxil. Respiratory Viruses Streptococcus pneumoniae bacteria continue to develop resistance to antibacterial medications. Patients with community acquired pneumonia may be infected with pneumococci resistant to penicillin, cefotaxime, and macrolides, and resistance to quinolones has been increasing worldwide. Resistance to lactams, macrolides, trimethoprimsulfamethoxazole, and quinolones has been found in some pneumococcal isolates.
