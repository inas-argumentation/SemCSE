The authors very aptly describe vernacular literature as 'performative, creative, irreverent and anti elitist' (136) It defies assumptions, exposes common myths that delegitimize vernaculars and makes an invitation to engage in creative transformation and knowledge construction. Applied linguistics and literary studies complement each other to form an integrated argument, one that could not have been achieved through any discipline alone. Merging theory and practice is difficult, but Nero and Ahmad do this well, achieving their goal of investigating 'the productive uses to which vernacular language and literature can be put in secondary and university classrooms' (4) This book provides the beginnings of excellent discussions surrounding vernacular languages and vernacular literature, yet, it also calls for more research that examines the implementation of these pedagogical strategies in different and diverse contexts. Moving away from monolingualist ideologies in an age of standardized testing is not easy, and as the authors note, many challenges remain. In today's plurilingual classrooms, it is imperative that we as teachers recognize students' vernacular languages as resources, yet, these efforts need to advocate further into policy and assessment if we are not to undermine our best intentions to validate difference by always returning to the Standard when the stakes are highest.
