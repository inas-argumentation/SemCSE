Again well chosen, these cover the various main approaches to the sociology of everyday life, and provide a fascinating and inspiring resource. Contributions include Lefebvre, Alfred Schutz, Agnes Heller, Pierre Bourdieu and Rita Felski. It is hard to find even minor quibbles with the selection of readings but, especially given the recent proliferation of 'anticapitalist' protest, it would have been good to see something from the Situationists. To varying extents each author uses their own research, and some usefully elaborate on their research methods. Diane Watson considers the ethnographic route to her findings on the pub, and Peter Redman discusses the role of interviews in exploring the romantic lives of young men. If it occasionally seems quite advanced for a generally introductory audience, the writing is for the most part wonderfully accessible. Most importantly, the undergraduates for whom the book is intended are given a rollicking good introduction to contemporary sociology. But undergraduates should not form this book's only readership. There is much for the general reader with a thirst to explore and understand 'the everyday' Meanwhile more advanced readers, some of whom might routinely use concepts like 'the everyday' and 'everyday life' as useful but vague shorthand, will find here a thorough summary of relevant sociological work.
