Editorial Comment on "Male to Female Genital Reassignment Surgery: A Retrospective Review of Surgical Technique and Complications in 60 Patients"
The topic of transgenderism has gained momentum recently in the media, and it is hard to go one week without coming across a newspaper, magazine, or social media article that does not make reference to the transgender community. Most of this publicity is centered around sensationalized topics, but there has also been a lot of coverage of the emerging healthcare rights of this patient population. Despite this movement, there is still a paucity of data published in the scientific and medical literature on transgender care and outcomes following medical and surgical transgenderspecific services. In this retrospective analysis, the authors describe their surgical technique used for male tofemale genital reassignment. They also review the outcomes of 60 of their cases, performed over a 5 year period with a minimum of 1 year of followup. They describe both their functional and esthetic outcomes. The biggest strengths of this descriptive study are the images and description of their technique. While there are published articles describing surgical techniques, this is one of the better ones, as it is detailed and the authors spend a considerable amount of time discussing the nuances of their operation. This is very helpful as their technique may be tried by other surgeons who currently perform these procedures, and who are looking to modify their technique or to learn about different ways to achieve certain outcomes. The community of surgeons who perform genital reassignment are not always as forthcoming about surgical technique and pearls, and so it is refreshing to see a large case series such as this one that is detailed and very well presented. An important take home message from this analysis is that in general, the outcomes for patients after this procedure are favorable, and patients tend to do well afterwards. The article does provide examples of the adverse events that are associated with this type of reconstructive surgery, such as blood loss and need for blood transfusion, wound dehiscence, urethral stenosis, and the risk of rectovaginal fistula formation. The risk of some of these events is low, while the incidence is higher for others; and, it is important for surgeons to share these types of experiences with other surgeons, as well as their patients. The authors describe a high rate of sexual functioning post surgery in their patient cohort. This rate has been reported in other series as well and is reflective of what we already know about sexual function and satisfaction in these patients. Patients frequently ask about this type of outcome at the time of surgical consultation, and the data continue to point to excellent functional outcomes. I believe that one of the most important reported findings in this article is the revision rate to achieve better functional and esthetic outcomes. Many patients will undergo a secondary smaller and less morbid procedure to either improve cosmesis or to address a small postoperative complication like vaginal narrowing or stenosis, urethral stenosis, or a diverting urinary stream. Many surgical articles fail to address these patient needs, as they do not consider them to be serious adverse events. But they are an important part of patient counseling and should not be overlooked. I commend the authors for sharing their work with the readers of the Journal of Sexual Medicine. It is important data that will be read and used by many. More articles like this are necessary, and I anticipate that we will be seeing more in the future.
