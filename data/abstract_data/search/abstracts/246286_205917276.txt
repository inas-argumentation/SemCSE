A Step Forward in Stroke Care in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka, with a population of 19 million, has shown a significant advance in neurological practices in recent decades. The Post Graduate Institute of Medicine, which is responsible for training postgraduate doctors, has expanded neurology services by introducing 24 neurologists. Although this figure is hardly adequate for the Island, it represents a significant increase from 1980, when there were only two neurologists for the entire population. The Association of Sri Lankan Neurologists (ASN) inaugurated in January 2007, was another land mark for progression of neurology services. The National Stroke Association of Sri Lanka (NSAS) is one of the oldest stroke associations in the south Asian region. Association membership consists of medical personal and high calibre, well respected nonmedical professionals. Since its inauguration in 2001, the organisation has facilitated a vast amount of activities for the benefit of stroke victims and for the primary prevention of stroke. Despite these measures, there is a vast room for improvement in stroke care in the Island. Ninety nine per cent of current stroke management is done in general medical wards, critically not prioritising cerebrovascular accidents for the attention of medical staff for both acute and long term care. A programme for thrombolysis with rtPA is available only at the national hospital in Colombo. In addition to the vast number of patients who need to be handled by physicians in medical wards, the lethargic attitude of the health hierarchy and the lack of availability of programmes for continuous professional development on stroke can be attributed to this serious situation. This was an ideal basis for a conference theme: 'A step forward in stroke care' in Sri Lanka. Within 2 years of inauguration, ASN, in collaboration with NSAS, hosted the first ever International Stroke Conference of the island from 21 22 November 2008, along with the Annual Academic Sessions. The International Stroke Conference, which was endorsed by the World Stroke Organization, was enriched by presentations from 18 highly regarded local and international speakers from the UK, Australia, Canada, US, India, Singapore and Pakistan. Stroke topics including epidemiology; use of thrombolytics in acute stroke; prevention by control of metabolic syndrome; surgical management of cerebral haemorrhage; and rehabilitation were addressed in depth. Dr Mandy Thrift, President of the Stroke Society of Australia, addressed the burden of stroke in Asia, highlighting the value of the conference in this region. The data of the first programme of thrombolysis implemented at the National Hospital of Sri Lanka, presented at the symposium on 'The experience of thrombolysis in acute stroke; experiences from Sydney, Malabar and Colombo' convinced the audience on the feasibility of implementing thrombolytic programmes in other hospitals in Sri Lanka. The need for and extent of detailed investigations for young stroke were highlighted by Prof. Graham Venables (UK) and Prof. Anand Vaishnav (USA) at presentations on 'Investigations and management of stroke in young people' and 'Blood dyscrasia in stroke' The vociferous debate on endarterectomy vs. stenting for carotid stenosis between the vascular surgeon and a cardiologist drew special attention. The debate concluded, convincing the audience that the alternative option of stenting against end arterectomy should not be taken lightly. Prof. Venables, President, Association of British Neurologists, and principal guest addressed the audience at the colourful inauguration on 'Implementing a National Strategy for Stroke' It was a revelation for an audience consisting mostly of specialists and administrators from the Ministry of Health. A step forward in stroke care in Sri Lanka was the presidential address and the theme of the conference, read by Dr Arjuna Fernando, secretary of the association (the Organizations President, Dr Padma S. Gunaratne a key co ordinator of the conference was unavoidably unable to participate) who highlighted flaws in and the urgent need for stroke care in Sri Lanka. The media conference arranged to educate the public and focus more attention on stroke care was well attended, the result being several published articles and television discussions on varying aspects of stroke. A meeting between the chief guest and the Honourable Minister of Health was also arranged. While the aim of the conference was to continue professional management of stroke care for medical practitioners, it was also to promote stroke management to administrators and highlight the need to improve stroke care in Sri Lanka. The ASN and the NSAS are grateful for the contributions of both national and international speakers; their contribution made the conference a tremendous success.
