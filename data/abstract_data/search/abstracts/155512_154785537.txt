Book Review: Courts Under Constraints: Judges, Generals, and Presidents in Argentina
Courts Under Constraints is a welcome addition to the growing scholarship in comparative politics on law and courts; however, it does much more than "fill a gap" in a developing literature. It is a model of how novel, theoretically driven research can illuminate a puzzling political phenomenon. Helmke asks why Argentine high court judges, who enjoy few de facto protections from political interference, have sometimes challenged the authority of the very officials who appointed them. Writing about judges who appear to strategically avoid conflict with politicians is common; writing about judges who appear to strategically invite such conflict is most certainly not. Unsurprisingly, existing theories of judicial behavior offer no explanation for such choices. Indeed, in the standard positive political account, strategic decisions are inherently deferential, designed to minimize confrontation with current members of government. In providing a theoretical explanation of this behavior and a wide array of empirical tests, Helmke does much to advance our understanding of interbranch politics, especially in states where institutions are unstable. Helmke's theoretical innovation is to recognize that because transitions from one ruling coalition to another can place considerable pressure on judges, the incentive to deviate from one's sincere view of the law may begin prior to the transition. This simple yet important observation nicely links the theoretical approach to judicial politics advocated by Epstein and Knight (1998) with the broader comparative focus on regime stability (e.g. Przeworkski, Alvarez, Cheibub, Limongi, 2002) Why would a judge strategically rule against his or her appointer? Helmke suggests that he or she may do so to avoid being punished by a subsequent government. Although this argument is briefly sketched in chapter 2 (pp. 28 29) we find the book's core theoretical contribution in the subsequent chapter, in which Helmke introduces a formal signaling model of judicial government interaction. In chapter 3, we learn that "strategic defection" from a current government arises out of fundamental political uncertainties. Because appointing a judge is not costless, even politicians capable of replacing the entire judiciary may elect not to do so. The choice depends on the government's beliefs about whether a judge's ideology is compatible with its own, something it may be able to learn from past decisions.
