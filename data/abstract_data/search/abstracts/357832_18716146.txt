Managerial Problems from Coevolving Causalities Unraveling Entangled Organizational Dynamics
The traditional, top down control dominated hierarchical organization is supposedly becoming a thing of the past. Nevertheless, many CEOs stay with tradition, even though high velocity environments and knowledge based technologies call for more emphasis on lower level networks and distributed intelligence and responsibilities. As a result, management is often ineffective. This paper proposes a fundamentally different approach featuring the "coevolution of causalities." The formal figure of entangled top down and bottom up causal forces in coevolutionary interaction offers a novel conceptual framework allowing us to rethink how organizations most effectively respond to constraints such as quality, cost, flexibility, and innovation in changing environments. We use a longitudinal study of a large international cosmetics firm to illustrate how our theory plays out during the corporation's formative years, as it pursues the challenge of explicating the relationship between the parent company and its various subunits. We focus on how intertwined causal forces are used to manage the control/autonomy mix at different stages of the firm's development, eventually returning to profitability. One might think that the top down hierarchical organizational form dominating history has become a thing of the past. The bureaucratic form, blamed for centralized hierarchy, inertia, and rigidity, and viewed as a leftover from the industrial standardization era, has been routinely criticized for half a century (Argyris, 1957; Morgan, 1997) Nevertheless, most firms continue to operate within the framework of hierarchical control. It still acts as the intrinsic logic underlying most conceptions of who is in charge of effective implementation of managerial processes (Jones, 2000) and strategy (Besanko, Dranove and Shanley, 2000) Even supposing a firm has a modern cellular network (Miles et al. 1999) and tries to manage according to complexity theory (Stacey, 1992; Marion, 1999) aims for effective organizational learning (Argote, 1999) and puts knowledge management to work (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Prusak, 1996) problems may arise: Who has the authority to make strategic decisions relevant to autonomous, interconnected systems? How to control, orient, or regulate a cellular network of autonomous units? Suppose the network innovates in random directions at shareholder expense without ramping any products up to market. What happens if there is no top down control to set it straight, or control is so strong that the cellular network, lower level autonomy, and innovation stops? What happens if those controlling from the top are so far removed from the forces motivating and steering the cellular networks that they are essentially blind to the relevant technological, market, strategic, and motivational issues, and thus blind as to whether more or less of their "control" is needed? Arguably, hierarchical control is still the most problematic aspect of organizational design there is a substantial downside risk whether there is too much or too little of it; given this, Halal (1999:57) asks: "What replaces the Hierarchy?" Is there a way to avoid the "too much" or "too little" problem? We say yes, but, instead of viewing organizations as consisting of coevolving structural "ingredients" like control systems, or strategy, design, production, and marketing units, or autonomous units and cellular networks, we treat organizational performance as a consequence of Coevolution of Causalities 2 (1) how the strength of one causal force affects the strength of another causal force; and (2) the rate at which this interaction occurs. We focus on the "coevolution of causalities." Two of the obvious causal forces in organizations are: top down influence (control) and bottom up influence (autonomy) We also take a cue from the "self organization biologists" (Van de Vijver, Salthe and Delpos, 1998) and add Aristotle's four causes to the mix: material, final, formal, and efficient. He used them to explain why one building project differs from another. Other causal dualities are discussed as well. We use some of them to describe changes going on inside a cosmetics firm. We respond to Halal's question by beginning the agenda of figuring out how to think beyond (1) the "ingredients" view of organizations and (2) the traditional normal science focus on a single cause Aristotle's efficient cause. If one views organizations as containers full of multiple coevolving causalities what the French imply by "tangled hierarchy" (hierarchie enchevetree) then: administrative science has to (1) base empirically justified explanation on multiple, coevolving causes, (2) worry about the possibility that managerial action based on one cause may materially change the likelihood that another may become engaged in ways leading to unintended consequences; and (3) expect that causes may willy nilly coevolve so as to create unexpected structural changes having positive or negative outcomes. To begin, we briefly review several causal dualities affecting the structuration and behavior of organizations, following this with an introduction to key French writers on entangled causes. Next we reprise elements of coevolutionary theory, translating it from coevolving entities to the ebb flow of coevolving forces. Our empirical analysis follows, where we tease out coevolving causal ebbs and flows over time, as our example firm goes about trying to implement new strategies and accompanying organizational forms. Operationally, their difficulty resides in the necessity to reveal and then manage the interdependencies among the different coevolving causalities up and down hierarchical levels and across functional units while preserving an appropriate degree of strategic focus and subunit autonomy at the same time.
