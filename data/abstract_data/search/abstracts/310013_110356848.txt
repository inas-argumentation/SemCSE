Semiconductor die manufacturing is the fastest growing sector within the electronics industry, with 17% annual growth rate over the last three decades. The engineers and scientists who work in this sector have a very diverse formal education: electrical, computer, chemical engineering, physics, materials science, etc. Undergraduate curricula in electrical and computer engineering provide an adequate education in system design, IC architecture and layout, whereas chemical engineers, chemists, physicists and material scientists are well positioned to perform and develop specific process steps. At the center of this multidisciplinary effort are "device" "product" "integration" engineering groups with the group designation varying fkom company to company. Device engineers are responsible for the integration of individual process steps into a process flow which would lead to robust, cost effective, high yield manufacturing of IC's with the best possible performance within the limits of the design window. They perform wafer level electrical testing and are responsible for relating the final characteristics of devices to their process history. These tasks demand an in depth understanding of the relations between product specifications, layout and device structure on one hand, and individual process steps and their interactions in an overall process flow on the other. Device engineers play a crucial coordinating role in the semiconductor industry. They are responsible for technology transfers and overall technology development with special emphasis on device scaling, cost reduction and product performance improvement whenever possible. The undergraduate microelectronic engineering program at R.1.T was created in 1982 to provide an education that specifically addresses the multidisciplinary needs of the semiconductor industry [l] In addition to fundamental engineering education, the ABET accredited curriculum includes 5 courses in electronic circuits, 5 in semiconductor and thin film processing, 5 in microlithography, 2 in device physics and 2 in statistical process control and design of experiments. In die manufacturing, the laboratory experience ranges from simple 4 level A1 gate PMOS to 9 level n and p well CMOS design, fabrication and testing. With an intensive formal education in processing, microlithography, electronics, and device physics, the students are particularly well prepared for positions in device and microlithography engineering.
