For him, philosophy is fundamentally in harmony with faith because it is ordered to the reality of the world, which is itself ultimately of God's making. This is not so far from Thomas' conviction, linked with his theory of the Transcendentals, that empirical truth is grounded in divine truth. Here again Eckhart is at his most medieval. But the prioritization of formal emanation, the understanding of creation as a fall, the prioritization of unity over plurality, of inner over outer, of intellect over body all show the tension that this introduced into his work. Despite the far reaching christological formations of his central philosophical ideas, which McGinn shows well, his anthropology and general epistemology are foundationally constructed around a form of platonic hierarchalism. If Karl Rahner is right to define pre Christian and Christian metaphysics in terms of the tendency of the former to view the finite as an obstacle to the knowledge of God and the tendency of the latter to view it as the mediation of God, then there is much of the former, though christologically expressed, to be found in Eckhart's work. Eckhart's "system" worked correctively in his own time however, and still does so today, for his Christian platonism can serve as a valuable antidote to the rampant materialism which characterises our own culture, as it contests the complacencies of the "self positing cogito" which, in Paul Ricoeur's view, is the enduring mythic self of modernity.
