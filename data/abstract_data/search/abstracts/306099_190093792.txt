Hollywood Pre Code
Mark A. Viera, Sin in Soft Focus: Pre Code Hollywood (Harry N. Abrams, Inc. 1999) 240 pp. photos. ISBN 0 81094475 8, $39.95. Thomas Doherty, Pre Code Hollywood: Sex, Immorality, and Insurrection in American Cinema 1930 1934 (New York: Columbia University Press 1999) 430 pp. photos, ISBN 0 231 11095 2, $19.50. In August 1999 New York City's Film Forum 2 opened its fourth pre Code film series. Advertised under the salacious title, "Joy of Pre Code: Sex, Booze, and Red Hot Jazz, 193033," the series featured forty four films, many of which were unavailable on video or DVD. Bruce Goldstein, the director of repertory programming at Film Forum noted that, "The period [1930 331 was completely overlooked. They never showed these films on television when I was growing up. Not one of them is a 'classic' and that's why it was a discovery for most people." Likewise, until recently, the pre Code era has been an overlooked period in film history. Within the past ten years the pre Code period, and specifically the Production Code Administration itself, has received much scholarly attention. Gregory Black's Hollywood Censored: Morality Codes, Catholics, and the Movies and Frank Walsh's Sin and Censorship: The Catholic Church and the Motion Picture Industry are only two of several scholarly works that examined the historical and social context of the Production Code Administration (PCA) and profited from the opening of the PCA files in 1983. Recently two books have been published which view pre Code film as a distinct body of work and use the films themselves as primary documents. Mark A. Viera's Sin in Soft Focus: Pre Code Hollywood is a lushly illustrated book which chronicles the development of the PCA from its beginnings as the Studio Relations Committee (SRC) under the direction of Colonel Jason Joy to the Production Code Administration headed by Joseph Breen. The book is organized chronologically, a chapter devoted to each year of the pre Code period, 1930 34. This periodization is bracketed by the year the production code was first written (1930) and the year when it was strictly enforced (1934) Viera's book primarily focuses on the PCA's machinations regarding particular films. The profuse amount of studio production stills (275 in all) is the major asset of this "coffee table" style book. Viera is in his metier here, an acclaimed portrait photographer and Hollywood historian whose previous credits include Hurrell's Hollywood, his knowledge of photography at a distinct advantage, especially in his selection of stills. Unfortunately, the downside to many "coffee table" books is that the text suffers in comparison to the visual material. Several photographs, for instance, have no textual reference that would provide the reader with further information on their particular relevance. Consequently, many films and topics are omitted from Viera's general discussion of the pre Code period. Thomas Doherty's Pre Code Hollywood: Sex, Immorality, and Insurrection in the American Cinema 1930 1934, as its title implies, takes a more scholarly approach to its subject matter. Using the films themselves as primary documents, Doherty examines the pre Code era "when censorship was lax and Hollywood made the most of it." Doherty claims that these films were, "More unbridled, salacious, subversive, and just plain bizarre than what came afterwards, they look like Hollywood cinema but the moral terrain is so off kilter they seem imported from a parallel universe" (2)
