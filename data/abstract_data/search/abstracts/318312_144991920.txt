Why Mark Erickson should read different histories of science
Mark Erickson's title is deliberately provocative but unintentionally ambiguous. Although his 'I' indicates that this will be a fashionably personalized account, the first paragraph is written in the conventional academic style that implies objectivity and from the beginning of the second, Erickson conceals his own reactions behind the all embracing, ill defined 'we' It remains unclear whether Erickson feels he 'should' read his selected books because he is a university sociologist trying to analyse a scholarly genre, or because as a responsible citizen, he is under some sort of moral obligation to improve his understanding of modern society. Is this an article about opinions held by Erickson (and if so, how would he define himself? or a detached description of the publishing world? Erickson stresses the significance of reception, but it is misleading to ignore production entirely: reasons for reading a book are closely tied up with reasons for writing one. Erickson's theoretical guru is Ludwig Fleck, but he highlighted the effects of two way interactions between audiences and authors. While it is salutary to remember that esoteric experts do not operate in isolation from exoteric consumers of their knowledge, it remains true that they influence readers. Authors of a new book naturally try to persuade their readers of its significance, and although Erickson quotes several examples of self promoting rhetoric that he finds unconvincing, the multiple purchasers of these bestsellers would presumably disagree. As he points out, many histories of science have been written by practising scientists, and these are often designed to stimulate interest in modern achievements rather than explore what happened in the past.
