Similar comparisons between Facebook's attempts to impose Free Basics Internet service in India (Chap. 4) and the Philippines (Chap. 7) as well as between Facebook's use in the 2016 U.S. presidential election (Chap. 6) and its work with the Bharatiya Janata Party in India and President Rodrigo Duterte in the Philippines (Chap. 7) serve to emphasize Facebook's power and reach, as well as its effects, in ways that more parochial examples would not. After all, as the author writes, "Facebook has universalizing tendencies and embodies a globalist ambition. But it does not work the same way in PhnomPenh as it does in Philadelphia" (p. 27) This attentiveness to the complexities of studying a global digital platform that is inherently tied to, and reflective of, local social norms and customsmakes this book stand out as an exemplar of how to study digital technologies. Although Antisocial Media provides readers with an exceptional discussion of the historical context and structural details of Facebook's operations, it falters, however, in its political analysis specifically regarding Facebook's relationship to democracy. Positing a distinction between "motivation" and "deliberation" (though arguing that both are necessary for democracy) Vaidhyanathan argues that the reasons why Facebook is good at the former make it "terrible" for the latter. Yet the model of deliberation that he seems to use to ground his analysis often appears as a somewhat nostalgic picture of a single, cohesive public, with a "common set of facts" (p. 14) and "sense of collective national fate" (p. 199) It is one in which citizens "subscribe to rules of intellectual engagement" (p. 14) that demand "full and calm consideration," and where there are "responsible and reputable news sources" (p. 8) that "we depend on to foster democratic deliberation and accountability" (p. 178) Certainly, this is a not uncommon model of deliberative democracy a national public sphere, governed by the norms of rational speech, and led by experts. But it is one that has been repeatedly challenged by scholars within the literature on deliberative democracy (e.g. Iris Young, "Activist Challenges to Deliberative Democracy," Political Theory, 29(5) 2001; Nancy Fraser, Rethinking the Public Sphere, 1990) Democracy, as it is practiced, is often much messier than it appears in Vaidhyanathan's telling. This is not to say that the author's main claim that "Facebook undermines our ability to think collectively about our problems" (p. 10) is wrong. But it might complicate his argument.Who, for example, does he mean when he talks about "our problems" and how, exactly, does he expect us to "think collectively" about them? These are questions that go unanswered in the book but they are also questions raised by the widespread use of Facebook. The company has certainly disrupted the practices of mainstream journalistic outlets like the New York Times, for example. But in so doing, it has also created spaces for previously marginalized or silenced voices and narratives to gain more widespread attention. If we take this messier reality of democratic politics as our starting point, in other words, maybe Facebook has not created "alternative cultural and political universes for us" (p. 89) as Vaidhyanathan claims, but has instead revealed divisions and disagreements that have always existed but have not been widely acknowledged until now. Like all technologies, Facebook resists simple classification; in exploring its effects on democratic politics, then, the author might have discussed these tensions in more detail. Of course, as Vaidhyanathan rightly argues, Facebook's advertising model and its unprecedented data collection methods both of which are unregulated by governments and opaque to users are cause for serious concern. But his call to reinvest in traditional "civil society organizations" and to "put Facebook in its proper place, perhaps merely as a source of social and familial contact" (p. 17) again invokes a sanitized vision of democratic politics one that separates "proper" political activity from the messier realities of our daily lived experiences. And upholding this separation causes us to overlook potentially productive alternatives. Facebook, as Vaidhyanathan notes, "fulfills needs and desires" (p. 17) And these needs and desires, given the site's popularity, are clearly quite powerful. As such, Facebook's global dominance invites scholars not just to consider how to contain it but also to think about how we might harness those needs and desires for more democratic ends andmake Facebook work for democracy.
