Albert Stunkard (born 1922)
Albert Stunkard, generally known as 'Mickey' by his friends, is one of the pioneers in understanding the psychology and the psychiatry associated with obesity and related conditions. Stunkard made several famous observations, decades before anyone took notice, and already in the 1950s he discussed conditions such as night eating, binge eating syndrome, socio economic status and obesity, etc. Albert Stunkard graduated from Yale University in 1943 and has spent most of his research time later in life at the Pennsylvania School of Medicine. Retirement is no common American term among top scientists at high age Stunkard came back to chair the department of psychiatry in Philadelphia. He has an impressive CV serving as the president of numerous American associations in the area of psychology, psychiatry and nutrition. He has received many high ranking prizes for his work, including the Willendorf award 1998 from the International Association for the Study of Obesity. Over the years, Stunkard has engaged in many of the modern studies on the genetics, psychological, therapeutic and development aspects of human obesity and eating disorders. Stunkard was one of the authors of the famous adoption studies together with Danish colleagues, which early demonstrated the importance of genes versus environment, using a Danish database. In 1985, he published the Three Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ) which is a standard tool for understanding eating aspects such as eating restraint, disinhibition and hunger. Restraint meant trying to resist from eating by conscious determination and control in order to achieve body weight control. Disinhibition measured the degree to which control over appetite and eating was lost, and the hunger scale picked up hunger feelings and craving for food. The TFEQ scale has been used worldwide to categorise patients with eating problems and also to measure development over time as treatment has changed this eating behaviour. In the 1960s, Stunkard was also one of the first scientists to demonstrate the strong relationship between socioeconomics and obesity, which lately developed into a scientific field of its own. Stunkard early described binge eating and he also was among the first to develop appropriate treatment strategies. The night eating syndrome (NES) did not receive much attention for decades, but has suddenly come back as an important issue for many, although not all patients, with weight problems. There still is a debate whether NES constitutes an entity of its own or whether it is a variant of the binge eating syndrome. NES may be seen as a way in which people with an underlying mood disorder try to medicate themselves with food to relieve themselves of problems such as stress, anxiety or depression. Many individuals with NES would easily skip breakfast, only have a light lunch but consume more than 50% of their total calories during the evening hours. It is still not clear whether NES is different from what has been called nocturnal eating, in which the subjects first fall asleep and then wake up with an irresistible hunger, often unconsciously emptying the refrigerator during night time and not being aware when they wake up in the morning about their nightly behaviour.
