Safety and efficacy of IV cladribine versus alemtuzumab in aggressive multiple sclerosis: a retrospective cohort study (P1.368)
Objective: To review safety and efficacy of IV cladribine and alemtuzumab in patients with MS. Background: Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune disease that can lead to significant neurologic disability. If inflammatory attacks continue despite first line agents, more powerful immunosuppression is considered. Two agents, cladribine and alemtuzumab, have been used in escalation treatment of relapsing remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) but have not been compared for safety or efficacy. Design/Methods: Consecutive patients treated at The Ottawa Hospital from 2006 onwards with either alemtuzumab or parenteral cladribine with at least 6 months of follow up were analyzed retrospectively. Results: There were 52 patients treated with cladribine and 45 with alemtuzumab, followed for average of 2.0 years and 1.5 years, respectively (p=0.38) Both groups had RRMS patients but 52% cladribine group were considered transitioning to secondary progressive (SP) disease. On average, the cladribine group had higher baseline expanded disability status scale (EDSS) (4.75vs.3, p=0.0001) and was older (44vs.36.5 years, p=0.0006) with longer disease duration (13vs.6 years, p=0.0002) At the end of follow up, the cladribine group had higher mean EDSS (0.25vs.0.0, p=0.009) and proportion of patients who progressed (50%vs.22% p=0.006) However, when comparing only RRMS patients from both groups, baseline characteristics and these outcomes were not statistically different. The proportion of RRMS patients with no evidence of disease activity (NEDA) at end of follow up was higher in the alemtuzumab group (69%vs.44% p=0.05) as was the proportion of patients with adverse events (89%vs.29% p Conclusions: The results of this retrospective study indicate that both cladribine and alemtuzumab reduce MS attacks. Alemtuzumab seems effective at achieving high rates of NEDA compared with cladribine, though difference in baseline patient risk and length of follow up may be confounders. Standardized prospective trials with similar patient groups are needed for more reliable comparisons between these therapeutic options. Disclosure: Dr. Bose has nothing to disclose.
