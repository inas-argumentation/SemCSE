Frequently, both planning and movement related 51 activity are exhibited in the very same neurons (Tanji and Evarts 1976; Weinrich and 52 Wise 1982) We therefore ask a basic question: why does PMd plan activity not drive 53 movement? 54 When a monkey is cued about the path of an upcoming reach but required to 55 withhold it until a go cue, PMd activity exhibits tuning for parameters of the reach during 56 the plan period (Churchland et al. 2006b; Godschalk et al. 1985; Hocherman and Wise 57 1991; Messier and Kalaska 2000; Riehle and Requin 1989) PMd activity also predicts 58 reaction time (RT; Churchland et al. 2006c; Riehle and Requin 1993) and variability in 59 the upcoming movement (Churchland et al. 2006a) Further arguing that PMd is 60 specifically involved in movement planning, disruption of PMd activity near the time of 61 the go cue delays movement onset (Churchland and Shenoy 2007) Additionally, it is 62 known that PMd sends projections down the spinal cord to motor interneurons (Dum and 63 Strick 1991) and microstimulation in PMd causes arm movements (e.g. Weinrich and 64 Wise 1982) Hence, movement epoch PMd activity presumably contributes to actually 65 driving the movement. Theoretical treatments thus often assume a 'gate' between plan 66 and movement related neurons (e.g. Bullock and Grossberg 1988; Cisek 2006a) and 67 pharmacological experiments have suggested that reduction of inhibition might impair 68 the ability to withhold premature movements (Sawaguchi et al. 1996) 69 Characterizing interneurons in monkey PMd 4 In the oculomotor system, this gating process is fairly well understood. During 70 saccade preparation, cortical areas such as the frontal eye fields (FEF) generate a 71 representation of the upcoming saccade. A downstream subcortical area (nucleus raphe 72 interpositus) contains 'omnipause neurons' (Cohen and Henn 1972) which tonically 73 inhibit saccade generating 'burst neurons' in the brainstem (paramedian pontine reticular 74 formation and rostral interstitial nucleus of the median longitudinal fasciculus; for 75 review, Fuchs et al. 1985) Omnipause neurons stop firing during the saccade, releasing 76 their inhibitory gate of burst neuron output (Evinger et al. 1982) A hypothetical mapping 77 of this mechanism to PMd is shown schematically in Fig. 1. In this mapping, 78 corticospinal neurons might correspond to burst neurons, local interneurons to omnipause 79 neurons, and local pyramidal neurons to upstream areas such as FEF. 80 While PMd sends axons down the spinal cord, analogously to burst neurons, it is a 81 cortical area, like FEF. There is also a small amount of preparatory activity even in the 82 spinal cord (Prut and Fetz 1999) Thus, it is not clear whether PMd should be more 83 closely analogous to FEF or brainstem saccade areas, or use some other mechanism 84 entirely to prevent premature movements. It is known that PMd can exert inhibitory as 85 well as excitatory effects on primary motor cortex (M1; Ghosh and Porter 1988; Keller 86 and Asanuma 1993; Tokuno and Nambu 2000) which might point towards a 87 feedforward inhibition gating mechanism. 88 A recent study examined whether an oculomotor like output gating mechanism 89 might be at play in forelimb movements in rats (Isomura et al. 2009) They found 90 evidence against such a mechanism, but they also note that rats do not have a clear PMd91 M1 separation and found that interneurons were only weakly tuned, in contrast to known 92 Characterizing interneurons in monkey PMd 5 interneuron tuning in monkey M1 (Merchant et al. 2008) Thus, it remains unclear what 93 mechanism is at work in preventing PMd plan activity from driving movement in the 94 monkey. 95 Different hypotheses about movement gating make different predictions regarding 96 the relative activity patterns of interneurons and pyramidal neurons. Examining 97 differences in their patterns of activity could thus be informative regarding the gating 98 mechanism. We therefore wished to identify interneurons and pyramidal neurons in our 99 recordings. Since interneurons have briefer action potentials than pyramidal neurons 100 (Connors and Gutnick 1990; McCormick et al. 1985) and the extracellular waveform 101 reflects the intracellular waveform (Henze et al. 2000) the extracellularly recorded 102 waveform duration can be used to distinguish interneurons from pyramidal neurons with 103 substantial reliability (Bartho et al. 2004) This technique has previously been used in 104 primary somatosensory cortex (Simons 1978; Swadlow 2003) prefrontal cortex (Diester 105 and Nieder 2008; Johnston et al. 2009; Rao et al. 1999; Wilson et al. 1994) V4 (Mitchell 106 et al. 2007) FEF (Cohen et al. 2009) and M1 (Merchant et al. 2008) 107 We first replicate prior findings of a bimodal distribution of spike waveform 108 durations for single electrode recordings. We then extend this method for use with 109 chronically implanted electrode arrays. We observed consistent differences in the activity 110 patterns of putative interneurons versus putative pyramidal neurons. Those differences 111 did not follow the predictions of the output gating 'omnipause' hypothesis; interneuron 112 activity was highest, not lowest, around movement onset. Nor were our results consistent 113 with other, more refined variants of the output gating hypothesis.
