Response to Holmes practical considerations for vector microbiome studies
In a comment in this issue, Holmes (2021) uses an earlier study by Kwan et al. (2017) as a case study to suggest improvements on sampling and statistical analysis of hostassociated microbiome analyses. The three main issues that Holmes raises pertain to sample collection, the method of summarizing species richness, and regression analysis. Here, we comment and respond to Holmes' suggestions to provide additional clarity on certain points raised and to discuss the appropriateness of his suggestions in the hope of broadening the conversation of how to best sample and analyse hostassociated bacterial communities in ecological contexts, particularly vectorassociated microbial communities. Since nextgeneration sequencing (NGS) approaches became readily accessible to ecologists, there have been numerous ampliconbased sequencing studies across varied ecological systems and hosts such as soil microbial communities (Fierer et al. 2012; Sun et al. 2013; Vik et al. 2013) wildlife microbiomes (Cheng et al. 2015; Desai et al. 2012; Gomez et al. 2015) and vectorassociated microbiome studies (Carpi et al. 2011; Dong et al. 2009; Ross et al. 2018; Segata et al. 2016; Weiss Aksoy, 2011) Many of these studies focused on the bacterial component of the microbiome by targeting regions of the bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) by NGS. We use the term "microbiome" here to refer to the bacterial component of microbial communities that many amplicon sequencing studies target. Nextgeneration sequencing techniques necessitated the development of protocols specific to microbiome sample handling practices (BundgaardNielsen et al. 2018; Kerckhof et al. 2014; Sergeant et al. 2012) library construction (Klindworth et al. 2013; Sinha et al. 2017) and sequence analysis (Callahan et al. 2016; Caporaso et al. 2010; Lozupone Knight, 2005; McMurdie Holmes, 2013; Wang et al. 2007) Guiding sampling design for microbiome studies in vectors or other assemblages are many decades of research that evaluated methods and best practices for population sampling and specimen or material collection for the purpose of studying ecological communities (Daniels et al. 2000; Falco et al. 1995; Milne, 1943; Springer et al. 2016; TalleklintEisen Lane, 2000; Van Buskirk Ostfeld, 1998) The study that was undertaken in Kwan et al. (2017) sought to combine best practices for ecological sampling and microbiome analysis to address three research objectives: (i) assess the microbiome composition of the three life stages of the tick, Ixodes pacificus, the primary vector of the Lyme disease bacterium (Borrelia burgdorferi) in the western United States, (ii) examine compositional differences in the microbiome of B. burgdorferi positive and negative nymphal I. pacificus, and (iii) determine if there are differences in microbiome richness or composition in field versus laboratoryreared I. pacificus. Based on our research objectives, our sample collection focused on analysis of ticks from the three life stages (larva, nymph, and adult) between B. burgdorferi infected and uninfected ticks, and between fieldand laboratoryreared ticks. Collection of ticks primarily occurred in local sites that were accessible and had known populations of I. pacificus. Holmes suggests that sampling randomly placed plots from the full geographic range of the species would allow for estimating mean bacterial richness for the species and suggests how sampling from different geographic strata should proceed. We agree that Holmes' points are crucial if the goal is to effectively characterize the microbiome of a species across its full range. However, this was not the goal of our study. Instead our sampling design was aimed at minimizing variation from potentially cofounding factors so that we could isolate differences among various classes of individuals within the same experimental group. This difference in goals therefore make Holmes' concerns about our study's approach to geographic sample collection, temporal sampling, and downsampling misplaced.
