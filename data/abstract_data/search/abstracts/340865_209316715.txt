Early life events influence asthma and food allergy, and how epitope binding can predict the outcome of oral immunotherapy
This issue of Pediatric Allergy and Immunology closes another year of publishing, serving research and education in our field. It is a good time to look back at the last months with a summary of the latest and most prominent publications in the three European Academy of Allergy and Immunology (EAACI) journals. The review article in Clinical Translational Allergy provides you with a summary of articles published in our journals related to skin allergy.1 Allergy features a review on articles reporting on respiratory diseases,2 and our journal summarizes the themes of food and drug allergy, as well as anaphylaxis.3 With this common initiative, we hope to give our readership and the members of EAACI a glance at the very vivid scientific life in our field. Wheezing is a major health problem in young children, which may evolve later in asthma. The first study high lighted in this editorial reports from patients recruited through the PASTURE study, with other aspects we have been reporting earlier.4,5 Brick et al investigated first year wheeze reports (by prospective diaries from 2 to 12 months of age and retrospective questionnaires at 12 months of age) and correlated them with determinants and the evolution at 6 years of age in relation to asthma and lung functions.6 Determinants of true positive recall (in 54% of wheezing children) were parental asthma, total number of wheezing episodes, and a short time interval between completion of the one year questionnaire and the last wheeze episode. True positive recall could be cor related with a higher risk of developing asthma and diminished lung functions as compared to false negative recall. Identifying predictors of asthma and allergies in the growing child is key for initiating preventive measures. A recent study has identified wheeze trajectories by phenotypes, after including individual and environmental risk factors.7 Similarly, environmental and personal factors have been studied in children with atopic dermatitis developing asthma.8,9 Early food sensitization is another well known common factor increasing the risk for asthma and allergy development later in life10,11
