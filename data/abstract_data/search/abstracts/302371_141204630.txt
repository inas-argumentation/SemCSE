Prescriptive Teaching A Cognitive Approach to Social and Interpersonal Learning
Changing Moral, Social, and Interpersonal Behavior Diagnostic prescnptlVe teaching refers to instruction based on a comprehensive assessment of a child's academic functioning. It has proved an effective way to help children who demonstrate specific learning disabilities. However, when a child with learning disabilities also exhibits interpersonal and social behaviors deviant enough to disrupt his own life and the lives of the people around him, diagnostic prescriptive teaching tends to be a limited educational intervention. Improved academic functioning, if it occurs, is not necessarily accompanied by growth in moral and social responsibility. Vocational success as well as social survival, however, depends on social, moral, and interpersonal skills as well as on academic, psychomotor, or occupational skills. Therefore, the wisdom of using diagnostic prescriptive teaching with socially deviant children would depend on whether or not its use can improve social as well as academic functioning. One obvious approach to this problem would be to develop instructional programs based on social, moral, and interpersonal as well as academic prescriptions. In fact, it may be argued that these prescriptions should be given priority over academic ones. As more nonschool agencies (e.g. VISTA, Job Corps, and various industries) assume responsibility for providing job information and training, it becomes increasingly important that the school help chldren develop a socially oriented value system into which academic and vocational information can be integrated. o A cognitive approach to effecting behavior changes. Most teachers concerned with changing a child's social behavior tend to utilize indirect means. One fairly common strategy is to structure the learning environment to ensure maximum academic success and minimal interpersonal friction. The goal is to provide a stable, friction free environment in which personal as well as reflected feelings of worth and success can be experienced frequently. The rationale underlying this approach is that the child who feels more at ease in school will adopt some of the values to which he is exposed. It also presumes that he will utilize more socially oriented coping mechanisms in his interpersonal relations. When successful methods of teaching appropriate material are used, children frequently do demonstrate improved social, moral, and interpersonal behavior. This improvement may, in fact, generalize to several out of the classroom situations so long as the student continues to attend class. All of this serves to reinforce teachers in their use of an indirect approach to the problem. Unfortunately, this type of improvement may be misleading.
