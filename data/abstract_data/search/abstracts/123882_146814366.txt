Eating Behavior in Children and Adolescents from Four European Countries: Socio Economic Self Regulatory and Peer Group Influences
Age, gender, socioeconomic status, and self regulation make important contributions to individuals' nutrition related behaviour, suggesting that self regulation strategies with regard to eating behaviours play an influential role in healthy food choices in adulthood (Anderson, Winett, Wojcik, 2007) Children and adolescents can develop intrapersonal strategies, such as self regulation, in order to deal with environment's temptations, but their eating behaviors also depend on interpersonal factors, such as the peer group (Gaspar, Matos, Santos, Albergaria, 2012) The concept of self regulatory competence (SRC) aids in understanding and dealing with situations that defy weight related behaviour performance (De Ridder De Wit, 2006; Tangney, Baumeister, Luzio, Boone, 2004) SRC concerns individual skills related to both self control i.e. being able to inhibit unwanted and/or impulsive responses to food and inactivity temptations) and the use of strategies to regulate and plan one's behaviour in the pursuit of long term goals (in this case, prevention of weight gain) Thus, SRC is, to some extent, influenced by one's individual character but, by definition, it includes skills that are developed during childhood and adolescence which may be promoted by intervention programmes (Dirks, Treat, Weersing, 2007; Matos, et al. 2012; Schunk Zimmerman, 1997) SRC harbors great potential to explain how individuals are able to deal with unavoidable threats to regulate food intake and activity as it emphasizes the skills necessary to deal with challenging environments. Moreover, SRC has been found to be associated with a wide range of desirable behaviours in youngsters such as interpersonal behaviour and educational attainment (Tangney et al. 2004) and this fact has also been shown to have an impact on behaviour (Dirks et al. 2007) Previous research has shown that self regulatory strategies indeed contribute to the successful control of food intake (Adriaanse, Vinkers, De Ridder, Hox, De Wit, 2011; Van den Bos De Ridder, 2006) Social and Economic Determinants of Eating Behaviors Peer group influence has been identified as a key factor related to both healthy and unhealthy eating (Kalavana, Maes, De Gucht, 2010) Social influences on eating behaviour tend to be extremely robust, often overcoming other influences including the individual's main intentions or goals. Frequently, individuals use meal companies as models for food quantity and quality and for direction in proper diet (Herman, Roth, Polivy, 2003) Eating behaviour in children can be influenced by gender and parents and friends. Such influences act in different ways regarding parent's influence and friend's influence. Salvy, Elmo, Nitecki, Kluczynski, and Roemmich (2011) concluded that children consumed less energy from unhealthy snacks when in the presence of their mothers than when in the company of their friends. Conversely, female adolescents consumed less energy from unhealthy snacks and more energy from healthy snacks when they are with their friends than with their mothers. Food selection is differentially influenced by the source of social influence and the age and sex of the child. Furthermore, parents may act as an inhibitory influence on unhealthy eating for younger children (Birch, Davison, 2001, Salvy, et al, 2011).Adolescent girls may try to convey a good impression of healthy eating when eating with same sex friends, but the eating habits of teenage boys are not as influenced by the social context. In a study on body image, Ata, Ludden, and Lally (2007) examined how gender, self esteem, social support, and family, friends, and media pressures relate to attitudes regarding body image and eating behaviors among male and female adolescents. These investigators found that females displayed more high risk eating behaviors which were associated with more psychosocial risk factors than males, whose high risk attitudes and behaviors were only associated with low parental support and greater pressure to be muscular.
