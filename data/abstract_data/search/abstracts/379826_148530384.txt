Sensuous Connections in Sleep: Feelings of Security and Interdependency in Japanese Sleep Rituals
Sleep, in a Japanese context, is often grounded in different relational experiences of some form, extending beyond just the person who sleeps. For Steger (2008, 231) early rising in Japan occurs for a mother as an example of her love for her family and secures a "smooth transition from night to day within the family." For Kaji (2007) sleep knickknacks (nemuri komono) are located in an object or person that is considered the "thing" that helps another person sleep at ease. Most common to literature on sleep in Japan, however, are references to soine (co sleeping) (Tanaka 1984; Ben Ari 1996; Rothbaum et al. 2002; Lebra 2004; Tahhan 2007, 2008) In the past, others have noted that when the Japanese child is of a young age, co sleeping is highly prevalent in the family (Caudill and Plath 1966; Tanaka 1984) it is still common for families to co sleep, particularly with small children. As the child grows older, however, it tends to move into its own room. Although there are various works that explore co sleeping rituals, there is little research conducted on the transition from co sleeping to sleeping in one's own room. 1
