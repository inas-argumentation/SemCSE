The Collapse of the Weimar Republic: German Emigres and the American Cold War
N early 100 years ago, the Revolution of 1918 toppled the German Empire, leaving in its wake Germany's first democratic republic. Fourteen years later, the Weimar Republic came to an end, toppled by elites who invited a racist, populist party to take over the government, a party without any coherent policies to address Weimar's economic crisis. Dictatorship was the solution, since for Hitler the problem was democracy itself. An intense debate arose already during the Republic about what democracy was: how the people's will fit with a system of political representation, the role of elites, the place of law in restraining politicians (or the people) and the role of values in a democracy. These debates continued after 1945, in both the United States and West Germany, and continue to influence discussions of democracy today. Udi Greenberg's new book argues that the Weimar debates on democracy were borne across the ocean to the United States by a brilliant group of emigres. These political exiles came to occupy leading positions in the intellectual and policy worlds of the United States, playing a key role in restructuring parts of Harvard University, for example, and helping to create the model of a Cold War university from the 1950s on. They re exported their ideas to western Germany after the war, contributing to its Cold War democratization. They were "fundamental to the architecture of postwar politics in bothWest Germany and the United States and in the Cold War alliance between them" (5) Greenberg focuses especially on the exiles' antiCommunism, their elitism, their search for values underlying democracy, and their quest to make democracies "militant," able to defend themselves. Greenberg's claim is strong, and implies that we cannot understand the U.S. Cold War without the background of Weimar Germany and the emigres who connected the two. He certainly does not mean to argue that the emigres alone provided the foundations for the ColdWar. One can certainly find indigenous sources for U.S. Cold War principles, ranging from the anti Communism of the Red Scare in the 1920s, the elitism of the Progressives, debates on how to restrain the passions and interests of the masses through representative government going back to the Federalist Papers, and the assertion that religion or values underpinned democracy in different evangelical movements of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. What matters is the way the emigres' ideas flowed into the intellectual and political life of North America, to help justify a new, liberal, elitist, and internationally oriented ideology of the Cold War. The five intellectuals Greenberg surveys indeed had a remarkable effect on ideas and institutions in both countries. Carl J. Friedrich was one of the most influential U.S. political scientists in the twentieth century; he brought his concept of an elitist democracy as well as his model for providing international training of elites over from Heidelberg University, and was instrumental in connecting the insular Harvard world with Washington, D.C. through new institutes for policy and area studies. He re exported these models of education as part of the army of emigres returning with the U.S. occupation. The labor lawyer Ernst Fraenkel brought with him from Berlin an affirmation of a pluralist social democracy, where interest groups supplemented individuals in creating social law.
