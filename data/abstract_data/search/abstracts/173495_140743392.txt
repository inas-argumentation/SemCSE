Head Start Children with a Putative Diagnosis of ADHD: A Four Year Follow Up of Special Education Placement
Considerable evidence exists that children with a diagnosis of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) have a wide range of school problems that persist into adolescence or even adulthood (Biederman, Faraone, Milberger, Curtis, Chen, Mars, Ouellette, Moore Spencer, 1996; Elia, Ambrosini, Rapoport, 1999; MacDonald Achenbach, 1996; Taylor, Chadwick, Hepinstall Danckaerts, 1996) Little research has been done, however, on children with ADHD in their very early school years (Sanson, Smart, Prior Oberklaid, 1992; Stormont Spurgin Zentall, 1995; Zametkin Ernst, 1999) Critical aspects of school functioning have therefore not been widely examined in young children with ADHD including special education needs, academic or social functioning, patterns of critical school events, or subsequent emotional or behavior problems. Although early detection and treatment of ADHD is critical, Weinberg and Weinberg (1990) have argued that school professionals tend often not to refer such children to special education or related services, mistakenly failing to see their disruptive behavior as symptomatic of ADHD. Special education is the only school program in which special assistance is mandated for children with school learning or behavior problems. Although a separate category of special education for children with ADHD has never been created, federal officials have suggested that such children may qualify for special education in the learning disability (LD) emotional disturbance (ED) or other health impaired (OH) categories (Davila, Williams MacDonald, 1991) ADHD was, in fact, recently added to the list of medical conditions that could qualify as a health impairment under the OH category under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (U.S. Department of Education, 2000) Forness and Kavale (2001) have reviewed available research on these eligibility issues and concluded that children with a diagnosis of ADHD comprise approximately 26% of children in LD programs, 43% in ED, and 40% in OH. Controversy also exists over appropriate treatment or intervention for children with ADHD (Armstrong, 1998; Dupaul Eckert, 1997; Forness, Kavale Crenshaw, 1999) There is some evidence that stimulant medication, such as methylphenidate or dextroamphetamine, is not only critical to effective outcome but may also be more effective than psychosocial interventions, such as school or home behavioral approaches (Abikoff Hechtman, 1996; Richters et al. 1995) While stimulant treatment for young children with ADHD of preschool age, such as those who began the present study, is relatively less common (Gadow, 1997) there is some evidence that preschool children with ADHD respond effectively to such treatment (Musten, Firestone, Pisterman, Bennett Mercer, 1997) Although results of a large multisite study on stimulant versus psychosocial treatment of children with ADHD suggests that stimulant treatment is indeed more effective than psychosocial intervention (MTA Cooperative Group, 1999a,b) children in that study were age seven or above at the time treatment was initiated. It is also important to note that combining stimulant treatment with behavioral or psychosocial treatment produced the best results. For this reason, further study seems needed that might suggest if stimulant treatment seems likewise critical in younger children with ADHD and whether stimulant use is related to special education eligibility. More recently, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control (CDC) published a study on childhood prevalence of ADHD as well as on medication use by, and special education placement of, children with ADHD (Pastor Reuben, 2002) They found an overall prevalence rate of 6.8% in a nationally representative sample of 8647 children, age 6 11 years (mean 8.3 years) Children with ADHD and comorbid learning disabilities were 51.5% of the ADHD group. While the CDC study did not break identification rates for special education into specific categories, the overall rate was 11.
