A Distribution Free Theory of Nonparametric Regression. Laszlo Gyorfi, Michael Kohler, Adam Krzyzak, and Harro Walk
The authors of this book endeavored to present a "modern approach" to nonparametric regression for the completely randomized design case. I was interested in this book because all of the ones I already own (e.g. Fan and Gijbels 1996; Eubank 1999; Simonoff 1996) have different approaches and cover different material. I would say that the book's main strength is the thoroughness of the proofs of consistency and convergence rate results for the estimators selected for discussion. Those who would bene t most from this book are graduate students with a graduate level background in probability; I do not think that this book is appropriate for undergraduates. The book comprises 27 relatively short chapters. The rst six chapters (97 pages) do not require a high level of probability experience but have a theoretical component, for example, a complete (with all of the steps) proof of Stone's theorem, as well as some rates of convergence proofs. The kernel nonparametric estimator receiving the most treatment is the Nadaraya Watson (local polynomial with degree 0) the local polynomial (degree 1) receives very little (1 1/2 pages) treatment. The authors suggest that the reason for this is that the local polynomial "has no global consistency properties," but I would have preferred that at least the leading terms of the conditional bias and variance had been stated. Chapter 6 is devoted to the k nearest neighbor type of estimator, with proofs of some consistency results. Chapters 7 and 8 provide a limited discussion of smoothing parameter selection, with Chapter 7 emphasizing a result for a selection method when Y is bounded and Chapter 8 presenting some proofs of results involving nearest neighbor methods. Chapters 9 13 contain thorough proofs involving Vapnik Chervonenkis classes, for example, as well as consistency and convergence rate results for least squares type estimates. Chapters 14 and 15 consider spline type estimators; Chapter 18, wavelet estimators. The rest of the chapters contain results for various topics from neural networks, empirical process theory, recursive estimates, and censored observations. This book is excellent as a reference, because the proofs are written in an extremely clear manner and the topics selected are discussed very clearly and are interesting. I encountered no typographical errors, and it seems that the authors have made an extensive effort to eliminate these. The authors have made an extraordinary effort to provide suf cient details along with an appendix with necessary preliminaries so that with the appropriate background, one can follow the proofs. For example, the appendix contains a complete proof of Berstein's inequality and Hoeffding's inequality with line by line steps no "it's easy to show" or "it's trivial that," with the rest left to the reader. I also appreciate the explicit drawings, which are very helpful in illustrating some concepts, specifically in the neural networks chapter. The book's form is theorem and proof, with some helpful illustrations (drawings) of the concepts. It is clear that this book should not be used as an undergraduate text. The question of whether to use it as a graduate text is more complicated, because the answer depends on the goal of the graduate course. Because of the book's demanding mathematical level, convening a class of suf cient size for a graduate class with students who can understand the book might be dif cult. Moreover, if an applications component is desired for the class, then an additional textbook would be required, because this book has no computer applications sections. Having said this, I am excited to know that I can tell my doctoral students to read parts of this book, knowing that they can follow all the steps and can easily do an independent study program with this book and require little of me. This book was not meant to be a review of what is known regarding the nonparametric estimators, or to be a review of the applications aspects of these estimators. The chapters are short and by no means exhaustive on the particular topics. The results for most of the chapters and the problems at the end of each chapter are theoretical. Hints, but no solutions, are provided for some of the problems. Illustrations, such as graphs, are used as examples, but the format is basically theorem and proof. Extensive references for speci c results are given at the end of sections. For me, the book is worthwhile because the material is interesting, and it would take a long time to collect and write out all the details myself. How nice that this book has done that already. In conclusion, this book is not like others I have read, nor should it be. I would use it as a reference and parts of it in graduate classes that I teach. I would recommend it to graduate students who want to know how certain results are obtained. It also would be a good starting place for approaching a new topic such as Chapter 18, orthogonal series estimators. Although the book contains numerous topics and is not exhaustive on any, the reader still has the sense of completeness of the subjects that are broached. The authors have provided an interesting collection of topics and results not replicated by others. The authors claim that their book is intended for a wide audience, and I certainly would like to think so, but would be surprised if many graduate level mathematics, engineering, or computer science students would have the necessary background for the material.
