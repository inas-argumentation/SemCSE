As with the book by Gibbons (1994) the long arm of U.S. EPA regulations also touches, even drives, much of the material presented in this book. The book comprises two parts. Part 1, "Detection and Quanti cation in the Laboratory," starts with Chapter 2, "Conceptual Foundations." Topics include measurement, calibration, sensitivity, accuracy, precision, detection and quanti cation, and within lab versus between lab bias and variability. The authors make distinctions in measurement for the critical level, the detection limit, and the quantitation limit, distinctions that are never made "perfectly clear." Chapter 3, "Statistical Foundations and Review," focuses on hypothesis testing and statistical intervals. Discussion of the former topic is built around and errors and is as complicated a treatment as can be found in any traditional statistics book. At BP, we have worked instead with the p values readily available in software packages, such as Statistica, and an understanding of the importance of adequate sample sizes. This simpli es the decision making process for scientists. That group probably could do without some of the preaching that they will encounter [e.g. "there is no single way to do it," i.e. hypothesis testing (p. 24) or "it is not broadly appreciated outside the statistical community that there are three main types of statistical intervals" (p. 27) The authors also obscure reasonably cogent discussions, which are especially good for intervals, with full page tables of text on hypothesis testing and risk analysis in everyday life and on the comparison of statistical intervals. This material would have been more useful as text (although much of it is simply redundant) Chapter 4 covers using regression for calibration models. It begins with a discussion of ordinary and weighted least squares estimation. More interesting for most statisticians will be the use of iteratively reweighted least squares and the application of models that relate the variance to the level for the predictor. Calibration curves are used for determining detection and quantitation limits in Chapters 7 and 8, but before this come two chapters on determining the same limits using data from "single concentration designs," which use the spiking of blank samples with a xed concentration of the compound of interest. The authors demonstrate and conclude that "singleconcentration decision and detection limits are of little practical value, despite their widespread use" (p. 56) The situation for quantitation limits is similar. There is plenty of well aimed bashing of EPA regulations here. Calibration designs are preferable, and the authors note that "the more modern approach involves a WLS solution" (p. 56) The calibration chapters put examples of calculations in boxes, which work quite well for this purpose. A lot of the original work for the calibration methods was done by one of the two authors. All of the material on limit determination presented thus far is recapitulated next in a chapter on signi cant digits, which argues for a "statistical characterization of measurement" (p. 90) Signi cant digits, never an easy topic, gets further obfuscated by the authors' argument for fractional signi cant digits, although better clarity is achieved by their statement that "at the detection limit, there are at least zero signi cant digits" (p. 98) The material on detection and quantitation in the laboratory concludes with chapters on the important topic of experimental design and the considerably less important topic of interlaboratory studies. The entire rst part of this book has a ne historical perspective, and it is rich in detail. However, this detail and the authors' propensity for writing in lists relegate this part of the book to reference material; it is not much fun to read or study. Part 2 is titled "Detection and Quantitation in the Field." The " eld" encompasses the particular task that is applicable. The 10 chapters include discussions for comparing measurements to a standard; detecting trend; and monitoring for detection, assessment, or corrective action. Other chapters provide some necessary statistical support: handling censored data, testing distributional assumptions, and testing for outliers. In the comparison for a single measurement to a regulatory standard (Chap. 12) the authors argue for con dence intervals for the true concentration. Chapter 13 presents the concepts and estimators for working with results that are below the limit of quantitation, which requires methods for censored data. A comprehensive historical survey is presented. For environmental applications, the authors recommend maximum likelihood estimators and the use of delta distributions, which assign some portion of the density to a single small value. Chapter 14, "Testing Distributional Assumptions," is concerned with normality and concludes that "no single method is suitable for generic use because none of the methods incorporate both multiple groups and censoring" (p. 182) Chapter 15 covers outliers. Chapter 16, "Detecting Trend," considers a common problem in environmental remediation, particularly after sites have been cleaned to appropriate regulatory standards. Sites do not always stay clean. Estimator methods in this chapter are all nonparametric. The ve remaining chapters all focus on detection monitoring. Chapter 17 provides an overview of detection monitoring, focusing on groundwater monitoring and drawing heavily on the work of Gibbons (1994) That book concentrated on controlling sitewide false positive rates with veri cation samples to control the false negative rate. Nonparametric limits are recommended for nondetections exceeding 50% Intrawell comparisons (which I use if possible) are also discussed. There is a lot more bashing of EPA standards. Chapter 18 provides an overview of situations in which detection monitoring is applied to assessments or to the evaluation of corrective actions. Speci c discussion is directed to several different environmental media. There follow chapters for two types of applications: comparison to a speci c regulatory standard and comparison of downgradient data to an upgradient. Assessment involves comparison for differences. Corrective action involves comparison to determine when there are no differences. Both parametric and nonparametric methods are discussed. Three short chapters conclude the book. One of these gives three brief case studies: long term monitoring, soil evaluations, and site wide groundwater evaluation. A one page chapter on software, mentions some specialized software packages. There is a two page nal summary concludes the text. If you are a statistician who is involved with environmental assessment and monitoring, then this is an essential desktop reference book. The same recommendation can be made to chemists or environmental engineers with good background in statistical theory. I do not recommend that anyone actually try to read this book, however. Long laundry lists, for whatever purpose, do not read well.
