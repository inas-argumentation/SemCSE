In a time when women have greater sexual freedoms than ever, when the narrative of ideal romantic love is up for grabs, how do women negotiate the longing for a perfect coupling with a justified fear of sexual exploitation and wariness about the institution of marriage? And how have books and media both reinforced and questioned this paradox? In The Glass Slipper: Women and Love Stories, Susan Ostrov Weisser aims to "recover the basis of the longstanding appeal of romantic narrative as it appears to, and appeals to, modern women, as well as to examine its hidden contradictions and disappointments" (16) Ostrov Weisser sees the modern narrative of romantic love as still deeply mired in Victorian gender values: the meeting of heart (woman) and head (man) and the prevailing idea that "meaning [can] be found in the individual pursuit of self fulfillment in intimate personal life" (3) Yet at the same time, there is widespread cynicism and anxiety today about marriage in 2005, for the first time, married women were in the minority complicating the narrative of romantic love. The result, according to Ostrov Weisser, is a heterosexual popular romance model of a kind of "postfeminist femininity, one that supposedly encompasses both social progress and traditional plots for women" (3) Ostrov Weisser begins by locating the romance narrative in Jane Austin, D.H. Lawrence, and Charlotte Bronte, and then moves into discussions of how this narrative is upheld (and also transformed) in a contemporary context. Of particular interest is the chapter "Romancing the Other," which shows how the concept of sexual complementarity in love is upheld in the trans species couplings between mermaids, beasts, vampires, and others.
