An Adaptive Multiprocessor Architecture For AUV Applications
Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUV's) require dynamic process ing throughput and fault tolerance combined with low power and volume. The required control processor functionality and throughput is dircctly re lated to mission complexity and the degree of vehicle autonomy. Complex sensor processing must be performed and integrated in real time, and deci sions based on uncertain data must bc formulated and executcd. The pro cessing required will be a combination of signal, algorithmic and symbolic processing The Adaptive Topology Processor (ATP) architecture has been devel oped as to address the performance requirements for AUV applications. The prototype ATP has a 32 bit RISC general purpose processor architec ture (20 100 Million Instructions Per Second) coupled with high through put >500 Million Operations Per Second) array signal processor via an extremely high bandwidth (8.2 gigabits/s) multipath network backplane. The ATP is dynamically reconfigurable for fault tolerance and provides for processor management to optimize power utilization. In this paper system requirements, computer architecture analysis, and the resulting ATP prototype as applied to an autonomous underwater ro botic control application will be discussed. The objective of the Adaptive Topology Processor Program is to develop and test hardware and operating system level concepts in support of knowledge based command and control for autonomous vehicles. This research concentrates in the areas of multicomputer hardware, system software, and the ways in which they can support knowledge based application software as well as traditional control programs and specialized signal processing. The primary goal of this program is to develop an multicomputer architcctural environmcnt capable of supporting dynamic topologics of heterogenous processing hardware, memory association and interconnection. The ATP can evaluate currently employed multicomputer architcctures allowing research into application specific or more theoretical topologies. Deyelopmclll of the ATP multicomputer allows for applied research in the areas of asynchrony, the effective use of local control, and the interactions of local controllers to effect system control. Specific goals defined in the ATP program include: 1) Ensure a configurable computing system architecture design which will meet embedded AUV system requirements and include all features and options of advanced and/or specialized instruction set architectures. 2) Provide for high speed interchange of data and control information between processors (greater than 30 Mbytes/second sustained) with minimal latency (less than 200 microseconds arbitration) and high integrity (bit error rate less than 1 *10**9) on 8 independently switched channels. The data interchange architecture must be capable of handling multi path data communications for sensor data distribution while meeting the instruction/data requirements for general purpose processing. A TP System Architecture The requirements for the ATP multicomputer were derived from eval uation of current approaches to autonomous vehicle control and the limi tations of their computational environments. Typical approaches range from smgle processor solutions with limited capabilities, to heterogenous and homogeneous networked systems, bus based multicomputers, and commercially available systems. Typically, commercially available sys tems are adapted to the vehicle control problem and are interconnected or modified to meet application specific requirements. The robotics commu nity has addressed the limitations of this approach by developing homoge neous systems supported by attached special purpose hardware to handle tasks such as vision' and servo control 2. Ideally, specialized heterogeneous hardware could be integrated directly into a computational topology by standardizing around the communications interface. This approach is then limited only by the throughput available in the system and the need to adapt to a specific system wide protocols. The devclopment of the BPIE addresses architectural and algorithmic issues associated with the design methodologies for distributed systems relying on adaptable and reconfigurable structures. This type of system provides a means to support fault tolerance based on distributed control schemes. To address these issues, the BPIE is partially based on the Multigrid Communications Architecture (MCA) definition by Dr. J. Maitan of Lock heed's Palo Alto Research Laboratory 4. MCA is a theoretical network ar chitecture for multiprocessor systems which features a reconfigurable and adaptable communication structure to derive an optimal solution in the routing and flow control problems for a wide class of interconnects ranging from backplane to local area and wide area networks. The MCA was in troduced to provide a method of improving the efficiency of solving prob lems with functions which can be distributed between processors without the use of a shared or global memory. Tasks communicate with other tasks residing at nonlocal nodes through the use of switches connected to high speed channels providing a reeonfigurable computer architecture. The MCA reconfigurable network can be visualized as a dynamic graph of si multaneous interconnections between communicating processors. MCA allows for simultaneous output links to multiple receivers (broadcast) and allows more than one input to each receiver. 101 The key algorithmic aspect of MCA applied to the BPIE is the use of these multi grid relaxation concepts to evaluate the effectiveness of various network control schemes. The power of relaxation schemes lies in their asynchronous nature and in their ability to be represented in discrete or continuous fonn.
