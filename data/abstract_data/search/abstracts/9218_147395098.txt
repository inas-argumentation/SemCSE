Maturational Changes In Morphology Of The Cortical Auditory Evoked Potential
We are currently investigating the time course of development in the central auditory system (CAS) as evidenced by cortical auditory evoked potentials (CAEPs) The CAEP can be recorded non invasively in children of all ages, and therefore may provide a clinically useful measure of CAS maturation. Additionally, this response may provide information about the underlying neurophysiologic mechanisms involved in auditory processing. Some recent studies examining CAEPs in children have shown that the neural generators of the CAEP continue to develop well into the second decade of life.1 3 Decreases in latency with increasing age are well documented for the P1 and N1 components of the CAEP.1,4,5 Decreases in latency with increasing age correspond with increases in myelination and synaptic efficiency in the CAS.6,7 However, other complex aspects of development in the CAS, such as the maturational time course of neuronal refractoriness and the neural recovery after stimulation, remain unclear. One way to examine such developmental changes is to compare waveform morphologies of the CAEP in a refractory rate, or fast habituation, paradigm. We have recorded CAEPs in normal hearing pre school and school age children in response to a natural speech sound /uh/ The sound was presented in a sequence of four presentations with successively decreasing inter stimulus intervals (ISIs) Our results reveal that specific changes in CAEP morphology occur as a function of age. The pattern of emergence and development of the N1 and P2 components, in particular, depends on the development of the refractory properties of the CAEP. In pre school age children, the N1 and P2 are either not seen or seen only at very slow rates of stimulation. As children grow older, the N1 and P2 components are seen robustly at slow rates of stimulation and, to some degree, at faster rates of stimulation. However, even by age 12 years, the N1 and P2 components are not adult like in their morphology at different stimulation rates. These findings support recent studies showing that the maturation of the CAS continues well into the second decade of life.1 3 Several developmental factors may be involved in the morphologic differences in the CAEP observed across age. These factors may include refinement of synapses through childhood, changes in orientation of the dipole generators of these responses, and changes in laminar axonal density in the neocortex during development.8 10 Further study of CAEP responses may provide better insight into the development of these neurophysiologic mechanisms throughout childhood. As we better understand the maturational changes in the normal CAS, we can use the CAEP to assess central auditory development in children who are at risk for abnormal CAS development, such as those with auditory processing disorders, language learning impairments, and deaf children receiving cochlear implants.
