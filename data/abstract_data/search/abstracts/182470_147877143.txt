We must preach what is practised: A radical review of statistical teaching
Students are often disillusioned with our introductory statistics coursesin the U.K. in the U.S. and elsewhere. They are "turned off by statistics" and "less numerate at the end of the course than at the beginning" This happens whatever their main subject (biology, economics, management, medicine, psychology, sociology, etc. and largely irrespective of whether the course is run by a subject matter specialist or by someone from a statistics department, or of how it is organized. A particular complaint is that "most of the course seems irrelevant to what we're doing" This is often said even with hindsight, years after the event. Not surprisingly, therefore, the students are right. Statistics courses are largely irrelevant not just boring or technically difficult, but irrelevant. My purpose here is to note why this is so. The crux of the matter lies in distinguishing between analysis problems which are exploratory and ones which are repetitive. In exploratory situations we have to make discoveries about new data in previously uncharted territory. This is what is mostly taught. In repetitive situations the specific data are also new but have a structure which is already well known from previous cases. This is what mostly occurs in practice.
