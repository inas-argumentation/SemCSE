The Taylor Creek Forest reserve (Bayelsa State) is one of the most important protected areas of the River Niger Delta region, southern Nigeria. unfortunately, however, no field studies are available on the diversity of the terrestrial vertebrates (amphibians, reptiles, mammals) in this protected territory. Here, we report the results of field surveys devoted to assess on a preliminary base the vertebrate diversity of Taylor Creek Forest reserve. Mammals and amphibians were assessed only qualitatively, whereas reptiles were assessed also quantitatively within 15 plots of 2 ha area. We detected about 70 vertebrate species (some genera may include multiple species that remained non detected in our surveys) Several of the recorded species were of high conservation concern (e.g. manatee, dwarf crocodile, hinge back tortoises, etc. The presence of the endangered Nigerian chimpanzee was suspected for the Forest Reserve, and indeed few groups of this species do occur in the surroundings of the protected area. Analysis of the reptile diversity suggested that, however, species dominance was high and evenness was low, with very few lizard species dominating the samples. Thus, the reptile community diversity profile revealed altered ecological conditions in this forest area. The conservation implications of the observed patterns are also discussed. The forest reserve falls within yenagoa Local Government Area (LGA, Bayelsa State) and Ahoada West LGA (Rivers State) It is bounded by Ogba Egbema Ndoni LGA (Rivers State) in the north, kolokuma Opokuma LGA (Bayelsa State) and Nun River in the west, by the East West Road in the South, and by the Taylor Creek River in the east. The Taylor Creek forest reserve is surrounded by the following communities Biseni, kaiama, kalama, Odi, Zarama, Okordia, Odoni, and Ikarama. Taylor Creek forest reserve is a lowland, generally below 30 m above sea level, and slopes gently in a northwest southeast direction towards the sea. Taylor Creek is the major drainage system draining the forest reserve together with its many freshwater lakes and creeks such as Oluku lake, Esiribi lake, Oruma, Oyulo, Asa, Egbegidi, Azari, Egbe, Opuzuno, Puro, Akpidetoru, Isemu, and Abaniigina. Taylor Creek forest reserve has a hot wet equatorial climate, with mean ambient air temperature and humidity being 25 degC and 80 respectively. Rain falls throughout the year, but 80 of the rainfall is distributed within the months of June to September with little break in August. The mean annual rainfall is greater than 3000 mm. The vegetation is freshwater swamp forest (Fig. 1) Water logging and annual periodic flooding occur during the rainy season. Seasonally flooded swamp forests are characterized by raphia hookeri, rattan palms, mitragyna ciliata (Abura) nauclea diderrichii (Opepe) khaya ivorensis (Mahogany) irvingia gabonensis (African bush mango) eleais guineensis (Oil palm) and musanga cecropoides (umbrella tree) among others. There are also many species of ferns, epiphytes and aquatic plants. Most part of the seasonally flooded forest has thick undergrowth of sedges, grasses and lianas which makes penetration often difficult. The riparian forests occur along the shores of Taylor Creek and at some places in karama, Adibawa, and Biseni. These forests are apparently thick and mature, with a high canopy assuring extensive shadow on the ground even by midday. The bulk of the timber producing trees and other economic trees were recorded in the riparian forests and seasonally flooded freshwater swamps. The derived savanna is found along the pipeline and the cleared area meant for the proposed yar'Adua International Airport at Nyambiri Zarama. The derived savanna habitat is now a grazing ground for cows. The protected territory is also heavily populated. The 1991 population census figure for Zarama, Okordia, and Biseni villages was given as 2,483, 2,266 and 7,205 (Nigeria, 1996) but these populations were expected to increase enormously in the next decades (see Appendix 1 for the projected populations for some communities of the study area, just to give an indication of the growing pressure on the natural environment by human population at the local scale) field methodology: Field surveys were carried out in 15 plots within the forest reserve (Table I) with their geographic coordinates determined by means of a GPS (Garmin CE 12 model) during the years 2011 to 2013, both in dry season and in wet season. Investigations on the diversity of reptiles occurring in the area was carried out following an initial reconnaissance survey, which gave insight into the selection of sampling stations, their sizes and orientation. Each of the surveyed plot measured 2 hectares. Sampling was based on the installation of 5 pitfall traps/plot, coupled with opportunistic visual encounter along footpaths, edge of the plantations, inspection of hunters traps (with permission) as well as dislodging logs, stones, and panels to expose any concealed reptiles. Each of the surveyed plots was sampled for exactly the same number of effort hours (n 24 per plot) distributed across 3 to 6 different field days in each plot. We also visited the local bushmeat bush bars and markets and took photographs of killed vertebrate species, in order to increase sample sizes. taxonomic considerations: Identification of the observed/ recorded individuals was done to the most accurate taxonomic level possible. However, identification of some species remained ambiguous. In this case, either we report the genus name followed by 'sp. (e.g. Genetta sp. or presented the possible options. For instance, for the geospecies Galagoides demidovii (i.e. demidovii group according to Grubb et al. 2003) we indicated it as Galagoides demidovii/thomasi. However, we established an Abundance Index for all of these species, that was empirically determined on the basis of our interviews with selected hunters and/or our field observations. We categorized each species' abundance into three rough groups: (i) very rare; (ii) uncommon; and (iii) common. Although we acknowledge that this abundance index does not have full scientific support, nonetheless it may give a preliminary indication of the relative abundance of the various species at least on the basis of the local knowledge. On the other hand, we analyzed the collected data on reptiles from a statistical point of view. For reptiles, the reliability of the sampling in terms of detection of the present species was estimated by an individual rarefaction curve, using bootstraps in order to calculate 95 confidence intervals of the estimates. This procedure was done using the software ESTIMATES.
