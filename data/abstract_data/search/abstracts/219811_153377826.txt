France and the American Tropics to 1700: Tropics of Discontent? By Philip P. Boucher. Baltimore, MD: The Johns Hopkins University Press, 2008. Pp.xiii, 372. $55.00, cloth; $24.95, paper
In his structure of the trade chapter, Wellington thankfully eschews regression equations in favor of comprehensible prose and argues (in step with the existing historiography) that during the eighteenth century, the trading products shifted from earlier Indian staples such as spices and textiles to China goods (tea and porcelain) and coffee from Moka and the island of Bourbon. The final hundred pages of the book contain appendices, in particular 12 detailed tables of imports and exports for the two major companies, as well as a useful glossary of the surfeit of terms used to describe the textile products in this rich trade. While the information contained in these tables will no doubt hold interest for students of the trade, Wellington makes little effort to analyze the information they contain systematically, or directly relate it to the conclusions in his previous chapters. Overall, therefore, this book contributes relatively little to the historiography of the trade. Its initial section, while engaging, offers nothing new to readers already familiar with the standard secondary works. Similarly, the second section, with the exception of Wellington's regression equations, offers scant information that has not already been published in earlier works on the trade and particularly Haudrere's opus. That said, for those who are not specialists in the field or those who are not able to read French, Wellington's work provides a useful introduction to the travails of the French quest for Asian trade from ca. 1500 1763, and for that alone he is to be commended.
