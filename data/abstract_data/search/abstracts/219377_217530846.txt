Editors' Introduction
'The Chinese Learner' discourse became notable in the teaching and learning literature since the publication of a seminal work, entitled The Chinese Learner: Cultural, Psychological and Contextual Influences (Biggs Watkins, 1996) Biggs and Watkins highlighted two aspects of the so called 'paradox of the Chinese learner' (1) the Chinese learners were taught in conditions not conducive to good learning according to Western standards, such as large classes, expository methods, relentless norm referenced assessment and harsh classroom climate, yet they out perform Western students, at least in science and mathematics, and have deeper, meaning oriented, approaches to learning; and (2) the Chinese learners are perceived as passive rote learners, yet they show high levels of understanding. Five years later, the paradox of the Chinese learner was extended to the Chinese teachers. In a follow up volume entitled Teaching the Chinese Learners: Psychological and Pedagogical Perspectives (Watkins Biggs, 2001) Watkins and Biggs found that the tightly orchestrated teachercentred pedagogy allowed students to be active in even large classes. Moreover, Western teaching innovations of constructivist teaching methods and problem based learning can work well with the Chinese learners if carefully implemented with the Chinese teachers concerned (p. 18) This leads to the conclusion that Western assumption that poor teaching being characterised by large classes, strict, expository teaching and passive learners can be challenged. It leads Watkins and Biggs to further conclude that it is necessary to investigate 'the approaches to (Chinese) teaching that worked' (p. 297) rather than stereotyping them. This special issue is a collection of articles that carry forward the Chinese learner debates to another level of discourse. As much as the notion is debatable and controversial, the authors contributing to this special issue provide a wide spectrum of views. Some carry on the notion of paradoxes of the Chinese learners in further understanding the East West divide in teaching and learning, such as the different approaches to mathematics teaching and learning (Wang Lin, and Callingham) Others extend the investigation of the Chinese learners beyond the cognitive aspects to uncover the emotive and social aspects of learning (such as the social reasons underlying the help seeking behaviour of the Chinese learners that might make outsiders mistakenly regard the Chinese learners as passive learners [Mok et al. and how the deepening of teacher/student relationship enhances learning (Harbon) Yet others move further to demystify the concepts of the Chinese learners, arguing that the Chinese learners, even though they may be different from learners of other cultures, still need to resolve problems common to all learners (Mak) Moreover, Chinese teaching cannot be classified as a stereotype.
