An elderly patient with Alzheimer's disease, normal pressure hydrocephalus and traumatic brain injury: presented with behavioural symptoms similar to behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia?
Dear Sir, It was with great interest that I read the article by Bulut et al. in which they present a patient who demonstrated clinical characteristics similar to frontotemporal dementia (FTD) but was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease (AD) normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) and traumatic brain injury (TBI) [1] I would like to comment on the article to question some of the authors' assumptions and correct certain misleading conclusions. The authors state that the patient's symptoms were similar to behavioural variant FTD. They emphasise the importance of conducting a comprehensive geriatric assessment, including detailed radiological examinations, to avoid misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment. However, I suggest that the process employed to diagnose and evaluate this index patient can be questioned in many aspects. For instance, I consider that the authors should have explained their diagnosis of AD in a more detailed manner, indicating which criteria were met for this patient. They merely mention that the patient had a problem with delayed recall (but did not specify which test was performed and the results) Furthermore, as I understand it, the foremost clinical characteristics were disturbances in executive functions and behavioural problems, which might be attributed to frontal functions. However, they have indicated that an MRI revealed hippocampal atrophy. Hence, as I understand it, they based their diagnosis of AD mainly on neuroimaging findings which do not appear to be sufficiently supported by the clinical signs in this patient. At this point, I would like to mention another subtype of dementia termed 'TBIassociated dementia' which has been acknowledged in recent studies [2, 3] Notably, a steadily growing body of evidence suggests that post TBI neurodegeneration may represent a disease process distinct from AD [2] Moreover, the clinical profile of dementia in elderly patients is known to differ between those with a history of TBI and those without such a history, suggesting that TBI associated dementia may be indistinguishable from some known dementia sub types, including AD [3] Besides, hippocampal atrophy has also been reported to be a frequent neuroimaging finding in TBI associated dementia, which further complicates the differential diagnosis [4] Notably, the authors have suggested in their conclusion on the case that TBI might have accelerated the development of AD due to the presence of neuroinflammation and neuronal loss [1] However, this comment is not supported in the literature, and their conclusions may be regarded as unscientific and considerably ambitious. In fact, the report referenced by Bulut et al. particularly emphasises that postTBI neurodegeneration might represent a disease process that is clinically and pathologically distinct from AD [2] Furthermore, in this referenced report [2] the authors also discuss the problem of misclassifying delayed post TBI cognitive and functional decline as AD, which they attribute to the absence of a more appropriate diagnostic label [2] Moreover, the authors consider an additional diagnosis of NPH, in this patient, focusing on the additional symptoms of gait impairment and urinary incontinence. However, these symptoms are also frequently encountered in many other types of dementia as well as TBI [5, 6] They mention that a cranial MRI showed ventricular dilation. Nevertheless, I do not agree with the authors that the MRI findings (illustrated in the text) suggest NPH as there is no callosal narrowing, disproportionately enlarged lateral ventricles, dilation of the Sylvian fissure out of proportion to sulcal enlargement, or other signs of NPH. Therefore, I could not understand the rationale for further tests for NPH in this patient. As evidence of the patients' s recovery, the authors report that Timed Up and Go (TUG) test had dropped from 17 seconds to 13 seconds. However, an improvement of 5 seconds on the TUG at the tap test (it was 4 seconds in this patient) has been found to be a significant predictive factor for improvement after shunt surgery [7] and the specificity of the tap test is not sufficiently high to confirm a diagnosis of NPH on its own [8] In conclusion, I do not agree with the justification for an additional diagnosis of NPH in this patient. Furthermore, I think that further details about the neurocognitive tests should be included to justify diagnosing this patient with AD and TBI. In addition, I think that, based on a case report, very strong evidence such as neuropathological findings of AD are required in order to reach these ambitious conclusions. In the absence of such evidence, it would be more rational to diagnose this patient with only TBI associated dementia which would be in accordance with the crucial principle of parsimony.
