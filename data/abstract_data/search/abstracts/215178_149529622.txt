On the promotional context of historical video games
The field of historical game studies continues to burgeon year on year. Scholarly work in the area ranges from considerations of the textual representation of history, to videogames' function as a form of historical representation in itself (Chapman 2016a) archeogaming (Reinhard 2017; Mol et al. 2017) using videogames to teach students about history (e.g. McCall 2016) and research into player reception of historical accuracy and heritage in videogames (O'Neill and Feenstra 2016; Copplestone 2016) Individual studies are often carried out of popular franchises such as Assassin's Creed (Hammar 2017; Shaw 2015; Murray 2017) and Sid Meier's Civilization (Chapman 2013) or the excessive amount of videogames concerned with representing the American West (Wills 2008) World Wars (Chapman 2016b; Potzsch and Sisler 2016; Ramsay 2015; Chapman and Linderoth 2015) or other lesser represented conflicts (Sterczewski 2016) Conversely, the history of individual games, the industry as a whole, and technological or hardware advancements is also an area of academic interest (e.g. Kocurek 2015; Arsenault 2017) Yet little sustained work thus far has been dedicated to considering promotional materials and other videogame paratexts or 'ancillary' materials (Barker 2017) as an intrinsic, inseparable aspect of the representation of the past that a particular game offers; and moreover, the historical experience a game may afford players. Though Chapman (2016a) provides the most extensive framework for analysis of historical videogames thus far, designed to be as applicable as possible to a number of videogame types, as Chapman himself acknowledges, [a]dditions' are necessary to study these texts beyond their formal components. That is, academics also need to incorporate lines of inquiry which consider historical content and themes, as well as 'other surrounding aspects of historical games, such as marketing elements like advertising and box art or the hardware on which these games are played' (Chapman 2016a, 269)
