"Just the facts" of schizophrenia in the context of human evolution: Commentary on Keshavan et al. (2011)
Table 1 compares the list compiled by Keshavan et al. (2011) of "the facts" reliably established, that need to be accounted for in any explanation of the phenomena of schizophrenia, with earlier attempts at such a summary. One may wonder whether some conclusions were not effectively reached 100 years ago. Kraepelin (pp 240 241)writes "About the part which external causes may play in the development of dementia praecox there is little to report. infectious causes can scarcely be the decisive factor. head injuries. are in any case so frequent, that they can only quite exceptionally be made use of establishing a cause" Tandon et al. (2009) state "Environmental factors linked to a higher likelihood of developing schizophrenia include cannabis use, prenatal infection or malnutrition, perinatal complications, and a history of winter birth; the exact relevance or nature of these contributions is, however, unclear" As the quality of the evidence has increased, support for pregnancy and birth complications and infections in utero, as etiological agents, has waned. In the 1958 UK National Perinatal Mortality survey neither a composite index of perinatal morbidity (Done et al. 1991) nor individual complications (Sacker et al. 1995) predicted later psychosis. The comprehensive meta analysis of case control studies of Cannon et al. (2002) gave no support to any single complication. In the same UK birth cohort the influenza theory predicted that 28.5 children of mothers who contracted influenza in the second trimester in the 1957 epidemic would develop schizophrenia; in reality two did so, almost exactly the expectation for this population (Crow, 1997c) These data indicate that pre natal exposure to influenza does not cause schizophrenia. Thus Kraepelin's simple view that the phenomena of psychosis are independent of the environment has survived refutation for over a century and has a claim to be added as a core feature to Table 1 (Crow, 1995a: postulate 2. There are no etiologically significant environmental agents)
