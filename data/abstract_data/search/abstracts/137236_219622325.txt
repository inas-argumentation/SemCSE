Book Reviews
Happiness is hot. Movements to make improvement of national well being an explicit aim of public policy are gaining steam in the UK, Switzerland, and elsewhere. Courses on positive psychology are some of the most popular offerings at prominent universities. And a spate of books on happiness has been published over the past few years, achieving a mass readership. None of these books, though, achieves quite what Sonja Lyubomirsky does in her appropriately titled new attempt to feed the reading public's apparently vast appetite for information about how to become happier. The how of happiness: A scientific approach to getting the life you want, authored as it is by Lyubomirsky, a professor of psychology at the University of California, Riverside, and authority on the science of happiness, is definitely grounded in solid empirical foundations. At the same time, it is no mere litany of facts and research findings about the science of well being, but rather a true 'how to' manual for those looking to increase their happiness. Lyubomirsky begins the book by declaring that no matter how happy or unhappy you are now, you can become happier. While this notion might sound perfectly plausible to the lay public, it contradicts an influential perspective in psychology, which holds that, in the words of Lykken and Tellegen (1996) [T]rying to be happier is as futile as trying to be taller This perspective says that people have a pre programmed set point or baseline level of happiness, which is rooted in temperament and other genetically determined factors. Happy or sad events that occur in a person's life lead to temporary deviations from their typical happiness level, but before too long after even the most momentous occurrences (e.g. winning the lottery) people adapt and return to their happiness set point. Lyubomirsky acknowledges that happiness set points and hedonic adaptation exist and do present barriers to sustainable changes in happiness. However, she argues that they are far from insurmountable. Drawing on an analysis advanced in a theoretical paper coauthored with Sheldon and Schkade (2005) Lyubomirsky explains how sustainable increases in happiness are possible. The combined results of published studies indicate that roughly 50% of the variance in happiness levels is caused by inherited and stable factors. Although this is a lot, it still leaves plenty of potential for people to rise above (or fall below) their own genetic set points. The circumstances of people's lives, whether they are rich or poor, married or single, successful or unsuccessful, seem intuitively to be important determinants of happiness. Many people believe that if only they could change circumstance x about their life, then they would be truly happy. Circumstances do matter, Lyubomirsky finds, but on average not greatly. Differences in circumstances seem to be responsible for 10% of the variance in happiness levels. Thus, a change in circumstances could conceivably make one happier, but it would be wise not to overestimate the benefits of a change in any one circumstance. If 50% of happiness is determined by one's genetic set point and 10% by circumstances, then 40% remains unaccounted for. This 40% is the source of Lyubomirsky's optimism about people's potential to become happier. She argues that the missing 40% is largely determined by the activities people engage in. This is fortunate for us because our activities are something we have a great deal of control over. We spend a huge portion of our waking lives engaged in one activity or another. Like circumstances, some activities seem to be more conducive to a state of well being than others. Importantly, whereas circumstances tend to be quickly adapted to, diminishing their hedonic impact, activities operate differently. Many activities we engage in over time require repeated bouts of effort (examples include working out and commuting) If the episodes of activity are fairly homogeneous, adaptation can still occur, but the often discrete, serial, and dynamic nature of pursuit of different activities tends to buffer against the forces of hedonic adaptation. The bulk of The how of happiness consists of descriptions of activities that have shown potential to increase happiness, accompanied by suggestions about how readers can take advantage of them in their own lives.
