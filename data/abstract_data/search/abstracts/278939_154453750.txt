China's Search for Security Andrew J. Nathan and Andrew Scobell. New York: Columbia University Press, 2012. xxiii 406 pp. PS22.95; $32.95. ISBN 978 0 231 14050 8
This book stands out as one of the very best in a crowded field of studies looking to illuminate the motivations, mechanisms and outcomes which shape China's "search for security." From its first line "[v]ulnerability to threats is the main driver of China's foreign policy" the book is a balanced effort to simplify but clarify, aimed not at the highly expert audience, but rather a readership of interested observers, including students, who need to better distill and understand the complexities and contradictions of China. That is not to say that experts will not enjoy this work; they shall and they should. But this balance means that China's Search for Security will rightly reach a wide audience. The authors make clear this volume builds on The Great Wall and the Empty Fortress: China's Search for Security, written by Andrew Nathan and Robert Ross (W.W. Norton, 1997) They also acknowledge that while the world and China's position in it have surely changed since then, the basic conclusion of the earlier work has not: "China is too bogged down in the security challenges in and around its borders to threaten the West unless the West weakens itself to the point of creating a power vacuum" (xi) They also find that the "puzzles of Chinese foreign policy most often yield answers through the insights of a theory called 'realism'" (xv) On balance, they are right. But, as discussed below, they sometimes err by relying on conclusions determined a very long decade and a half ago. Readers will appreciate the clarity of organization, language and consistency. Regular reference throughout thebook to the"four rings" concentric circles aroundChinawhich make up its security environment: the "home" territorywhichChina controls or claims; a second ring comprising some 20 countries immediately adjacent to China (including, in this framework, the United States) a third ring of "six nearby multistate regional systems" and the fourth outer ring of "the rest of the world" proves a useful framework for learning and understanding. The chapters proceed in a well ordered fashion, starting fromwhat and who drives Chinese foreign policy (chapters one and two) and thenmoving across the "four rings" to explain and interpret China's increasingly global security environment (chapters three to seven).More analytically oriented chapters follow on territorial integrity (chapters eight and nine) and the instruments of power (economic strength, military modernization, and "soft power" chapters ten to twelve) Chapter eight, which delves in to the "problems of stateness," and in particular the challenges for China's security picture of Tibet, Xinjiang, Hong Kong and Taiwan, is especially incisive and engaging. Yet, while the book gives some dedicated space in chapter eight and parts of chapter ten to the importance of stability in China's "first ring" its home territory, controlled or claimed this aspect of China's security picture deserves a far more extensive treatment given its fundamental importance to China's perception of "vulnerability" and its powerful role in shaping China's security policy more broadly.
