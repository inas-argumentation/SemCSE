Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder is associated with poorer academic performance
ED FROM Barbaresi WJ, Katusic SK, Colligan RC, et al. Long term school outcomes for children with attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a population based perspective. J Dev Behav Pediatr 2007;28:265 73. C O M M EN TA R Y T his retrospective cohort study investigates the long term educational and academic outcomes of a population based sample of children with ADHD. There have been several previous reports on this Rochester cohort reflecting the incidence of ADHD, long term medication treatment, use of services, and other outcomes. The authors have adopted a careful approach to attempt to find all possible cases of ADHD in a defined local area and have had access to comprehensive school based information. The majority of previous studies examining the longterm academic outcomes of children with ADHD have involved clinically referred samples, with the possibility that other factors which may have led to the referral may have also confounded the relationship with long term outcomes. Although the present study reflects a community identified sample, the majority of children appear to have received a clinical diagnosis of ADHD. This might reflect local recognition practice and service availability and may mean that the findings do not necessarily readily generalise to community based samples of children with ADHD in other areas or countries. These findings add to existing evidence that children in the community with high levels of hyperactivity and inattention have poor educational and academic outcomes even after controlling for IQ and reading ability. The authors found evidence for impaired outcomes in the four domains that they examined (impaired achievement in reading, repetition of an academic year, early dropping out from school, and a slight increase in the number of days absent from school) when compared against a control group who did not have ADHD. In the paper, there was no information about the sample's IQ or learning so it is unclear as to whether these may have partially mediated these adverse academic outcomes. Despite these possible confounders, this paper has a number of strengths and demonstrates the long term risks for children with ADHD. The findings highlight the importance of identifying affected children relatively early in their school careers and the need for the further development and testing of school based educational and behavioural interventions that might help to ameliorate affected children's academic outcomes.
