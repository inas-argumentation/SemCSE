The Archives of Ancient Greek Cities: Preservation and Use of Documents
In order to illustrate the subject, I will briefly present an essential inscription for the study of archives in ancient Greek cities. It is a proposal of regulation from Paros dated at c. 170 150 B.C. concerning the archiving of mnemonika grammata (mnemonika grammata) documents of legal acts between private individuals that were dealt with by mnemones (mnemones) who played a notarial role in Paros (for a comprehensive commentary, cf. LAMBRINUDAKIS W. WORRLE M. "Ein hellenistisches Reformgesetz uber das offentliche Urkundenwesen von Paros" Chiron 13 (1983) p. 283 368) The text contains several words related to archives and other areas of the administrative and civic life, and addresses subjects such as falsification, preservation and access to documents. The ordinance has been issued in response to the discovery of falsified documents among the mnemonika grammata that have been stored in the sanctuary of Apollo, Artemis and Leto the Pythion. A commission of three citizens has thus been elected by the assembly of the people of Paros to suggest new rules for resolving issues concerning both current and future archiving of these documents in the Pythion. The text can be divided in six parts an introduction that states by whom the motion is proposed and its subject (l. 1 7) threats of both sacred and profane nature in form of public curses and prosecutions towards those responsible for records tampering (l. 7 32) the core of the reform, transitional measures regarding current documents (l. 32 52) and a final regulation for future ones (l. 52 65) conditions of access to the documents (l. 65 83) concluding clauses concerning the publication of the motion's text if it is adopted by the people of Paros (l. 84 90) To prevent any further tampering of the mnemonika grammata that had been stored (anaphero/anaphero) in the sanctuary (ieron tou Apollonos kai tes Artemidos kai tes Letous/ieron tou Apollonos kai tes Artemidos kai tes Letous, Puthion/Pythion) and those that would be in the future, tampering such as erasures (exaleipho/exaleipho) or insertions (eggrapho/engrapho) the commission proposed the following regulation. Mnemones were to give monthly all mnemonika grammata to the apodektes in charge of the archives of the Pythion (apodektes o epimelomenos ton tou Puthiou/apodektes o epimelomenos ton tou Pythiou) They were also obliged to record (anagrapho/anagrapho) in chronological order on papyrus rolls (bublion/byblion) copies (antigraphon/antigraphon) of all mnemonika grammata brought to the Pythion and to give (paradidomi/paradidomi) them to the archontes (arkhontes) After having received (paralambano/paralambano) copies from the mnemones, having noted down (apographo/apographo) their reception in the state archives of the city (demosion/demosion) and having certified that they were identical to the documents in the Pythion, the archontes gave them to the apodektes in charge of the archives of the city (apodektes o epimelomenos ton kata polin/apodektes o epimelomenos ton kata polin) who placed (tithemi/tithemi) them immediately, in the presence of the archontes, in a container (emballo eis ten kiboton/emballo eis ten kiboton) inside the sanctuary of Hestia (ieron tes Estias/ieron tes Hestias) These copies were only accessible if someone suspected that a document had not been identically recorded in the sanctuary of Hestia and in the Pythion, and wanted to examine (episkopeo/episkopeo, episkepsis/episkepsis) both texts for verification purposes. However the procedure to do so was strictly and heavily regulated. The request had to be made in advance to the archontes in a regular assembly of the citizens and it had to specify which documents were called into question. The apodektes in charge of the archives of the city would then open (anoignumi/anoignumi) on a precise date, the sanctuary of Hestia and show (epideiknumi/epideiknumi) the copies to the requester in the presence of the archontes. Taking rolls of papyrus outside the sanctuary was strictly forbidden and the apodektes and the archontes were in charge of preventing it. They could be held accountable for any wrongdoing concerning the documents or their inspection, just as could be anyone for any wrongdoing concerning documents stored in the sanctuary of Hestia as well as in the Pythion. In addition to these well protected archives stored in a restricted access container, documents were also available for current use in the Pythion, as the text implies. Recording same documents in two different places was a way for the people of Paros to ensure the integrity and authenticity of these acts, which could be verified by legal copies approved by the city, and to protect them from any intentional or unintentional damage. The fact that copies of private acts were stored in the sanctuary of Hestia, where were also recorded public documents of the state archives, shows that the city wanted to protect documents of private legal acts with the same validity and legitimacy as public ones. Preservation and protection of documents were an important matter in ancient Greek cities because written acts were records of past events (oral agreements of any kind, public as well as private) that could be used in official contexts, in legal proceedings as evidence to support a claim for example. Civilizations have been archiving documents for a very long time. Earliest records of that practice are dated from the Bronze Age (from the third millennium B.C. in the Near East (ex. Ebla, Mari, Ugarit, Hattusa, Nippur, Nuzi) where thousands of baked clay tablets have been discovered in the rubble of the palaces archives and libraries that stored them. In the second millennium B.C. sets of documents of the same nature have been found in the Greek world, in Minoan Crete (ex. Knossos, Malia) and Mycenaean Greece (ex. Pylos, Mycenae) Since then, archiving has developed and evolved a lot, and it now faces new challenges. The arrival of digital technology has changed our relationship with documents it is now possible to store as many data as we want, without apparent physical storage limit, even those that will not be of any use in the future. However, the use of documents is precisely the condition for their archiving, not the result we store documents because we use them, not the other way around. Blindly archiving data without operating any active choices and without transmitting the key to understanding those might result in an accumulation of documents that will not be readable and comprehensible in the future, both technically and intellectually. The archival system in ancient Greece stood far from these issues. In ancient Greek cities, archives were used to preserve documents for both evidentiary and informatory purposes, in daily practical as well as long term retrospective uses. They would thus have contained public and private documents of current use, with temporary lifetime expectancy, and documents with a more permanent value, stored with no time limit. Archives were therefore at the centre of institutional and civic activity and were proof of the increasing importance of writing in a society that had long been dominated by oral transmission of knowledge. Studying the archives of Ancient Greece has been made difficult by the loss of nearly all primary sources, due to the disappearance of the perishable supports on which documents were kept, such as wooden tablets or papyri. The archival system of the Greek cities can thus only be "reconstructed" through the traces that it left in ancient testimonies (literary, legal, epigraphical, papyrological, archaeological or iconographic) and which prove its existence. However, correctly understanding the written sources is particularly tricky, since there is, in these texts, a very large number of technical and polysemous words, which can have loads of different meanings depending on the context, period and place. The goal of my PhD thesis is precisely to highlight the various aspects of archiving in the Greek world by studying the technical words related to them in the ancient written sources. Based on greek and latin literary, epigraphical and papyrological texts from c. 3rd c. B.C. to c. 3rd c. A.D. the study aims to develop a structured, systematic and functional lexicon. This tool will be used to identify the components of the Greek archival system, i.e. supports, types of documents, actors, procedures and places. While also providing easier access to the ancient sources and their study, the resulting work will ensure a better understanding of the features and mechanisms of the Greek cities archives and of their place in the administrative and everyday life of Greek people.
