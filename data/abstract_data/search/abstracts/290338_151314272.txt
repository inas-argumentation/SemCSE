Understanding the school curriculum: Theory, politics and principles
School curriculum development provides grist for constant discussion throughout areas of education. Parents, students, teachers, administrators, and elected officials at all levels seek answers to subjects to be covered, impact of curriculum on learning, and who gets to decide these issues. Moore articulates a view of curriculum that allows readers to look more deeply and competently at such questions and others, as well as providing theoretical and philosophical examples and arguments that engage the reader to search for contextual answers. He questions the way too many curriculum developers continue to accept the status quo and its impact on learning when it comes to curriculum decisions made within a changing world. The book highlights students' current and growing learning needs when creating curriculum. Moore asks educators to think about whether they want knowledge to be prescribed or rather developed throughout a learning process. Ideals of traditional or reproduction curriculum models versus progressive or revolutionary curriculum models are examined. Through this examination, tensions that come from these curriculum models such as conservative and liberal models, and Deleuze and Guattari's schizophrenia and paranoia models are discussed (Deleuze Guattari, 1977) Moore suggests conservative and paranoia models as more controlled and resistant to change and liberal and schizophrenia models as open ended with emphasis on individual interests. Questions such as "What is Curriculum?" and "What is it For?" illuminate these ideas further. For example, is curriculum a fixed product of learned knowledge and skills like in an orchestra or more as a free flowing exploration of knowledge and skills as in a jazz band? A reader is tasked to consider these questions throughout the book by looking at how one defines knowledge. Moore brings to the reader a view of a global education now that technology has allowed access to faster and more up to date information. Students around the world communicate with and inquire about other cultures and lands instantaneously through technology. What once took hours to discover in a library is now immediately within reach. These advances along with an internationalization of education move learning away from a pure national view and more toward a global experience. Are educators to continue traditional methods in which they look out for the best of one's national success or see a global learning community that looks out for the best for all countries? Here, Moore submits that there are challenges to a global curriculum, but argues that a curriculum that challenges rather than constrains opens students' views to all cultures and values producing a more humanitarian world view. Moore continues with a critically important discussion of who decides the curriculum. Is it the government, the administration, the teachers, or the parents? Should all have a voice, and where do students' interests fit? He interrogates all levels of decision making options by looking at what roles educational stakeholders play in curriculum development process. Traditionally the state makes the majority of decisions leaving teachers to decide how it is implemented. From his discussion, it can be concluded that the one positive option may be to have all stakeholders involved by using their various talents and expertise to create a more balanced and practical curriculum that meets the needs of everyone. This seemingly relates to some of Schwab's practical inquiry ideas where schools are the center of practical inquiry, creating a "curriculum group" including principals, community members, teachers, and students to make decisions to best meet the needs of individual settings (Schubert, 1986) Moore also investigates the "Null Curriculum" and how the intentional absence of certain subjects or teachings often acts to ignore student individualities and interests it is not enough to just alter how teachers teach to provide a more complete educational experience. A complete change in curriculum development is needed. This begins by leaving behind traditional top down curriculum models and introducing a curriculum development process that promotes interaction between all stakeholders. Through communication and cooperation, a curriculum that better meets the individual needs of students and their interests can develop and thrive. Moore delivers information and arguments of differing perspectives and beliefs to assist educators, policy makers, and curriculum students (undergraduate and graduate) to form critical stances about curriculum development. The book engages curriculum reform, arguing the status quo no longer serves an educational community. Readers are invited to journey on a path to better understand curriculum from various perspectives, while encouraging educators to strive for a more student centered curriculum developed and supported by all invested parties.
