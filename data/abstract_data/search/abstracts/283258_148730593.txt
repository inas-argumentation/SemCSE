1969 Maria Carmen Guaman Shunaula's angel wings are slightly crooked, but the pleated red and blue bandanas are arranged neatly on the altar behind her along with the candles and the flowers. Sitting on a low wooden bench, the drummer and accordionist play their music while men and women, girls and boys, dance, whirling and stomping on the dirt floor. Jeshuco moves through the crowd with a bottle of contraband aguardiente and a cup, insistently offering a drink, "courtesy of taita Manuel" to anyone he can corner. He fills the small cup and waits until the recipient drinks, grimaces in response to the undiluted power of the raw cane alcohol, flips the remaining drops on the floor, and hands the cup back with the thanks, "God will pay you." All night long there is much laugh ter, drinking, dancing, singing, shouting, and talking. A few people have left the party after having eaten bowls of mutton soup. A few others have passed out, drunk or tired, and are lying covered with ponchos or blankets on the floor. Now the sun has long since risen above the edge of the Andes. Maria Carmen is placed into the tiny coffin on the table. Children and adults gather around, placing flower petals and notes on scraps of paper into the coffin. The coffin is closed.
