New avenues for epilepsy treatment
As the epilepsy community prepares to converge on London, UK, for the 10th European Congress on Epileptology, there has never been a more buoyant time for the fi eld. In the past few years, there has been a fl urry of activity to produce initiatives that assess the needs of and improve the care and support for people with epilepsy. Over the past two decades, many drugs that ameliorate seizure control and associated comorbidities have come to market. However, despite this increase in the pharmacological options available, there are substantial unmet clinical needs: up to 30% of people with epilepsy will fail to have their seizures controlled by current drugs; for many others, adverse eff ects or contraindications will make these drugs an untenable option. Only surgical options remain for such patients. The subject of how to move forward with the development of new treatments is, however, a contentious one. Although common nomenclature categorises the available treatments as antiepileptic, these, in eff ect, are only targeting the symptoms (seizures) of the epilepsy. The opinion of researchers in the fi eld is divided between those who advocate the development of new antiseizure drugs with novel mechanistic targets, to erode the proportion of people for whom treatments are not effi cacious, and those who think that the way forward is to target not the seizures but rather the underlying epileptogenic mechanisms themselves, with antiepileptogenic treatments. Of the potential problems confounding the development of eff ective antiepileptogenic drug regimens, the most challenging lie in the very nature of epileptic disorders. Epilepsy is heterogeneous with respect to causes, phenotypes, and symptoms. To complicate things further, the changes in the brain that precipitate the disorder, whether they have a genetic cause or are due to neurological insult, are dynamic as the disease progresses from the initial incident, through latency, and beyond diagnosis. Eff orts to abort the epileptogenic process with antiseizure therapies have so far met with no success in clinical trials, leading many to conclude that these drugs have little or no eff ect on epileptogenesis or the trial designs were fl awed. When faced with a situation akin to trying to hit a moving and continually changing target, it is little wonder that many will continue to favour tried and trusted symptomatic treatments, rather than expending valuable resources and scarce funds on drugs for which screening protocols are currently untested and unvalidated and the prospects for eventual regulatory approval are far from assured. So, how far have we come towards making antiepileptogenic treatments a realistic goal? Investigations into the molecular mechanisms that lead to epileptogenic changes have revealed potential targets for intervention that have shown promise in preclinical models; candidate biomarkers of epileptogenic development, such as EEG spikes, highfrequency oscillations, microseizures, and structural and metabolic changes seen on imaging, are currently being validated; epidemiological studies are underway, such as FEBSTAT, which aims to explore whether febrile status epilepticus in children causes mesial temporal sclerosis and temporal lobe epilepsy; and pioneering treatments, such as the interleukin converting enzyme/caspase 1 subfamily caspase inhibitor VX 765, which targets localised brain infl ammation, and everolimus, which targets mTOR in patients with tuberous sclerosis, are being trialed. As more candidate antiepileptogenic drugs move closer to clinical trials, study designs will need to be refi ned to take into account the lessons learned from past mistakes and to refl ect novel data on the development of epileptic disorders. Better and more appropriate preclinical models will be needed to accurately represent the diff erences among antiepileptogenic processes, as will homogeneity of patient cohorts (eg, seizure type or initial insult) Much work is also needed to validate biomarkers to assess the effi cacy of treatments and to stage the epileptogenic processes to identify the optimum treatment window. It would seem that the momentum for antiepileptogenic therapies is already in place, but, despite cautious optimism, there is much work still to do. Antiepileptogenic interventions will undoubtedly be a valuable addition to the existing toolkit of treatment options and will go some way to closing the gaps in the eff ectiveness and tolerability of treatment for all people with epilepsy. The current optimism in the epilepsy fi eld should provide the impetus to bring these new treatments to the clinic.
