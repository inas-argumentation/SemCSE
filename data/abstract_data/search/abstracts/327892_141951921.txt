Crafting Flesh, Crafting the Self: Violence and Identity in Early Nineteenth Century German Literature
Lyon, John B. Crafting Flesh, Crafting the Self: Violence and Identity in Early Nineteenth Century German Literature. Lewisburg: Bucknell University Press, 2006. 280 pp. $52.50 hardcover. In Crafting Flesh, Crafting the Self, John B. Lyon explores the role of violence, physical mutilations, and other forms of "wounding" as metaphors that physically ground abstract definitions of the self. His study identifies a gradual shift within the epistemological paradigm of self definition that evolves from idealism's objectification of the body to an aesthetics of "material reality," where the body and its wounds offer corporeal reality, if not painful subjectivity. Lyon structures his argument into four chapters that examine four separate works by Holderlin, Brentano, Kleist, and Buchner each from a different philosophical framework. Even though each work presents a different relationship between violence and self definition, the prevalence of the wound unifies the texts and provides a cohesive narrative that counters post Kantian metaphysics and challenges idealism's privileged supersensible conception of the self. Lyon first examines Holderlin's Hyperion as a rewriting of Fichte's ego philosophy. He argues that Holderlin attempts to offer tangible, physical experience that overcomes Fichte's division of subject (das Ich) and object (das nicht Ich) through the physical paradigm of "perpetrator " and "victim" (45) In Lyon's view, the metaphor of the wound not only enables this paradigmatic shift, but also becomes the central metaphor for reading the text of Hyperion as well as for understanding the development of Hyperion's own subjectivity. His wound engenders a dual subject, a self torn (zerrissen) between the status of a reflecting victim (the text) and a narrating subject (Hyperion) In the second chapter, Lyon begins his interpretation of Brentano's Godwi within the Romantic sphere of Fichte and Schelling, but soon transitions to a Freudian analysis. He focuses on the psychological trauma of wounding and argues that Godwi's model of self definition presents a "psychological and emotional framework" that engages transcendental models of the "conscious, rational self" (81) Similar to Hyperion, the wound again engenders self definition through a violent division of subject and object. However, in Godwi Lyon suggests this division is ironically constructive in its apparent destruction. In this work, characters transform trauma and wounds into art, textuality and their own subjectivity. Lyon continues to examine the paradoxical relationship between the destructive and constructive nature of violence in his third chapter on Kleist's Der Zerbrochne Krug. He argues wounds narrate conflicts of subjectivity between an individual and the state, both violently attempting to assert self affirmation. To develop his thesis, Lyon relies on Walter Benjamin's essay "Zur Kritik der Gewalt," which sees violence as a necessary component of self determination. However, this violence does not lead to stable subjects. In fact the opposite holds true. Violence only seems able to ensure ambiguous definitions of self that are as insecure as the timing of fate itself. This seemingly pessimistic view of self definition continues in the fourth chapter on Buchner's Dantons Tod. Here Lyon analyzes a subject afflicted with fragmentation who ironically seeks wholeness through the destruction of an other: "compensation for deficiency always involves wounding, fragmenting another body to ensure the wholeness of one's own" (175)
