New light on ethnic differences in adult lactose intolarance
In a two part article published in this journal in 1969 and 1970 (1, 2) I made an effort to relate the history of milk use to the exciting new medical evidence that revealed striking differences in the incidence of primary adult lactose intolerance among the world's peoples. My review of the medical literature led to the conclusion that genetic determinants of such differences likely are most important. I suggested that before milkable domesticated animals were available to humans (after about 10,000 Be)all human populations were characterized by the enzyme pattern typical of land mammals, in volving high activity of intestinal lactase during nursing and a drop in such activity to prevailing low levels in adulthood. In this view, primary adult lactose intolerance, a condition characterizing the vast majority of the world's peoples, was the early and normal state of mankind. The changes that led to low incidences of such intolerance in certain human groups, I hypothesized, were brought on by selective pressures under specific ecologic conditions that favored the aberrant individual who experienced high lactase activity throughout life. Important questions bearing on the above hypothesis have been raised by subsequent medical research, and I intend to treat some of them in forthcoming papers. In this paper I will focus on the distribution patterns of tolerance and intolerance.
