Seasons of Misery: Catastrophe and Colonial Settlement in Early America
Just before William Bradford begins to chronicle the descriptive qualities of the New World in his highly anthologized journal, Of Plymouth Plantation, he pauses. Attempting to account for the astounding transformation between the ambitious Englishmen who arrived at Plymouth and the profound destitution of current circumstances, Bradford interrupts the familiar genre of colonial survey to command us, too, to "stand half amazed" at the change. He writes, "'But here I cannot but stay and make a pause, and stand half amazed at this poor people's present condition; and so I think will the reader, too, when he considers the same'" (207) Kathleen Donegan's provocative analysis of a foundational "discourse of catastrophe" in accounts of early English settlement in America concludes with this rather famous pause (8) Seasons of Misery: Catastrophe and Colonial Settlement in Early America argues that the earliest specimens of colonial literature are marked by physical and epistemological ruptures. These ruptures, like Bradford's, are prompted by the settlers' sense of their own suffering. Indeed, Donegan argues that it is through the estranged experience of suffering that settlers came to understand themselves as such. Throughout four case studies, Donegan argues for an understanding of catastrophe as both a primary trope of colonial expression and the basis for a collective colonial identity removed from that of the settlers' fellow Englishmen. She establishes this claim by dwelling in the difficulty of colonial testimony, analyzing the textual representation of traumatic events through their formal breaks and narrative idiosyncrasies.
