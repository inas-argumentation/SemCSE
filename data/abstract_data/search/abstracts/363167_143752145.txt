The Continuities of German History: Nation, Religion, and Race across the Long Nineteenth Century By Helmut Walser Smith. Cambridge and New York: Cambridge University Press. 2008. Pp. 246. Cloth $75.00. ISBN 978 0 521 89588 0. Paper $22.99. ISBN 978 0 521 72025 0.
Current research, writes Helmut Walser Smith, has neglected investigations of long term influences on modern German history. Emphasis on rupture, fragmentation, and skepticism toward master narratives has foreshortened the temporal frameworks for explaining National Socialism and the Holocaust. Recent historiographical focus on biomedical racial hygiene and other distinctly modern aspects of the Nazi dystopia, however important, has diverted attention from longer continuities that impinge on the question of Nazi anti Semitism and genocidal practice. Smith identifies historians' preoccupation with shattered discontinuity as a "new orthodoxy" fragmentation has assumed the status of a "root metaphor" that precludes "traditional historical narrative" and the possibility of telling stories that strive for a "coherent whole" (pp. 4 5) To resist this trend and direct attention to deeper historical processes, Smith has designed five loosely connected essays as a "series of attempts to construct bridges across chronological chasms" (p. 6) These studies respectively examine the perspectives and blind spots of historical generations and schools interpreting the Third Reich; how Germans learned to spatialize, sense, and imagine the nation; the collective trauma of the Thirty Years' War on Germany's national memory that occluded Jewish remembrance; anti Jewish violence in Germany and eastern Europe over the long nineteenth century; and, finally, the intellectual origins of eliminationist racism. The sweep and intellectual breadth of these essays is stunning, all the more so because they move beyond the book's ostensible subject of Germany's long nineteenth century to address both continental Europe and the early modern period. The interwoven themes of nationalism, racism, and anti Semitism furthermore lend coherence to the collection, allowing the reader to think about their notional development over centuries. Collectively, the essays point to a longue duree of anti Semitic imagination, exclusion, and violence that current interpretations of genocidal practices largely overlook. In asking to extend the Holocaust's temporal and spatial "depth of field," the author raises key issues in method, interpretation, Central European History 42 (2009) 745 749.
