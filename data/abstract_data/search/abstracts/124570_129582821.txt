A key issue is the degree of synchroneity between different river systems, although problems with dating mean that this is, at present, dif cult or even impossible to establish. There follows a series of papers that have palynology as their focus. Atherden et al. take us to Macedonia, and a research project examining the changing loci and relative importance of ports and settlements within the Strymon Delta from the Bronze Age through to the nineteenth century. Hoaen and Coles consider an issue relating to the rural landscape, and describe the use of several genera of fungi in indicating the presence of herbivore dung. Mighall et al. consider the impact of prehistoric copper mining in southwest Ireland, and argue that this was not as devastating as previously thought. The often neglected subject of woodland management is considered, and an argument put forward that mining, and its associated demands for timber and charcoal, probably led to the retention and more careful management of woodland, rather than its destruction. The late Camila Dickson, to whom the volume is dedicated, addresses similar issues in the context of prehistoric Orkney, although she argues that the demands of iron production led to the loss of woodland. Cheverrell and Atherden describe the results of palynological investigations in the North York Moors on Iron Age through to post Roman peat sequences, a period for which we have relatively little off site palaeoenvironmental material. Interestingly, the incorporation of this region into the Roman world does not appear to have had a signi cant environmental impact, and in the post Roman period woodland regeneration does not appear until the sixth century, and even then very brie y. Later periods of woodland regeneration may relate to the eleventh century 'Harry of the North' following the Norman Conquest, and the dissolution of the monasteries in the mid sixteenth century. This paper raises some important issues, not least the problems of relating phenomena observed in the palaeoenvironmental record with documented cultural events; as with Tipping's paper, dating is a key problem. In another important paper, Caseldine et al. demonstrate the value of interdisciplinary landscape research in their study of human in uence in the mid Devon landscape. Apart from redressing the upland bias in southwestern environmental studies, this paper shows how palynology can be combined with a range of archaeological (aerial reconnaissance, eldwalking, excavation) and documentary evidence (place and river names) They similarly demonstrate that the Romano British period did not see any particular increase in the intensity of landscape exploitation. End eld et al. consider the impact of Conquest in the central Mexican highlands and demonstrate that, counter to the traditional view that environmental degradation resulted from the arrival of the Spanish, it was the native population that caused the greatest damage. As a balance to this, however, Steele et al. and Patton remind us of how the arrival of humans on several Mediterranean islands and in South America led to the disappearance of some native fauna. The wider range of environmental indicators is demonstrated by a number of the remaining papers. Schelvis uses mites to measure the extent of marine in uence in coastal regions of the Netherlands. Smith looks at the disappearance of 'Rif e' beetles from lowland river systems in Britain, and the implication this has for dating the onset of widespread alluviation. In perhaps the most innovative paper of all, Webb uses ethnographic data to reconstruct recent patterns of vegetation in western Australia, showing that the depletion of the ora in this area is a recent phenomenon, and that until the nineteenth century the region was quite capable of supporting viable hunter gatherer communities. As is the way with conference proceedings, there has been some delay in the publication of these papers (the Symposium was in 1995) but it was worth the wait. They contain some important data and stimulating discussion. While pondering the perceived 'mid life crisis' affecting environmental archaeology, the editors also stress that 'environmental archaeology is archaeology, because archaeology is about people, not artefacts, and people are, inescapably, an integral part of terrestrial environments' This is very true, and the case studies presented here are an excellent example of how cultural and environmental studies should be integrated. This archaeologist is left to wonder whether it is the archaeologists who overlook environmental data and issues who should be having the crisis.
