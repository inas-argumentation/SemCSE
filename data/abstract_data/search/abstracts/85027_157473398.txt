Special issue on low carbon mobility
Low carbon mobility (LCM) features strongly in the debates about the sustainability of cities and their resilience in the face of demographic, economic, and climate change. Transport is a major source of carbon emissions and there are indications that these continue to increase, despite the considerable recent advances in vehicle, engine, and fuel technologies. Reducing carbon emissions from transport may be becoming more difficult, not easier. Further, just what constitutes low carbon mobility is a key concern for researchers, policy makers, and analysts alike. There is an emerging international consensus that significant research on low carbon mobility is needed, largely because of the complexity of the topic and the need for packages of mutually reinforcing policies and strategies. An optimum blend of technological development, infrastructure planning, innovative policy developments, and community behavior change is apparent. Each of those areas requires substantial research in its own right, and then the optimum combination needs to be explored. One major question is just what policies should be considered and how the policy settings can be optimized. For example, the specific development processes of individual cities may significantly affect the feasibility and effectiveness of different policies. Some of the research issues identified as important for low carbon mobility include health co benefits of active transport modes, demand estimation and forecasting accounting for travel behavior change, the utilization of electric and alternative fuel vehicles and implications for infrastructure provision, mechanisms for decoupling economic performance and transport emissions, urban form and land use transport integration, and travel substitution. The specification, formulation, and development of LCM modeling, interpretation, and visualization tools also need attention. This special issue of International Journal of Sustainable Transportation is intended to provide a coherent overview of current research on low carbon mobility and the implications for future urban developments. It contains six articles that cover a number of these topics, as well as a critical review of some of the present policies related to low carbon mobility. Mulley and Tsai address issues in land use transport integration, with a focus on the potential impacts of public transport infrastructure improvement on housing prices, assessed by considering changes in accessibility. They constructed a repeat sales model to investigate the impact of a new transitway development in Sydney, Australia, on residential housing prices and accessibility. They then identified the extent to which the transitway has an impact on local housing prices through accessibility improvements to the study area. Their analysis provides an interpretation of how the quantification of increases in land value from this infrastructure upgrade can represent part of the wider economic benefits of investment in new urban public transport infrastructure. Hendrigan and Newman continue the consideration of the potential impacts of land use transport integration, adding the potential impacts of urban design, especially transit oriented development (TOD) There is a longstanding premise that sustainable transportation ideally requires the arrangement of new urban growth in close proximity to major public transport services, but the empirical evidence is not yet conclusive. Their article describes a modeling process undertaken for an urban redevelopment project to understand the potential, and limitations, of alternative modes of transport to support increased activity density in the redevelopment. Several of the standard policy levers were considered, such as parking ratios, land use mix, and building height, and compared with the trip generation and transit mode capacity to reveal potential real estate yields. The outcomes indicate not only the immediate yields but also the capacity for urban transformation due to each level of sustainable transport investments. Electric vehicles (EVs) are widely promulgated as a solution for low carbon mobility through the direct reduction of carbon emissions from their use, but a deeper consideration of systemwide emissions may throw some doubt on the reality. In order establish the extent to which EVs will deliver abatement, Mills and MacGill seek an understanding of the electricity and transport sector greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions impacts arising from different approaches to integrating EVs into the power system. For them, a key issue is the extent to which GHG emissions are a function of where and when EV charging will be enabled (or disabled) by the provision of recharging infrastructure and implementation of charging management strategies by the electricity industry. They provide an investigation of the carbon emissions arising from electricity and gasoline consumption by plug in hybrid EVs under a range of standard EV power system integration scenarios. An assessment framework is presented, and carbon emissions from EV use are assessed using 2011 data from the Australian National Electricity Market (NEM) They conclude that there is a range of possible outcomes depending on the integration scenario and emissions accounting approach used. A temporally explicit assessment approach in capturing the temporal alignment of electricity sector emission intensity and EV charging is found to be valuable in this regard.
