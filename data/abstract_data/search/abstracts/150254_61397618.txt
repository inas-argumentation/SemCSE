In the words of David Meeks, sports editor of the Times Picayune: 'We tried to check out stuff as best as we could, but we had stuff get out there that turned out to be not quite right, or not true, that's the nature of the beast' (p. 30) They also used their position to blast federal authorities for their incompetence and to rally the community behind their local leaders. The authors applaud the advocating role assumed by journalists during Katrina. But they could have been a bit more critical in their analysis. Although their instant reporting was helpful to those seeking to communicate in the midst of a technology blackout, it also created a conduit for wild rumors. Local journalists failed to verify and counter unfounded stories about babies being raped and helicopters being shot at. Although the reporters credit their reporting for getting the federal government finally moving, the response was, in fact, delayed by the multitude of rumors suggesting that New Orleans had descended into anarchy. The second change was a much more critical approach toward local institutions and leaders. For instance, local reporting of the Times Picayune eventually helped to bring to justice a group of rogue officers of the New Orleans Police Department (now known as the Danziger Bridge incident) The BP oil disaster became a source of frustration for local journalists. Ready to be critical, they never came close to the disaster as BP officials held them at bay. The authors show how the local journalists relied on local officials for information, undermining their new found role as critical watchdogs. Local journalists did not challenge Louisiana Governor Bobby Jindal when he presented solutions to the oil problem that found little or no support in scientific research. Not understanding the science of the oil gusher, they were soon sidelined by a cast of pseudo experts who effectively pretended they did understand. The authors end the book with a clarion call for local journalism. But there is little reason for optimism. The Times Picayune, lauded during and after Katrina, recently succumbed to the relentless economic pressures affecting all contemporary journalism. So, the authors ask, how will we learn about the next disaster? This passionately written and rewarding book provides the building blocks for a much needed discussion on the future of disaster journalism.
