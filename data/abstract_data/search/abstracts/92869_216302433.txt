Urban ministry reconsidered: contexts and approaches
Urban Ministry Reconsidered, is timely in an age of unprecedented growth in urbanization with 55% of the world's population now living in urban areas and expected to increase to 68% by 2050. The introduction leads smoothly into four main sections: Urban Conceptual World views (8 chapters) Urban Community Formation (9 chapters) Urban Social Policy (8 chapters) and Urban Ministry Adaptations (7 chapters) The primary question it poses is, what does it mean to minister in diverse urban environments, conditions and contexts? (10) The book's editors feature contemporary approaches and methods used by urban churches and urban ministries that offer possible policy reforms and effective frameworks for the promotion of human flourishing in the twentyfirst century. What is outstanding about this book is the scope of experience it brings together from 32 contributors across ecumenical divides. It is an excellent resource for pastors, urban missionaries, church planters, bible school students, theology professors, lay leaders, project managers, and theological students. The book's limitation is found in the lack of representation of perspectives from South America, the Middle East and Asia. Its primary location is the United States of America, even though it incorporates seven ministry contexts located in Africa and Europe. With ten women contributors, the gender mix could have been better and the voice of millennials is underrepresented. That being said, the book calls us to be respectful of the 4400 cities of the world and to acknowledge that these new (and not so new) urban social, economic, cultural, and religious configurations and realities offer diverse and serious challenges to the twenty first century Christian Church. Peters warns us of the diminishing influence of Christianity in "the urban public square" and the growing possibility that Christianity will be relegated to being a "cultural residue from a past society" (20) The variation of contexts, approaches and methodologies demands first, a responsive and flexible mobilization of all the gifts and graces found in the Christian Church and second, collaboration between other urban partners, such as ecclesiastical bodies, local government agencies, community self help groups, etc. for example, the Bible Center Church's Oasis Food Project raised up by Boddie (183) What is certainly a unifying principle in this book is summarized by Slayton and Kolbe "urban ministry's ultimate purpose is to affirm the Imago Dei (Image and Likeness of God) in each person and to create value and to contribute to promoting human flourishing"(73) Ministry approaches that are "insufficiently alert to these contextual factors are at risk of missing significant life shaping details of those who the ministry is directed to and may even be oblivious of the daily realities of those persons lives" (2) We therefore could end up with a counter productive situation where "ministry" is no longer serving human flourishing, instead it undermines and minimizes it.
