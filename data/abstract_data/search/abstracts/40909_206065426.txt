The radiotherapeutic technique of palliation in late cancer of the breast
IT is indeed difficult to draw the line between curative and palliative t reatment in cancer of the breast, so in order to simplify my task of focusing part of our discussion to day upon the technique of palliative radiotherapy, I propose to draw an arbitrary line at the point where deep fixity of the pr imary tumour or of secondary glands renders radical operation impracticable, and hope of permanent cure remote. Beyond this arbitrary line the object of palliative t reatment may be primari ly to lengthen life at one end of the scale, or merely to relieve some distressing sign or symptom at the other. The decision to embark upon palliative treatment in the first case follows naturally upon recognition that the lesion is no longer operable. In the second case the decision is by no means automatic and may in fact call for most careful assessment of a number of considerations. The radiotherapist frequently receives advanced cancer cases with a request that he should " give this poor soul a few palliative X rays " Sometimes symptomatic relief is intended sometimes the treatment is meant merely as a placebo. Irradiat ion is obviously being looked upon as the last means of " doing something " and, moreover, as being a safe means. I t is true that when used correctly radiotherapy can at t imes make an intolerable condition bearable, but illconceived attempts at palliative radiotherapy, however well intentioned, may not only fail to afford relief but may themselves cause great distress. Ten years ago Ralston Paterson at a meeting of the Facul ty in Liverpool read a most comprehensive paper about palliation, and Mr. Durden Smith in opening this discussion to day has refreshed our memories about the important features of the subject. When the radiotherapist is presented with the advanced case, not only has the decision " to treat or not to t r e a t " to be made, but the technique and dosage have to be decided. In order to make these decisions wisely it seems to me that for each individual case one requires first of all to answer five questions. First what sign or symptom is most distressing to this patient These may vary widely in breast cancer, including amongst others, an ulcerated skin nodule, a mass causing pressure pain, a spinal deposit with compression of the cord, the sickness of liver metastases, or the cough and h~emoptysis of lung secondaries. The two last usually imply that palliation is impossible, though they do not necessarily exclude the need for placebo therapy in a few particular cases. Secondly, what therapeutic agent that offers a good chance of relieving this distressing symptom is available The degree of radio sensitivity of carcinoma of the breast is sufficiently l imited to make one hesitate to offer radiotherapy as the automatic choice in every case. Moreove r and here, sir, I realize that I introduce controversial ma t t e r the degree of radio sensit ivity is such that very often if the desired palliation is to be achieved a radical dose (albeit to a very limited volume) must be employed. Furthermore, as radiotherapists we should not forget the value of analgesics, nor lose sight of the possibilities offered by the new euphoriants. Despondency and fear are dreadful symptoms which do not respond to radiation, and which tend to increase the perception of persistent pain.
