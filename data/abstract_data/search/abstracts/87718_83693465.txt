South Africa is reaching for new possibilities, with green fingers Dishing up the future
"We are living at a time on earth when we have to figure out what it means to be human when every living system is declining, and the rate of decline is accelerating." These words, spoken by Paul Hawken, environmental activist, in 2009 are even more applicable today. Green Chemistry, also known as sustainable or benign chemistry, is a science that is seriously looking at this challenge. Sustainable practices are capable of being maintained at a steady level without exhausting natural resources or causing severe environmental damage. Current chemistry practices are not sustainable. There will come a time when Green Chemistry will no longer be a nice option; it will be the only option. Green Chemistry is a new approach to chemical research and engineering that invents, designs and applies products and processes that reduce or remove the use and generation of harmful substances. The basic principle of green chemistry is pollution prevention during the design and development phase rather than afterwards. Green chemistry processes try to be 'benign by design' This means they are designed to protect humans, wildlife and the environment, while conserving energy and resources, which in turn have significant financial benefits. Green Chemistry is environmentally sustainable and can solve today's most pressing environmental problems, such as pollution, global warming and ozone depletion. Initially, it will cost more to implement Green Chemistry processes, but in the long run, the economic benefits will be astounding. 2011 is the International Year of Chemistry and is a perfect opportunity to get young people fired up about the possibilities of Green Chemistry. The concept of Green Chemistry came about in the 1990s following 'The Pollution Prevention Act of 1990' For chemistry to even be considered 'green' it must be environmentally benign, economically viable and must perform as well or better than existing options. Green Chemistry aims to do this by minimising waste; removing expensive end of pipe treatments; producing safer, less toxic products; using safer solvents; saving energy and resources and improving competitiveness of chemical manufacturers and their customers. Although the environmental benefits of Green Chemistry are indisputable, many people will wonder if it is a good place to pour energy into when South Africa has so many other problems to deal with. South Africa's burdens include poverty, lack of education, unemployment and the epidemics of HIV/AIDS, malaria, cholera and tuberculosis, all of which have a negative impact on the economy because they affect the productivity of South Africa's workforce. Green Chemistry would probably become a priority if it could address these issues, as well as being economically beneficial in its own right. Green Chemistry can in fact be used to improve healthcare, create more jobs, improve living conditions, improve agriculture and save money in industries. Green Chemistry makes it possible for rich and poor alike to access technology and is closely linked to some of the United Nations' Millennium Development Goals which are to ensure environmental sustainability and combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases. Green Chemistry has already had several breakthroughs in the treatment of the diseases that are seriously affecting South African's economically active population. The drug substances to cure these diseases are difficult to produce, needing many steps, large amounts of solvents, and extensive purifications. Green Chemistry can reduce the costs of making drugs, improve purity and make them more efficient in the patient by using microwave irradiation and biocatalysis (a process which uses biodegradable, renewable enzymes or clays rather than harmful chemical catalysts to accelerate chemical reactions) Clays and microwave irradiation can be used to make compounds that have inhibit HIV 1 protease, one of the key enzymes responsible for proliferation of the virus that causes AIDS.
