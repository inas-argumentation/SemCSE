Many are reproduced in colour or as full page illustrations. The rich visual record uncovered by Garner suggests the makings of an engaging exhibition. Born in a Tent covers the sweep of Australian history from the arrival of Cook in 1770 onwards, but with a focus on the 1820s 1920s. His narrative begins with the landing of the First Fleet, which carried with it over six hundred tents to use as temporary accommodation. He then looks at the camps of convicts, officials, explorers, settlers, the unemployed, diggers and democrats, workers, surveyors, utopians and idealists, youths and soldiers, intellectuals and eccentrics. Chapters also cover well known artistic and literary campers, along with present day camping activists. The final chapter's consideration of the Aboriginal Tent Embassy, erected as a form of protest in front of Parliament House in 1972, is especially attuned to camping as a subjective and contested activity. I expected a history of Australian camping to have drilled deeper into the cultural history of holidaying previously explored in Richard White's On Holidays: A History of Getting Away in Australia (2005) However, the title chosen for the book signals Garner's over riding historiographical concern. It alludes to Henry Lawson's own reference to his origins and, by extension, the well worn debate about the origins of late nineteenth century Australian national identity. In places, Born in a Tent reads as romantic rebuttal to Graeme Davison's 1978 critique of Russel Ward's The Australian Legend, with Garner reifying experience in the bush to challenge Davison's 'urban context' argument for, and cultural explanations of, the legend. There are drawbacks to singling out the primacy of 'reality' in the formation of national identity. To start with, it works against transnational comparisons, even though Australia's history of camping echoes that of New Zealand and other settler societies. Garner also dismisses the rapid rise of recreational camping in the mid twentieth century in Australia, ostensibly because it is well known, but arguably because it sits uncomfortably within his argument of national exceptionalism. His alternative explanation for the enthusiastic uptake of holiday camping stresses continuity that Australians have always camped for pleasure. He even speculates that 'at times goldfields camping was not unlike a holiday' (79) Rather than treating the summer camping holiday as a distinctive, historically contingent experience and cultural practice, Garner reduces it to 'a ritual re enactment of return, and of playing out the nation's foundation story' (22) If this were the case, then supporting evidence is needed. Oral histories could provide sadly lacking insights into what camping, past and present, means to camping and non camping Australians. While Garner notices the tents in the historical landscape he has not shown how individual campers themselves made sense of living under canvas and yarning by the campfire. Because Born in a Tent has been pegged so securely to The Australian Legend, there is still room for other histories of camping to be pitched.
