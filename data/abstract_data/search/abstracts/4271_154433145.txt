Rules Rather than Discretion: Did Europe Forget about the Inconsistency of Optimal Plans?
As soon as the first economic downturn occurred since it was agreed upon in 1997 by all countries joining EMU, the Stability and Growth Pact came under fire. It has been blamed to be not flexible enough, to operate asymmetrically over the cycle, to disregard the aggregate fiscal stance, to be unfavourable to long term investment. This paper builds up a simple theoretical model, which explores the interactions between governments choosing taxes and public debt and a central bank setting monetary policy. Th em odel clearly shows that in the presence of a not completely independent central bank a rule limiting the amount of government debt may be necessary in order to avoid undesired inflation. The paper also points out that a debt rule is especially recommendable under a monetary union, in which the cost of negligent behavior by one country may be borne by other countries, above all when differences in debt levels among countries are sizable. The model provides a useful framework that enables to easily investigate appealing and undesirable features of such a rule.
