'FRIENDS ALONGSIDE'
In her stimulating article 'Feminism Seduced: Globalisation and the Uses of Gender' Hester Eisenstein has raised some important questions about the relationships 'between mainstream feminism and globalisation' She asks troubling questions such as 'In what ways do aspects of the contemporary feminist project aid neoliberalism or free market capitalism? (Eisenstein this issue) Indeed, she finds that a version of contemporary feminism has become incorporated by powerful neoliberal forces into the agendas of international corporate capitalism which enslaves poor women (and men) in the Third World to the depredations of international capital, hell bent upon the race to the bottom. Some of the issues addressed in Eisenstein's paper resonate with my own work on the relationships between Indian and Western women and feminists in Kolkata in the early twentieth century. Her paper has led me to ruminate upon three issues in relationship to my work. First is the nature of relationships between the powerful/colonisers and the oppressed/colonised. Should these be thought of only as highly binarised relationships? Associated with this must be the issue of the agency of those oppressed and/or colonised. Such questions are raised by Chilla Bulbeck in her groundbreaking work Re orienting Western Feminisms, where she ponders dualism and 'strategies for disrupting black and white oppositions' and explores 'the limitations of a dualist approach' (Bulbeck 1998, 18) Finally there is the question of feminism and its relationships to other current political, economic and social forces and movements. While feminism cannot be thought of either as unsullied or an autonomous movement, removed from social, political and economic contexts, what does its intertwining with other forces mean? What good, if any, for women from colonised or oppressed groups, can come from such intermingling of agendas, particularly when feminist ideas (or at least ideas about the advancement of women) are on the agenda of powerful foreign forces? Thinking about these issues in relation to the situation in Kolkata in the first three decades of the twentieth century, I argue that a conception of sharp binaries is not helpful and obscures much. In the early twentieth century, Indians, both men and women, faced, in the British Empire, a powerful foe, which had dominated their society, economy and polity for many decades. This power was challenged, particularly in Bengal. Indeed, the revolutionary terrorism practised by a section of the Indian nationalist movement meant that Bengal and Kolkata at times were under martial law (Sarkar 1987, 135) While this can be seen as a highly binarised situation, at the same time Indian feminists worked with Western feminists and also with British officials in the cause of women's education and advancement.
