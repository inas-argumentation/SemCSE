A Faithful Account of the Race: African American Historical Writing in Nineteenth Century America
A Faithful Account of the Race: African American Historical Writing in Nineteenth Century America. By Stephen G. Hall. The John Hope Franklin Series in African American History and Culture. (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, c. 2009. Pp. [xviii] 334. Paper, $22.95, ISBN 978 08078 5967 4; cloth, $65.00, ISBN 978 0 8078 3305 6. Stephen G. Hall's A Faithful Account of the Race: African American Historical Writing in Nineteenth Century America celebrates nineteenth century African American writers who strove to correct negative images of African Americans and Africans during that time. Hall opens with a discussion of William Wells Brown and Brown's race history titled The Rising Son; or, The Antecedents and Advancement of the Colored Race (Boston, 1874) which questioned if Carthage was a Phoenician colony and asserted that Queen Dido, a fugitive from Tyre, was welcomed as ruler by a population "the larger portion of whom were from Ethiopia, and the surrounding country" (Brown, p. 49) Thus, Brown presumably regarded Hannibal, whose biography he condenses from Jacob Abbott's History of Hannibal, the Carthaginian (New York, 1849) to be of Ethiopic extraction. Given his subjects' obsessions, Hall is unexplainably hostile toward the term, if not the concept, Afrocentrism, considering it "modernist" and lacking in seriousness and complexity (pp. 9, 63) Traditional Afrocentrism is hardly modernist in the sense in which that word is usually employed. The term modernist usually applies to the New Negro movement between the World Wars, when Langston Hughes, Leopold Senghor, and others executed the dexterous intermingling of classical Ethiopianism with contemporary Negritude. Classical black nationalism and Afrocentrism clashed with the modernist tempo of the jazz age, which was clearly antithetical to their Victorian gentility. The nineteenth century constructs of Brown, Edward Wilmot Blyden, Alexander Crummell, Pauline E. Hopkins, and the early work of W. E. B. Du Bois were elite and neoclassical, simultaneously traditional and inventive, seeking to harmonize black nationalism with cosmopolitanism. Du Bois was immersed in the Victorian zeitgeist, which paradoxically dominated his pan Africanism, as it did Blyden's and Crummell's. Hall's methodology unfortunately minimizes Blyden, as does John Ernest's noteworthy Liberation Historiography: African American Writers and the Challenge of History, 1794 1861 (Chapel Hill, 2004) often cited by Hall. Yet Blyden's work influenced Du Bois's The Negro (New York, 1915) another expression of classical Afrocentrism.
