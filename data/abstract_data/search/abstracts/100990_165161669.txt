Animism as an Affective Consciousness of the Earth Remembering Ecological Indigeneity in the Anthropocene
As a part of the renewed interest in animism among environmental humanists and religious ecologists, this paper regards animism as a set of human earth universals which is inherently connected with meanings of indigeneity, both existing and to be explored. It reconceptualizes animism as what the author calls an "affective consciousness of the earth." It permeates, ensouls, and animates plants, animals, humans, and their living environments, and enables their sentient agencies. Meanwhile, this planetary affective consciousness also expresses itself through the felt or believed forces of the formless beings, e.g. spirits, deities, gods, and goddesses, who have been regarded as companions or spiritual authorities of environmentally conditioned and religiously and culturally specific human nonhuman communities. In this reconceptualization process, the author wishes to articulate three sets of meanings of animism (1) a sentient expression of the earth, (2) an index of ecological indigeneity, and (3) an affective and transcending power that affords ethically and spiritually empathetic understandings of the interconnected habitats, trans habitat environmental flows, and different modes of being on the earth. Animism along with environmental determinism in the history of social sciences and humanities played a significant role in the hyperseparation of "the primitives" and "the civilized" and constructing racial traits of diverse peoples in different parts of the world (Plumwood 1993; Rose? While its historical contribution to the denial of the coevalness of indigenous populations with larger cultural and civilizational systems has been widely critiqued in post colonial studies (Fabian 2002) scholars, especially those situated in environmental humanities, are also showing their renewed interest in the eco religious aspect of animism in their search for innovative ways and means to address human earth relations (Jenkins, Tucker and Grim 2017; Drew 2014) spiritual beliefs based modes of sustainable living (Smyer Yu 2014; and others) planetary environmental ethics (Bauman 2014) inter species relationships in the more than human world (Abram 1997) and human induced climate change and livelihood challenges In this context, animism as a renewed ecological concept is being recognized as a node of human earth universals rather than an instrument of dichotomizing the earth and humans, with the latter as the oppressive and destructive agent to the former. The emphasis of this endeavor is given to a reconceptualization of animism as what I call an "affective consciousness of the earth." The last set of the meanings is particularly pertinent to the endeavors of scholars situated in environmental humanities calling for "the salvation of the physical world" (Duara 2015) from the global environmental crisis, for the relevance of locally specific indigenous ecological knowledge for alleviating global environmental agonies, and for envisioning and building a sustainable future of the world (Grim 2001; Tucker and Grim 2017; Smyer Yu 2017) in the Anthropocene, a human induced geological era. All these endeavors require the transcendence of national sovereignty (Duara 2015) conceived by modern cartographical practice rigidly demarcating human centered physical territories indifferent to the interconnectedness and interdependence of edged or overlapped regional habitats since the inception of the earth. Transcendence in this regard does not refer to the otherworldly divinity in but not of this world, with its detached presence on earth. Instead it is a pan worldly vision of the earth as a psychic physical union of all existences, sentient and non sentient, and organic and inorganic. It affords the moral and ethical foundation for the restoration of environmental integrity and equitable human earth and human nonhuman relations. In this regard, the affective consciousness of the earth identified through the reconceptualization of animism is a planetary universal germane to the earth, its diversely evolved ecosystems and all life forms. Hereby, it is also the intent of this paper to propose what I call "rhizomic ethics of sustainability" based on the entwined affective fabrics of living beings as a precondition of the sustainable future symbiotically implaced in the earth. Upon referencing voluminous ethnographic descriptions of human animistic practices around the world, Edward Tylor, a progenitor of animism studies, wrote in the late nineteenth century: To the theory of Animism belong those endless tales which all nations tell of the presiding genii of nature, the spirits of cliffs, wells, waterfalls, volcanoes, the elves and wood nymphs seen at times by human eyes when wandering by moonlight or assembled at their fairy festivals (Tylor 1920:294 vol.I) The manifold spiritual physical unions in human animistic modes of being are the primary defining feature in Tylor's conceptualization of animism as a subject matter of social sciences. To him, animism constitutes "an ancient and worldwide philosophy" found in "the religions of the savage and the civilized world" (Tylor 1920:427) Herein, to make a post colonial amendment, "the savage" in Tylor's text mainly refers the world's indigenous populations whom European missionaries, colonists, travelers, and scholars encountered. Obviously, animism is not merely a religious trait of non European peoples. It is equally found among Europeans. By affirming its "comparative universality" (Tylor 1920:21 vol.I) Tylor delineated animism as "the belief in spiritual beings" (1920: "the deep lying doctrine of spiritual beings" (1920:425 vol.I) and "the groundwork of the philosophy of religion" (1920:426 vol.I) pertaining to the interchangeable forms of human and animal souls and to the physical world animated by spirits (1920) The perpetual transmigrations of the souls of humans and animals, and the spiritual beings, e.g. local deities, formless supernatural beings, and spirits of human ancestors, constitute the invisible but intuited inner contents of the earth, plants, animals, and humans. Tylor's peers and scholars of later generations have presented their geographically varied case studies but converged on the "mystic," "magic," or "occult" powers (Levy Bruhl 1923; Clodd 1905; Tylor 1920) as the forces animating the earth and living beings. In Lucien Levy Bruhl's work, for instance, the earth conceived in an animist cosmovision is "a synthetic whole" that combines the visible and invisible worlds into one.
