Reading between the Lines: Translation Quality from Classical Antiquity to the Early Modern Period
Williams and Chesterman quote Salman Rushdi as saying, "description is itself a political act," implying that even empirical concepts are never entirely value free. Behind every definition, there inevitably lies a statement of particular expectations and particular values accordingly. Almost all translation theorists offer a definition for translation (as a product or a process) and the very definition of translation conveys, at the same time, unique features per se that distinguish translation (as defined by the theorist) from other textual products/practices. These distinguishing characteristics are in fact an expression of certain values: a manifestation of expectation about what the translation should or should not be. Most translation theories, therefore, are evaluative in nature because they explicitly or implicitly prescribe some specific translational behaviors and practices to be adopted and some others to be avoided. If we accept the definition of ideology simply put as "a system of evaluations," then all translation theories are deemed to be ideologically motivated in some way or the other. There are always some hidden aspects of quality underlying almost all writings on translation that could be revealed if they are read critically, even when they seem to have nothing to do directly with the concept of quality. I fully agree with House, when she aptly asserts that the concept of Translation Quality Assessment (TQA) is inherent in each and every translation theory: "Translation quality assessment presupposes a theory of translation. Thus different views of translation itself lead to different concepts of translation quality, and different ways of assessing it." The practice of translation is as old as human history. It is a myth that even predates history back to the time when the Tower of Babel was allegedly built and God created variety in languages. The history of the practice of translation can be traced back up to four thousand years ago. The history of systematic approaches to translation, however, is fairly short, dating back to the beginning of the twentieth century when linguistically oriented methodologies became the fashionable tradition of the time. The history of "Translation Studies" as an academic discipline is even shorter, beginning from the 1970s. This does not mean, however, that there was no such thing as translation theory before the twentieth century. According to Munday, translation theory did exist at that time, but it was generally scattered across "broad series of prefaces and comments by practitioners who often ignored, or were ignorant of, most of what had been written before."
