Facts and Fallacies About Goal Theory: Reply to Deci
Deci's (1992) recent discussion of motivation theory was devoted mainly to goal theory; however, I believe that his discussion revealed many misunderstandings of goal theory. I propose to clarify as many of these misunderstandings as space permits. Deci accused goal theory of being incomplete, because it fails to deal with many "interesting and important motivational questions" (p. 168) Of course, every theory in psychology is incomplete. What a careful theorist must do is to clearly specify the domain of phenomena which his or her theory encompasses. Goal setting theory (Locke Latham, 1990) attempts to explain the way in which conscious goals regulate performance and affect on work tasks and to specify the mediators and moderators of these effects. It seems pointless to criticize the theory on the grounds that it should be concerned with different phenomena. Deci also criticized goal theory for not stressing the distinction between what he calls freely chosen actions and actions which are externally compelled. We do not stress this distinction because, except in the case in which a person is confronted by physical force or the threat of force, we do not consider the distinction to be fundamental. We believe that all action is chosen in the sense that the individual must consent to accept the external pressures before they will affect action (Binswanger, 1991) Further, even the distinction between action taken under psychological pressure and that not taken under such pressure is not as easy to draw as Deci implied. What one person views as a high pressure tactic may not be so viewed by another person because of their different personal contexts and histories. Deci complained that from goal setting theory "one learns very little about promoting the acceptance of goals" (p. 170) This is a puzzling statement in that our book devotes two entire chapters to the subject of goal commitment and includes a summary table (Locke Latham, 1990, Table 6 4, p. 151) which lists 32( separate factors that we believe affect commitment.
