Green Energy Gains Ground in the Arab World
Oil was the Middle East's preeminent source of wealth in the twentieth century and is still its main export in the twenty first, but its composite states are actively seeking prospects in the renewables market as well. New systems for solar, wind, and hydroelectric have arisen across the Arab region in the last few years, under the aegis of official "green" agendas that the governments of Saudi Arabia, Morocco, Jordan, and other Arab states have promulgated in the last few years. Some of these agendas are quite ambitious: Saudi Arabia's plan, for example, calls for the kingdom to obtain 33% of its electricity from renewable sources by 2032. The whole region, furthermore, had 64 new renewable energy projects under development in 2013, representing a fourfold increase over existing capacity. National self interest has much to do with it. The Arab states' domestic energy needs are growing quickly, and large shares of the oil that they drill are consequently burned for domestic use. In Saudi Arabia, for example, overall energy demand is expected to rise from 55 GW in 2014 to 121 GW by 2032, according to the International Energy Agency, which also forecasts Saudi domestic oil consumption tripling in that time frame to fill the shortfall an outcome that would subject the region to massive increases in smog pollution and toxic effects on public health. Expanding renewable energy output would enable Arab states to attain the electricity that they need without endangering their people's physical well being in the process. That said, the region's gain would also be a gain for the whole world. In the near term, rising renewable energy capacity will allow the Arab states to place more of their oil on the world market, thereby lowering oil prices. In the longer term, the region could become an abundant source of clean energy exports such as solar cells and hydrogen fuel. And as it fills this new niche, it will simultaneously erase much of its present day carbon footprint no small change, given the immense volume of carbon dioxide that the region's vast oil infrastructure has long been adding to Earth's biosphere. The Arab Forum for Environment and Development (AFED) is a Lebanon based think tank that is advocating for energy sustainability throughout the region. The think tank's secretary general, Najib Saab, recently shared his thoughts on this subject with World Future Review (WFR)
