FRAMEWORK FOR EMPIRICAL STUDIES CONCERNING IMPACT OF 3 D CAD ON PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT PROCESSES
This paper attempts to establish a theoretical framework regarding the potential and present reality of information technology in its capacity to function as a medium that promotes inter personal and inter organizational communication and integrates a diversity of perspectives through an examination of three dimensional Computer Aided Design (3 D CAD) This study will identify the potential of new generation 3 D CAD to promote early and effective problem solving that goes beyond a specific department (or enterprise) in a product development project. 1. Research Question This study attempts to establish through an examination of three dimensional Computer Aided Design (3 D CAD) the potential and present reality of information technology in its capacity to function as a medium that promotes inter personal and inter organizational communication and integrates a diversity of perspectives. In supporting a multitude of individual and organizational activities, computer and network related technology encompasses two dimensions: It can be used both to "automate" activities previously carried out by human beings, and to "mediate" communication among individuals and across organizations (Zuboff, 1988) When computers were first used in business management, the intention was to advance automation within the enterprise. From the technological standpoint also, the use of computers was limited to batch processing and other such automated tasks and the computers did not have the ability to mediate communication. Today, however, open architecture, distributed networks, and the growth of multimedia are strengthening the role of information technology as an interactive medium and are influencing the ways in which computer networks are used. One such phenomenon has been the evolving use of 3 D CAD in product development. 3 D CAD was first used in Japan for product development in the late 1970s. At first, its principal use was as a tool for analysis and for automating the transfer of a product's surface shape accurately to a mold or die. In the 1990s, however, a new generation of 3 D CAD architectures, solid modelers began to gain popularity in Japan. In comparison with the older techniques, this new generation of 3 D CAD, through its ability to provide an immediate visual representation of a product during its development, started to take on greater prominence as a communication medium that could facilitate the imaging of consumer impressions of a product, relationships between parts and components, and effects on downstream processes. It should be noted that the spread of new generation 3D CAD techniques was accompanied, more or less concurrently, by the rise of the "frontloading" movement, which aims to extract and solve problems early in the product development process. Capable of communicating product images in a direct, intuitive manner, 3 D CAD in its current form can be regarded as having evolved into a medium that is used to leverage communications beyond the design department to departments responsible for downstream processes, and to sift out the issues from a wide range of viewpoints, at an early stage of the project. Furthermore, because products can be defined with strict precision, 3 D CAD enables accurate modeling and iterative, detailed simulation testing. This characteristic, together with 3 D CAD's function as a communication medium, can be seen as complementary factors that support early problem solving. Framed within an understanding that the growing emphasis on the capacity of information technology to mediate communication has the potential to extend the range of problem awareness both within and across organizations, and to increase the speed and level at which this range of opinion converges, this study attempts to establish the potential of new generation 3 D CAD to promote timely and effective problem solving that goes beyond a specific department (or enterprise) in a product development project. Using case studies of individual projects, we will first of all compare projects that use new generation CAD with other projects in the same product category that do not use new generation CAD, in order to examine the relationship between deployment of newgeneration CAD and the level and timing of assimilation of diversity. Secondly, by comparing product categories in which new generation CAD is used to a significant extent with product categories in which new generation 3 D CAD has not diffused yet, we will attempt to establish the relationship between product characteristics and the decision to adopt new generation 3 D CAD. 2. Theoretical Background Clark and Fujimoto (1991; 1987) viewed the process of product development as a chain of problem solving cycles consisting of five steps: (1) problem recognition, (2) alternative generation, (3) model building, (4) evaluation, and (5) decision making. Model building within the product development process of the organization is relatively clearly defined, progressing from the concept and design description, through to mockups, drawings, and 3 D models, physical prototyping and eventual mass production. In addition to serving as the media for model building, these models also become information assets in which the level of knowledge gathered in the product development process is progressively deepened and refined (Clark and Fujimoto, 1991) Product development is a typical example of the process of creating new information, a process in which delineating the problem and exploring the alternatives are important stages. Of crucial interest in this context is a broadening of perspective on the problem at hand. Specific means for broadening the perspective include contact with external information sources to gain ideas that lead to innovation (Utterback, 1974; Ancona and Caldwell, 1992) participation in development by users (von Hippel, 1976) and by suppliers (Asanuma, 1989; Sako, 1990; and Nishiguchi 1992) and cross functional communication conducted at a close level. It has been noted, through analyses of Japanese firms' success in the 1980s, that overlapping the processes of functionally disparate departments for the reduction of development lead time (Clark and Fujimoto, 1987) intensifies collaborative communication between departments, fosters interchange of disparate viewpoints thereby generating an awareness of manufacturability at the design stage, for example and results in the achievement of high performance, not only in terms of lead time but also in terms of quality and costs (Imai et al. 1986; Clark and Fujimoto, 1991; Womack et al. 1990) Japanese style product development organizations are, first of all, autonomous groups whose specific functions are ambiguously defined. Internally, they are characterized by deliberately stirring up various conflicts in interpreting the market environment, by homing in on the contradictions through discussion and dialogue, and by working toward a consensus of opinion in understanding the problems. To generate diverse awareness of the commercial environment and purposely impart tension within the organization, top management imparts challenging and equivocal goals, gives autonomy to product development organizations, and draws people together from a range of different backgrounds. At the implementation level, while middle management assumes the leadership and oversees the whole operation, development phases are overlapped, face to face communication occurs frequently both within and across department boundaries, and the diverse range of viewpoints gradually converge and evolve into the formulation of a product (Takeuchi and Nonaka, 1993; Nonaka, 1993; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995) Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) argue that this process is used by Japanese firms not only to develop products but also to create learning and selfevolution of the organization as a whole. The emergence of equivocality and conflict is actively sought, and the organization develops, not according to a plan handed down from above, but as a self organizing system that is ordered by meaningful information rather than formal precepts (Nonaka, 1993) The advent of the "front loading" movement, based on the exploitation of new generation 3 D CAD, can be ascribed to features of that media, such as the ability to directly communicate visual images of a product and to readily anticipate problems that may occur in downstream processes or at the consumption stage, which have lead to the use of 3 D CAD for inter departmental coordination in order to promote varied viewpoints and to arrive at a shared vision in approaching problems earlier in a project.
