Anglo Irish Pastoral, War, and Revolution: The Wild Swans at Coole (1919) Michael Robartes and the Dancer (1921)
The Wild Swans at Coole shifts the focus of Yeats's poetic concerns away from the public, urban, and overtly politicized world of Responsibilities and Dublin's middle class nationalism toward the pastoral retreat of Lady Gregory's estate in the west of Ireland at Coole Park, Galway, and toward Yeats's personal life his romantic relationships and marriage. Although he spent a good deal of time in England during the First World War, and was in London when it was subjected to air raids by German Zeppelins, Yeats was not inspired to write poetry about the war.1 Nor did he admire the English war poets, such as Wilfred Owen, whose complexity he missed; he seems to have thought they only expressed the meaningless suffering of war. Yeats later wrote: "I have a distaste for certain poems written in the midst of the great war passive suffering is not a theme for poetry. In all the great tragedies, tragedy is a joy to the man who dies; in Greece the tragic chorus danced."2 But he did begin to share the modernist preoccupations of Eliot and Pound with the cultural wreckage of which the war was the most spectacular symptom. The aesthetics of modernism for Yeats (and Joyce) though, took rather different forms than they did for the expatriate Americans, or the European modernists, in that they were also extensively shaped by the Irish experience of colonization.
