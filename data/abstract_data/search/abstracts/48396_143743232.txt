The judges, the House of Lords and politics
Robert Stevens is the doyen of judicial commentators, more accurate in his predictions than Lee, more nuanced than Griffith, more boisterous than Blom Cooper and Drewry, more daring than Dickson, and more provocative than Paterson. Robert reigns supreme as the judicial chronicler of choice in twenty first century England and Wales. With three books, 19 articles and 12 entries in the Dictionary of National Biography on judges, Robert's productivity in the field is unrivalled. Stevens is certainly a green scholar he has a deft penchant for re cycling but what he re cycles is ideas and with each iteration he adds refinement. To read Robert's work is to become a convert to the linear and the pendulum theories of progress at the same time the spiral or 3D theory of development. Robert is a legal historian not in the caricature sense of the mediaevalist whose proudest boast is to tell you what Robert of Gravelin spent on silver spoons at a banquet on 2 May 1434 nor again in the mould of such heavy metal post modernists such as Foucault or Bourdieu. Not for him the abstruse analysis of the legal philosopher, he will regularly discuss judicial law making, judicial activism and judicial restraint but always in a broad brush way. Yes, he can toss intellectual feathers as eloquently and elegantly as Jaffe when examining judicial creativity in the House of Lords, but one of Robert's foibles is a disinclination to define his terms. He knows about MacCormick on consequentialism or Ronald Dworkin on "Hercules and the one right answer" but neither of them to his mind enlightens the intelligent layman's understanding of the operation of today's final court of appeal. And that I think is the key. Robert Stevens' great strength as compared with most judicial scholars is his sheer readability you can take his work on a plane or to the beach on holiday and he retains the ability to capture your attention. Robert is a great communicator and his audience is a broad church. Robert Stevens probably hopes that his work is understandable by any intelligent bystander with an interest in modern affairs and he would be right. With one exception. Robert has a weakness for Latin phrases which he forbears to translate for those less erudite than himself which is unusual in one so natural a radical in his thinking. INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF THE LEGAL PROFESSION, VOL. 16, NO. 1, MARCH 2009
