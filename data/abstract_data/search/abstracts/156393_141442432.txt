A University Joins the Community: Universities, Schools, and Communities Can Collaborate to Ensure That Children Living in Low Income Communities Have the Supports They Need to Be Successful in School
Ten four good buddy! Truckers across the country know this usually happy sound that conveys a sense of community and collegiality. For us, ten four ten (10 4 10) was opening day for the University Assisted Community School (UACS) after school program, a partnership between Pond Gap Elementary School in Knoxville, Tenn. and the nearby University of Tennessee. This partnership centered on the idea that education done in and with the community can play a central role in addressing systemic inequities and providing academic and nonacademic learning for students. Winn and Behizadeh (2011) recognized "that the lack of opportunities for youth to engage in meaningful academic practices such as reading, writing, and speaking feeds the schoolprison nexus" (p. 149) But budget cuts, teacher layoffs, and program depletions make providing a rigorous, well rounded education increasingly difficult. Providing such an education, however, is still possible if K 12 schools, universities, and social service agencies are willing to collaborate. Pond Gap Elementary School (PGES) and its extended day program grew out of the work done by one of the authors, Bob Kronick (2005) an education professor who has collaborated with Title I elementary schools in the southeast United States since 1998. The UACS model shows how a school can do more with less. Pond Gap is a K 5 school with 320 students that needed such an advantage. Its students come from 23 different countries and speak 19 languages; English is not the dominant language for most. Nestled in a comparatively diverse community, Pond Gap is a Title I school with a 34% mobility rate where 90% of students receive a free or reducedfee lunch. The university chose Pond Gap as its partner because of principal Susan Espiritu's collaborative outlook. She wanted to provide PGES students with after school opportunities for cultural, academic, and sports activities. She and Kronick also wanted to provide programming during the hours when youths are most vulnerable to becoming crime victims or engaging in unhealthy activities, such as drug use. Even before the UACS was started, the school and university had collaborated to provide some services to students during regular school hours. Counseling and psychology university students worked with elementary students each week, providing them with consistent relationships and emotional support. In addition, other university and community volunteers tutored students who needed academic support during the school day. The university also eventually helped convert space used as a school closet into a school clinic where university pre med and nursing students volunteered to provide medical support. After receiving a three year grant totaling $321,000 from a local businessman, Kronick and Espiritu launched the Pond Gap after school program. The amount of money given would have paid for the incarceration of three juveniles for one year. The grant allowed the UACS after school project to hire an after school director and staff members and provide programming for 50 students plus educational opportunities and support to their parents and the community at large. The partnership and start of the UACS after school program was and still is an arduous task, filled with unexpected twists and turns. It is beginning to show dividends. Getting started The first and perhaps the most challenging task was getting everyone on the same page. At its core, the UACS afterschool program was both conservative and progressive because it sought to conserve the school and the community and also incorporate noncurricular programs. The overarching goal was to engage students in formal and informal learning activities, support students academically, emotion ally, and socially as they worked toward high school graduation, while encouraging them to pursue post secondary opportunities. However, this was easier said than done because it required collaboration between two systems a K 5 school that is part of a large school district and a major university that each had its own set of procedures and cultural expectations.
