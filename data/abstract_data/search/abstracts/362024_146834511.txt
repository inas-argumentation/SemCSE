Mimesis and Media Teleparticipation We may conclude that primordial mimetic practices, originating from ritual or based on concrete social relationships, constituted an enactive knowledge. This practical knowledge building connected to the body is not possible in practices of teleparticipation in which viewers remain connected to situations/contexts exclusively through audiovisual images. In this case, social interactions are replaced by what Morin (1990) called projection identification processes. In contemporary telematic environments, in which everything can be represented in a continuous exercise of abstraction, one of the few things that are impossible are the somato sensory images of Damasio, also making impossible that imagination could sprout since it is not guided by the instrumental and technological nature of industrialized media formats but an ancestral memory inscribed on the body of mankind. Mimetic behaviors are almost always automated. Damasio (2000, 404) points out: "There are too many images being generated (in the mind) and there is too much competition to the relatively small window of the mind where images can become conscious in other words, metaphorically, there is in fact an underground beneath the conscious mind." This "underground" of images is well known in psychology, translating what already since the end of the nineteenth century we consider the unconscious. Behaviors harking back to somato sensory images are difficult to emerge into consciousness, and this is also because behaviors are built through relationships of empathy. Empathy, as we have already addressed elsewhere, is an emotion and as such has an unconscious background. Mimetic processes of an empathic nature are in parts unconscious due to the principle of "attention economy" Consciousness requires a focused attention demanding an enormous mental energy expenditure as opposed to, for example, automated processes. Anyone who drives a car or plays a musical instrument is at least partly acting "automatically" We become unaware of automated actions due to the economy of attention, namely, the dynamics of mental energy, and it is certainly easier to automate ritualistic body gestures of sociability than, say, to learn a foreign language after early childhood. For our species, thus, not practicing mimesis is impossible. According to de Waal (2010, 41) we may even say there is a powerful "herd instinct" among the foundations of our society. Therefore, the substitution of empathic mimetic relationships by relationships of projection identification in contemporary society is a new social situation in which the body motor function does not participate directly in learning new ways of generating cognition through mimetic behavior. Due to their technical nature, audiovisual images have an extra somato sensory support. The relevance of this technical nature is not to be minimized by considering the transformations occurring in modes of cognition a constant interaction between man and imaging electronic machine and computer information. What then are the features of this new form of cognition generated by mimetic behaviors developed through teleparticipation?
