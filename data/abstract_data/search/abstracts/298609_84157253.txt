Welcome to papers from the 4th International Symposium on Dinosaur Eggs and Babies
This issue of Historical Biology consists of research papers stemming from the 4th International Symposium on Dinosaur Eggs and Babies held in Bozeman, MT, USA during August 2009. The first symposium was held in Isona, Spain, in 1999 and later meetings in Montpellier, France (2003) and Plaza Huincul, Argentina (2006) The Bozeman gathering thus represented the first opportunity to convene in North America. Approximately 40 participants (Figure 1) gathered from eight different countries at the Museum of the Rockies, Montana State University for 2 days of talks and presentations, followed by a 2 day fieldtrip to the Cretaceous Egg Mountain locality in western Montana. The small meeting allowed researchers with a wide geographic and analytical experience to share their work and ideas in a relatively relaxed setting. Presentations at the symposium spanned a diverse array of topics including taphonomy, egg and embryo morphology, biogeography, eggshell function and incubation, nesting behaviour, ontogeny and reproduction in extant vertebrates. Hayward (Andrews University, USA) provided an excellent summary of his taphonomic work on eggs that he and his students have been conducting for the last 20 years (see Varricchio et al. 2009 for an abstract of this and other cited works) Several presenters described collections of dinosaur and avian eggs and eggshell from China (Whyte, University of Sheffield, UK) Spain (Donaire, Universidad Complutense de Madrid, Spain; Moreno Azanza, University of Zaragoza, Spain) and New Mexico, USA (Tanaka, Hokkaido University, Japan) Others examined the functional and phylogenetic implications of eggshell microstructure (Jackson and Kern, Montana State University, USA; Lopez Martinez, Universidad Complutense de Madrid, Spain) and the relationship of insect (Martin, Emory University, USA) and dinosaur (Breithaupt, University of Wyoming, USA) trace fossils in understanding dinosaur nesting and growth. The diversity of fossil eggshell continues to grow, and understanding its significance will require both functional and detailed taphonomic work. Additional topics covered included scientists and the media (Botzer, National Geographic, USA) a Cretaceous turtle with internally preserved eggs (Knell, Montana State University, USA) the potential oceanic transport of eggs (Evans, Montana State University, USA) sauropod nesting (Fowler, Montana State University, USA; Vila, Universitat Autonoma de Barcelona, Spain) and dinosaur life history strategies (Varricchio, Montana State University, USA) Among the highlights of the symposium were several talks documenting the importance of technological advances in the study of dinosaur eggs and embryos. Shaw (Montana State University, USA) and MorenoAzanza described how electron backscatter diffraction permits m scale mapping of crystal orientations within eggshell. The details revealed may help to refine functional and phylogenetic understanding of eggshell microstructure. Remes (University of Bonn, Germany) and Fernandez (European Snychrotron Radiation Facility, France) demonstrated the incredible power of threedimensional imaging using X ray micro computedtomography (mCT) and synchrotron radiation based X ray microtomography (SR mCT) respectively. Both described embryonic remains and showed how these techniques can facilitate the taxonomic identification of poorly ossified embryonic remains even within eggs, a fundamental step needed in understanding the reproductive patterns of extinct taxa. Additionally, a National Science Foundation grant (NSF grant EAR 0847777 to D. Varricchio) supported the participation of four reproductive biologists as a means of bridging the gap between paleontologists and neontologists. Each provided an extended lecture during the symposium on reproduction in extant diapsids. Peter
