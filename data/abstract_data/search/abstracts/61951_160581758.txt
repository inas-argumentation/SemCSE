Everyday Eclecticism: William Morris and the Suburban Picturesque
Established in London in 1875, the early garden suburb of Bedford Park represented the best effort of architects and designers to live up to the ideals of the Aesthetic Movement. In most contemporary accounts, visitors remark on the picturesqueness, variety, and novelty of the Queen Anne architecture, in stark contrast to the drab monotony they would find in the ordinary middle class housing estate. William Morris visited the suburb in 1879 and soon after began writing of the historical dilemma provoked by the Queen Anne style, which captured the qualities of the picturesque only by eclectic imitation. Morris' revision of the idea of the picturesque and the writing of his utopian News from Nowhere emerge in dialogue with the aesthetic and communitarian principles of Bedford Park. The picturesque eclecticism of Bedford Park remains as a persuasive image of the road not taken in twentieth century domestic architecture, a failure of vision more easily explained by Morris' socialist critique of the picturesque aesthetic. Frequently characterized as middle class utopias, suburban "estates" embodied the nineteenth century ideal of "middlingness." Neither urban nor agrarian, neither proletarian nor aristocratic, the suburb charted a middle course in aesthetics and politics. The favorite architectural and decorative style of the nineteenth century suburb was, broadly speaking, picturesque. Most of the dwellings and the objects they contained were imitations, fakes, and copies of art and architecture that captured the ideals of home, community, and success. Appropriately, the picturesque as a style aspires to a condition of "middlingness" picturesque scenery thrives between wilderness and civilization, emerging from the action of memory and imagination upon what is seen, while the picturesque aesthetic emerges in the transition from classic to romantic art. In his landmark 1927 study, Christopher Hussey characterizes "the picturesque interregnum" as a vital, "intermediate process, of proving the truth of the visual sensation by a comparison to painting" (4 5) Many of those who have written about the picturesque since Hussey also interpret it as transitional, imitative, derivative, and even decadent, because it aims to organize and to produce a superior version of the real. Moreover, the use of literary and pictorial allusions in the picturesque promoted a "habitual exercise of comparison and association," while the close linking of art and nature naturalized certain social and political structures (Andrews, The Search for the Picturesque 3) In the eighteenth century, the picturesque was the province of the wealthy few, appreciated by those who could afford to admire poverty, wilderness, and decay; but soon bastard versions, like bastard languages, developed, against the wishes of the aristocratic cliques whose travels and education had made enjoyment of the picturesque possible. Wealthy dilettantes like Richard Payne Knight, an early connoisseur of the picturesque, viewed themselves as the legitimate inheritors of the entire spectrum of high culture, while the newly empowered middle classes were forced to construct their own relationship to the aristocratic culture from which they had been historically excluded. This tendency of the picturesque to produce offspring was inevitable, because it was, from its inception, an eclectic style. In "nature," anything could be picturesque; when artificially reproduced, almost any style or mixture of styles could be manipulated to achieve a similar effect. Thus an early "eclecticism of taste," as architectural historian Henry Russell Hitchcock termed it in 1954, quickly degenerated into a blissful eclecticism of tastelessness. By the late nineteenth century, the picturesque had become the favorite style of the middle class suburb, a compromise between architecture as "capital and scenery," and between landscape as city and country. As Ann Bermingham suggests in Landscape and Ideology, "The eclectic result of these compromises" was a "logical extension" of the love of variety typical of the picturesque (166, 170)
