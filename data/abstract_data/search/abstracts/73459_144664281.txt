The Institution of Public Television
RECENTLY the Bishop of Bayeux in northern France ordered less ringing of church bells on Sunday afternoons. The public had been complaining that the electric mechanism used to ring the bells these days caused "snow" on their television screens. Clearly that public would have its television, even if this meant cutting back on a tradition of bell ringing that was at least a thousand years old. Today the American public is being offered something new in television, a service that has recently appeared under the name of Public Television. What is it and how much of their bells or money will Americans have to give up in order to get it? In several senses, all of our broadcast television is public it uses a communication channel which, by its physical limitation as well as its strategic national importance, is clearly in the public domain just as much as our rivers, our atmosphere, and our national forests. Because our broadcast spectrum is necessarily in the public domain, our broadcasting law specifies that all operations on it shall be "in the public interest, convenience, and necessity." Furthermore, most of broadcast television is generally and freely receivable on sets owned by the general public. Nevertheless, there has recently come about a preemption of the term "Public Television" for one part of such broadcast activity.
