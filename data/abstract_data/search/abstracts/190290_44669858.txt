All of these approaches are based on recent advances in the understanding of motor control, motor learning, brain plasticity, and development. We do not need to discuss which approach is the most efficient since they are intended to meet different purposes. The intention when using HABIT is to improve the frequency and quality of bimanual hand use. By practising bimanual activities, children will be more successful and thereby recognize the benefit of using the two hands. CI therapy was developed to overcome learned nonuse in adults with hemiplegia, and, although children with hemiplegia do not need to relearn to use the hand, they need to learn what the hand can do.2 The typical aim is to improve the variability and quality of grasping. Here, it is important to remember that it is the elicited practice, rather than restraint, that is responsible for the improved motor performance. Goal Oriented Training is a third form of training that promotes increased independence in daily life by addressing and practising to achieve the goals that families have identified as being important and meaningful to them. Typical goals for children of school age concern age related activities like cutting food and tying shoelaces. In this case the children practise the specified task and nothing else. The fourth type of intervention is more global, the children need to learn cognitive strategies to solve critical and unpredictable situations in daily life.4 Examples are how to deal with difficult situations and complex activities such as how to manage to put food in a plastic bag in a shop and how to handle money when people are queueing behind them. In general, it is not easy to improve hand function, but giving families the possibility to work on something carefully and with intensity might be beneficial. It also means that after a period of therapy we can expect the children to be practising what they have learned.
