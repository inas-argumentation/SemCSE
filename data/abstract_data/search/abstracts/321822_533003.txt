New York, NY, APA Press, 2012, 223 pages.Address correspondence to: Paul Mitrani, MD, PhD, Child and Adolescent Consultation and Liaison Service, Stony Brook, NY 11794 8790, E mail: paul.mitrani@stonybrookmedicine.eduLooking back on my experiences in pediatrics, I realize that my perspective on pain has evolved quite a bit. As an intern, I was very sensitive and empathetic to both patients and families as they suffered through recurrent sickle cell crises, failed transplants, or unremitting dystonia. There was a pervasive helplessness as we pushed medications that provided only fleeting comfort. Over time, I learned to adjust my expectations for what analgesics could do, and questioned my growing cynicism when complaints of pain did not match with a patient's presentation. Although some were clearly in anguish, others appeared to be looking for an escape from something other than pain. Further confusion arose when the "objective" physical signs of pain, such as blood pressure and heart rate, became unreliable in the setting of chronic pain. Simultaneously, I saw how simply spending more than 10 minutes talking with a patient could bring relief as substantial as morphine could. After 5 years of seeing the same patients, I became aware that pain was a problem that wreaked havoc on a child's life beyond the physical realm.The American Pain Society describes chronic pain as the "result of a dynamic integration of biological processes, psychological factors, and sociocultural context, considered within a developmental trajectory." This is particularly important to recognize, as each child will have an entirely distinct way of experiencing and coping with chronic pain compared with an adult. Medically, the management of chronic pain is complicated by fears of overtreating that typically result in undertreatment. Moreover, a lack of emphasis on social and psychological factors can exacerbate the severity and dysfunction caused by pain. The current practice of pain management in children and adolescents stresses the use of a multidisciplinary team with multimodal therapy, involving biological, physical, and psychological methods. According to epidemiological surveys, 25 40% of pediatric patients report recurrent or chronic pain caused by underlying medical conditions, such as sickle cell disease, Crohn's disease, or cancer, or pain of unclear etiology. In cases of pediatric chronic pain, the additional obstacle of underdiagnosis continues to impede the implementation of care, resulting in a more prolonged course that interferes with normal development.Tonya M. Palermo's book on cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is an important addition to the literature on physical and mental health in pediatrics. As a psychologist who specializes in the psychological treatment of chronic pain, she focuses on teaching children cognitive behavioral strategies, such as relaxation and positive thinking, to better manage their pain and reduce the negative impact it has on their activity, socialization, and academics. She is in the forefront of pediatric research into the psychosocial and family factors that affect pain perception, daily functioning, and quality of life.In her book, Palermo highlights the biopsychosocial model of pain and how it contributes to a child's experience of pain and subsequent disability. By emphasizing the interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors, as well as health habits, in the complex dysfunction caused by chronic pain, Palermo lays the foundation for how CBT interventions can be applied to diminish suffering and reduce maladaptive behaviors. Palermo effectively provides a comprehensive hands on guide for the implementation of CBT for children and their families as they attempt to manage the distress caused by chronic pain.
