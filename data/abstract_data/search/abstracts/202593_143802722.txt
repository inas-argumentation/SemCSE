Early images of children and youth were influenced by a romanticized and idealized understanding of children and adolescent development as separate and distinct from adults' development. Thus they required different television shows and literary production from adults. Some shows dealt with children's moral and ethical development, while others problematized the construction of children's identities. Later on, the issues of sexuality and violence, rebellion and resistance were integrated into children's shows and literature. The most recent shift in media representation of children and youth was caused by the emergence of a consumer society in which children and young people have become a prime target of advertising. The child as a consumer required a different approach from a child who needed the protection of adults. Not surprisingly, television programme makers started to produce programmes focusing on this new, more independent audience. The youthful characters created their own social world in which their peers became their family of choice. So, what is the future of the family? As most of the research on this topic shows, the family is still very much alive, but with increasingly diverse social relationships and altered power arrangements. Marriage continues to be a desirable arrangement for most adults. The public struggle of gay and lesbian couples for legalization of their relationships shows mainstreaming of what used to be seen as alternative lifestyles. Also, poor women postpone their marriages, but not their childrearing, in order to fulfil their ideal of 'companionship marriage' They are waiting for the 'right' man who is harder to find because he has to offer his spouse emotional fulfilment as well as economic partnership. However, the depiction of the family, both in public discourse and the media, continues to be about mainstream, mainly white, middle class families. Estella Tincknell's book offers a comprehensive analysis of the shortcomings of such representations of the family and its complexities. Her knowledge of popular culture is extensive and her choice of examples enriches her theoretical discussion. The only shortcoming of the book may be that it will inspire the reader to watch more television.
