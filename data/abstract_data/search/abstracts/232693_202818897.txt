Commentary on: Borrini M, Garlaschelli L. A BPA Approach to the Shroud of Turin. J Forensic Sci 2019;64(1):137 43.
Sir, We have read with interest the paper entitled, "A BPA Approach to the Shroud of Turin," by Matteo Borrini and Luigi Garlaschelli (1) in which a living volunteer was employed to investigate arms and body position required to obtain the pattern of blood of arms and trunk visible in the image of the Turin Shroud. This is a linen cloth, 4.4 m long and 1.1 m wide, bearing the double image of a man who suffered physical trauma in a fashion consistent with crucifixion after being beaten, scourged, and crowned with thorns (2,3) Borrini and Garlaschelli state that their study "is partially inspired by previous investigations; however, it is focused by a more forensic and empirical approach on the analysis of the left hand, of the forearms (where traces are visible from the wrist to the elbow) and of the 'lance wound' and of the blood stains near the waist." Doing science means proving a hypothesis and having that proof replicated by one's peers. Regardless of the personal points of view and the limits of the techniques, the studies published in scientific journals have been substantially dedicated to answering the following questions: (i) the age of the Turin Shroud and (ii) the new serological and molecular tools and microscopy techniques are useful for advancing information on blood stains and features of the human fingerprint and testing the authenticity of the relic (4) In our opinion, the paper of Borrini and Garlaschelli contains several points that need to be addressed: Under the heading Conclusions, Borrini and Garlaschelli argue "The present study represents the first proper experimental Bloodstain Pattern Analysis (BPA) performed to better understand the evidence on the Turin Shroud and the behavior of the blood flowing from the wounds of a crucified individual." However, they also state as follows: "the inconsistencies identified by the authors seem not only to point against their own reality, but against the authenticity of the Shroud itself, suggesting that the Turin linen was an artistic or 'didactic' representation from the XIV century. This new Bloodstain Pattern Analysis supports the historical records, the radiocarbon dating, and the chemical analysis." Careful conclusions in modern science are based on facts and rely on multiple approaches that support and confirm each other, and the use of techniques and methods that cross check and verify specific findings is mandatory to rule out artifacts. The experiments made by Borrini and Garlaschelli have been conducted on a living and healthy human being without traumatic wounds of any kind and on a dummy vaguely reminiscent of a human trunk. But if it is not done with a living human being who has suffered the same wounds and with the same chronology supposed for the Man of the Shroud, nor with a corpse that meets the same requirements, then the experiment does not reproduce, not even approximately, the circumstances in which the blood stains originated. In addition, the blood was collected in CPDA (citrate phosphate dextrose adenine) as anticoagulant and stored at 5degC until use. Moreover, several variables such as hair of the skin, dirt, sweat, clots of previous bleeding, movements and muscle contractions, temperature, and humidity have not been considered. The voluntary subject on which the experiment was performed had no significant hair on the skin of the forearms, at least this is what can be seen in the photographs of the article, while the Man of the Shroud maybe had it or not: We do not have scientific data on this point. However, if he had them, the capillary stems are obstacles to the passage of any fluid, modifying its trajectory. Other points to consider are that the blood of a crucified man was pathological, due to the bleeding suffered and its pH was acid, a consequence of asphyxia, and that the subject was probably dehydrated and his hematocrit was higher. All these facts suggest that the behavior of the blood of the Man of the Shroud was different from that a blood of a living and healthy man. If these were not enough, from the wound on the side, there was not only a living blood flow but also postmortem blood clots. In particular for the lesion of the trunk, postmortem blood clots, pleural fluid, pericardial fluid (both following the scourging) and the fluid of pulmonary edema due to asphyxia could all be present. All these fluids are unlikely to come out mixed homogeneously and most likely, they did it in a heterogeneous way. This was not reproduced in the experiments made by Borrini and Garlaschelli. Other authors have approached the study of the blood stains of the Turin Shroud with a more scientific method. The transfer of blood clots to cloth as distinctive images is another complex point and was extensively studied by Lavoie et al. (5) considering also the time of clots formation and retraction. In addition, we think that the characteristic of the textile material, the time from bleeding and cloth contact, the duration of contact, and the movement of the body respect the cloth must be considered in the experimental design that has the objective to investigate these aspects. In addition, we must remember that when there are deep wounds, the cadavers have postmortem hemorrhages, especially when they are moved, and the body of the Man of the Shroud has certainly been moved and manipulated, then bleeding profusely from his wounds and his natural orifices. In addition, that blood was cadaveric blood, not living blood, or even chemically anticoagulated blood. Considering the above reported statement, it is likely that the "blood belt" was not produced by the flow of blood between the corpse and the cloth, but while placing the body on the linen, the corpse bled and released a trail of blood that perfectly reproduces the relative path between the corpse and the textile material that absorbed this blood. This fact, too, was not taken into consideration. All these considerations together suggest that the study of Borrini and Garlaschelli presents several limitations and that the conclusions of the authors are not supported by the experimental data.
