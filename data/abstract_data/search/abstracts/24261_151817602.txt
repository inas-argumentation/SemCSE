For example, she claims that Israel sets a "world record" in its use of pregnancy screening for healthy babies (p. 30) "[P]arents and doctors in Israel tend to go for abortion in cases that would generate hesitation in many Western countries" (p. 31) Israel has a "liberal approach to abortion" (p. 31) or, "Israel is number one in the world in its rate of child rejection due to impairments" (p. 32) These are all highly provocative and intrinsically compelling statements, but the first three statements are the views of one Israeli doctor or professor, and the final statement is based on the views of administrators in medical centers in two U.S. cities. No comparative statistics or data are given. What is solid in Weiss' book concerns the way in which imperfect bodies, bodies with physical disabilities, are marginalized. Her dissertation documented parents rejecting babies born with visible physical impairments, even those that could easily be fixed, but accepting babies with internal defects, even if they were life threatening. People with developmental disabilities are institutionalized in Israel, and the state does little to accommodate or integrate the handicapped, even handicapped veterans. Weiss argues that these bodies diverge from the Zionist image of the perfect man and are, therefore, shunned. Other topics are presented with a haphazard combination of poems, film, media coverage, or art that are not convincing because we are not given any sense of the overall context. Often, some information and data are presented in the short space of a few pages from which inappropriate, premature, and strong conclusions are drawn. I was riveted by Weiss' research in a national morgue and her statement that it maintains "different standards for Jews and non Jews, Palestinians and Israelis, soldiers and civilians" (p. 58) However, in a mere three paragraphs, she explained how male Jewish bodies are treated by the group that applies religious law concerning death and burial, and how soldiers' bodies are never tampered with or used for skin harvesting. It is left to the reader to infer how Palestinian bodies are treated, without any data on the subject. Weiss' book, quite ironically, employs a highly disembodied approach. The overall style of the book is dizzying as it zig zags through social time and space without an analytical framework. Weiss uses poetry but does not offer us the needed textual analysis. Finally, her italicized reflections are, for the most part, trivial. At the end of the book, she agonizes about how subversive her book is. Unfortunately, I was left wondering exactly what she is subverting and how, because a sufficient case has not been made. Weiss is not a native English speaker, so it is surprising that Stanford University Press left such a plethora of obvious problems uncorrected: direct translations from Hebrew (e.g. "open the radio" grammatical and spelling errors, awkward syntax and phrasing, and semantic redundancies. This book fails to tell us much about the chosen body that others have not done already. And it fails in its other goal, which is implicitly to underscore the disembodiment of the other in Israeli society Palestinians, Ethiopians, Sephardim, women, and gays.
