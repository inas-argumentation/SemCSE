Conception of Threat in Chinese Strategic Culture: Medicine as Analogy
This paper reexamines the studies of Chinese strategic culture as a conceptual alternative to mainstream international relations theories. Existing studies often adopt the positivist methodological approach to analyse Chinese strategic culture and treat it as an independent variable of cause/effect analysis. This paper argues that such an approach is itself culturally biased. And it fails to recognize the role of strategic culture as a conceptual map that determines what constitute threat in security deliberation. To have a deeper understanding of Chinese strategic culture, this paper uses medicine as an analogy to decipher the different conceptions of threat in Western and Chinese culture. This paper shows that Chinese medicine and Western biomedicine use different sets of vocabulary and conceptual schema in their diagnoses and responses to the threats to the human body. The holistic perspective in the Chinese notion of security is more tolerant to threats that are traditionally viewed in Western culture as something needs to be eliminated. The Chinese approach regards these threats as reflecting a deeper structural imbalance which is the real source of insecurity. This conceptual difference in the security of the human body metaphorically informs our conceptualisation of the world system, making Chinese and Western approaches to security bifurcate at a foundational level. In other words, adopting the Chinese cultural perspective on security would require us to ask a different set of questions in global governance than that informed by traditional positivist IR theories.
