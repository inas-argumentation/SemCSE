Book Review: Understanding Digital Culture
No. 145 November 2012 Introduction, and is in fact a major strength of the collection, as it reaches beyond the current fascination with Facebook, Twitter, Tumblr and the rest to provide historical context and conceptual breadth for students and researchers of current practices and phenomena. For example, individual chapters provide histories of some nowubiquitous terms like 'meme' 'trolling' and 'Web 2.0' Other chapters are seminal texts by the originators of such commonplace (and crucial) concepts: Henry Jenkins on Convergence; Chris Anderson on the Long Tail; Lawrence Lessig on the Creative Commons; Clay Shirky on the Social Surplus; Siva Vaidhyanathan on Open Source; danah boyd on 'always on lifestyles' Yochai Benkler on Shareable Goods; and Jay Rosen on the 'people formerly known as the audience' The collection seems to tilt towards an optimistic account of aspects of social media's capacity for collaborative and participatory activity. Certainly, a number of the authors are positive about the opportunities for democratisation of information and for creativity born of openness and sharing online. (The book is published under Creative Commons Attribution Noncommercial ShareAlike licence, and most of the individual chapters hold similar licences without the commercial restriction. The bullish approach, which foregrounds openness and participation, is matched against a blistering critique in a chapter by Felix Stalder, but generally optimism wins the day. There are some notable absentees, such as Manuel Castells and Howard Rheingold, and some topics left relatively unexplored, such as the place of social media in the lives of children and young people (see Sonia Livingstone and David Buckingham) social media research from outside of America and Europe (see Mizuko Ito, Larissa Hjorth and Sun Sun Lim) and especially such research in developing contexts (see Jason Abbott and Jonathan Donner) However, the collection on the whole is a useful assemblage of important and useful readings, recommended for consideration for inclusion in introductory courses or for interested researchers seeking a grounding in the field, with the essential coda that it be regarded as 'a' not 'the' social media reader.
