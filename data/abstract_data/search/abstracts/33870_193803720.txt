Reading across climate fiction, Trexler suggests that, while novelists are generally interested in the facts, 'scientific knowledge is discarded, ignored, exaggerated, reimagined, and counterinterpreted, for narrative, imaginative and political reasons' (60 61) But to look to fiction to educate about facts, or indeed to deliver any 'message' is of course to miss the point. Rather, Trexler argues (via the work of Bruno Latour) that fiction provides ways of exploring how knowledge is produced and how it is circulated throughout society. This often raises questions about types of response, varieties of prejudice, modes of denial and so on something particularly evident in those novels which are set in the present, rather than the future, as when the scientist character in Kingsolver's Flight Behaviour finds himself unable to communicate climate change properly to the media or the public. Trexler is in fact relatively unenthusiastic about Flight Behaviour, a novel which depicts the impact of climate change on a small Tennessee town caught in the midst of science, activism, the popular media and party politics. For him, the novel serves as an indicator that 'contemporary realism seems unable to imagine the political innovations or new ways of living that could address the challenges of the Anthropocene' (229) This argument artificially limits how fiction portrays 'new ways of living' Of course Kim Stanley Robinson's SF is more likely to articulate new forms of social and political structure but, as we see in the hard and bitter agony of Kingsolver's symbolic and visceral final set piece (which involves the birth of a premature lamb) there are multiple ways that literature might explore new ways of relating to and imagining the Anthropocene's changing ecologies, and perhaps the symbolic does something that the speculative might not. It would be interesting to see how Trexler's discussion of Anthropocene realism would fit with the fiction (and poetry) of someone like Ben Lerner whose novel 10:04 very much about 'a time of climate change' was published too recently to have been included in Anthropocene Fictions. Trexler highlights a tension between the present state of the Anthropocene and fictions which project themselves into a (near or distant) future of climate change. He is right that accounts of the latter are crucial to our sense of the former but his tendency to focus on stories of climate change to come, risks detracting from a more nuanced view of the Anthropocene, which has a past as well as a present and future. Nevertheless, this book is perhaps the most comprehensive study of climate change in fiction to date. Due to its range and its willingness to ask how the idea of climate change might transform how fiction is written and read, it may well be an important reference point for some time to come.
