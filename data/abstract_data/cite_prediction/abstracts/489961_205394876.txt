Frontal pole function: what is specifically human?
The frontal pole cortex (Brodmann’s area 10) is the prefrontal region that has evolved the most in humans [1]. This region in humans includes a medial and lateral part, often referred to as the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and the lateral frontopolar cortex (lFPC), respectively. In monkeys, anatomical studies [2] suggest that the frontal pole cortex corresponds with the human vmPFC only. According to neuroimaging studies, the human vmPFC subserves the monitoring of outcomes expected from the ongoing course of action [3–6]. In their recent article in TiCS, Tsujimoto et al. [7] review the first electrophysiological study of neuronal activity in the frontal pole of monkeys [8], showing that the function of frontal pole neurons is to monitor action outcomes. Thus, the monkey frontal pole cortex further appears to be the functional analog of the human vmPFC. In the study reviewed by Tsujimoto et al., the frontal pole neurons in themonkeys were selective for the two exclusive task sets that the monkeys had to select alternatively, namely ‘make a left versus right movement in response to the go signal’. However, these neurons responded neither to the external cues instructing themonkeys toswitchbetween the two task sets nor to the go signals. They responded only after an action according to the outcome predicted by each task set, given external cues. Moreover, their activity anticipated outcome occurrences and was independent of outcome values. The authors interpreted these findings as reflecting the encoding and retrospective monitoring of chosen goals. I suggest another possible interpretation, that these neurons implemented forward, internal models associated with every task set. Such models learn action outcomes associated with every task set according to stimuli. Computational models [9] show that such forward models are required to make appropriate inferences about task set reliability across successive trials and especially to decide whether it is reliable to stay with the same task set or to switch to another (probably in other prefrontal regions). Overall, these findings and interpretations aremore consistent with vmPFC than lFPC activations observed in human neuroimaging studies [3–6].
