COMPUTING IN SCHOOLS: Computer science, computational thinking, programming, coding: the anomalies of transitivity in K-12 computer science education
OPINION I n the last ten years, computer science (CS), as seen from the viewpoint of K–12 CS education (CSE), has undergone two extraction processes. The first was a rationalized extraction, stemming from a meaningful view of CS. The second was probably inspired by computing professionals. For the sake of accuracy, the roots of the second are more than ten years old, but in the last few years, it cannot be ignored—it is everywhere. Although both extract CS to a relatively small core, they constitute two different cores. One is a concentrated essence of CS. The other presents a very dilute extraction of CS, hovering over the margins of CS, its circumference. Is this a contradiction, a dilute extraction? It is. Indeed, the second extraction is not really an extraction. Supposedly, it depicts more of a reduced version of CS, just a pale image of it, deem-phasizing the challenges and the thinking patterns. Hence, these two extractions seem to constitute two opposite extremes. Can you guess what these two extractions are? For now, I will postpone revealing the second one. The first originated ten years ago, in 2006. It is the extraction of CS that yields Jeannette Wing's computational thinking [10]. In her widely cited and influential viewpoint article Wing argued that CS is characterized by several thinking patterns or habits of mind, or in Wing's words, mental tools. She did not define computational thinking (CT), but rather, exemplified it by providing a long list of mental tools. These include efficiency, approximation, randomization, and using reduction, embedding, transformation or simulation for translating a difficult problem into one whose solution is already known, as well as recursion, concurrency, using abstraction or decomposition as tools for handling a complex problem, trade-off, nondeterminism, and many others. Wing called for teaching CT in every school and to every student, based on her argument that this set of mental tools is important for everyone and should be a part of everyone's basic education , not just that of computer scientists. Her rationale was that these tools are used for problem solving, with problems that come from various domains outside of CS, and solutions that are not necessarily intended to be executed by computers.
