Learners do not appear, for example, to understand two of the most robust effects from the cognitive psychology literature—namely, the testing effect (that practicing retrieval leads to better long-term retention, compared even to re-reading; e.g., Roediger & Karpicke, 2006a) and the spacing effect (that spacing repeated study sessions leads to better long-term retention than does massing repetitions; e.g., Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, & Rohrer, 2006; Dempster, 1988). A survey of 472 undergraduate students by Kornell and Bjork (2007)—which was replicated by Hartwig and Dunlosky (2012)—showed that students underappreciate the learning benefits of testing. Similarly, Karpicke, Butler, and Roediger (2009) surveyed students’ study strategies and found that re-reading was by far the most popular study strategy and that self-testing tended to be used only to assess whether some level of learning had been achieved, not to enhance subsequent recall. Even when students have some appreciation of effective strategies they often do not implement those strategies.
