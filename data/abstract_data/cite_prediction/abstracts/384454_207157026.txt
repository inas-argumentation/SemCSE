Spatial Multiplexing in Fiber Optics: The 10X Scaling of Metro/Core Capacities
Peter J. Winzer Demand and Supply of Fiber Capacity T he amount of information created, transported, and replicated annually not only by individuals, but increasingly by machines through distributed computing, sensor networks, and cloud-based services has long surpassed the Zettabyte mark, with growth rates between 40% and 50% per year [1]. Increasingly this growth will be also be driven by machines, connecting and exchanging data through distributed computing, or by sensor networks connecting to cloud-based services, and, in turn, to the global array of people that want to consume these services. Fortunately, micro-processing capabilities are growing at around 80% annually [2], which allows processing supply to stay just ahead of demand. This also requires proportional growth in computer interface rates as reflected by Amdahl's rule of thumb [3]. As the very essence of information is to be shared after proper processing that maximizes its value to the end user, it is not surprising to see bandwidth demands across all network segments exhibiting high growth rates as well, from local-area and access networks to interand intra-datacenter networks and to metro and core networks. It is also interesting to note the slight downward correction of traffic growth rates that has been observed in the past few years [6–8]. With typical traffic growth rates in the 20% to 60% range, Table II examines these growth rates in terms of the evolution of networking technologies. The first 100G interfaces and the first 10-Tb/s WDM systems were commercially available in 2010 [9, 10], which makes that year the time reference for cutting-edge market introduction. Today, the industry is about to cross the Terabit interface hurdle [11] “just in time” for providers experiencing traffic growth rates as high as 60%. On the other hand, today's commercial WDM systems typically support 20 Tb/s and are therefore starting to lack the required total capacity (50 Tb/s). This paper examines technology options that will allow optical interface rates and network capacities to scale to meet the challenging requirements of the decade ahead.
