The Challenges of Research in African Business Schools
Unlike their leading Western counterparts, which have recently been criticized for placing too much emphasis on research to the detriment of teaching (Bennis & O’Toole, 2005; Birnik & Billsberry, 2007; Starkey & Tempest, 2005), African business schools still allocate enough of their scarce resources to infrastructure, program design, development, and delivery in order to meet the needs of a rapidly growing business education market. In most schools, research in all its aspects is poorly funded and remains a marginal activity when it is not totally disregarded. This is especially true in schools in which the main goal is to realize profits. As a result, both the quantity and quality of the research output in most African business schools are below international standards. There is a definite lack of African-based theories in business education in Africa. Textbooks and other didactic materials used in African business schools are generally imported either from Europe or North America and contain theories that have not been externally validated and practices often unproven in the African context. There is, however, a general consensus among leading African business school administrators and key stakeholders that more should be done to teach what is relevant to Africa and to develop new knowledge for the understanding and advancement of business practices in Africa. For any significant progress to occur in these two areas, research is pivotal. Accordingly, the Association of African Business Schools (AABS) has set the minimum intellectual output that is expected from full-time faculty in member schools in either one of the following three types of scholarship: discipline-based scholarship, contributions to practice, and learning and pedagogical scholarship. As AABS member schools strive to differentiate themselves from a growing number of competitors in their local markets, these publication expectations will likely increase, at least in terms of quality requirements. This is because academic research positively influences the long-term perceptions of various key business school stakeholders, including academics, business recruiters, and students (Mitra &Golder, 2008). In addition, leading African business schools must raise their scholarship profile and obtain international accreditations such as AACSB and EQUIS in order to compete effectively with foreign schools. Two major benefits are expected from such a move: a) the ability to offer local competitive alternatives to both undergraduate and graduate students who are able to afford higher tuition fees and might consider education abroad, and b) the ability to secure higher market shares in the most lucrative local and regional segments of MBAs and other executive management training programs that also attract foreign providers. African business schools are working on various fronts to develop and implement institutional changes that can stimulate and support research development, including improving the qualifications of academic staff members, creating their own publication outlets, developing Ph.D. programs and other professional development activities, and reducing teaching loads. Even so, they face common challenges that may dilute some of these efforts. This editorial identifies and discusses overload teaching, consulting and consultancy disease, and administrative and political appointments as three of such challenges. This editorial also raises issues associated with the creation of publication outlets in African business schools and the adoption of imported quality criteria to assess research output in Africa. Finally, the editorial ends with a few concluding remarks.
