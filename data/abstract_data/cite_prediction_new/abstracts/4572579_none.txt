The Contribution of Mergers to Concentration Growth: A Reply to Professor Hart [On Bias and Concentration]
THE work on long-run changes in industrial concentration inaugurated by the classic I956 article of Professors Hart and Prais [6] and continued by theml and by others has reached generally agreed conclusions about the direction and magnitude of change. Concentration rose in the first three decades of the century, was stagnant or declined in the following two decades and began to rise again in the I950S and I96os. Professor Hart [5] is correct in pointing to a number of areas of real agreement, and we take this opportunity to reiterate our faith in these basic conclusions. We find some recent selective and polemical 'refutations'2 of their results wholly unpersuasive. We attempted [4] to decompose the growth of concentration into two elements; we measured the contribution of merger and attributed the rest to internal growth. Internal growth, in turn, can itself be sub-divided into two components. Large firms may grow, on average, more rapidly than small. If they do, then concentration will increase through what we might call 'differential'growth'. But even if firms of all sizes have the same expectation of growth, concentration will still increase because there is some dispersion of realized growth rates about the average. This we called the 'Gibrat effect': internal growth is the sum of this and any differential growth. If, as is the case for the period I957-69, the likely magnitude of the Gibrat effect is greater than the total contribution of internal growth to concentration increase, it would follow that the contribution of differential internal growth to concentration increase was actually negative. In other words, if we exclude the effect of merger, large firms would on average have grown more slowly than small firms. These points are illustrated in Table I.
