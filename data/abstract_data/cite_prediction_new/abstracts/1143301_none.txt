Among them are CRF, serotonin, nerve growth factor, myosin light chain kinase, substance P, calcitonin generelated peptide, and mast cells.8 Water avoidance stress (WAS) and restraint, which are frequently used as models of acute psychological/physical stress, induce rapid CRF gene transcription in the parvocellular region of the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus associated with activation of the hypothalamic– pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis.5 Acute or chronic partial restraint stress facilitates visceral sensitivity to rectal distension in rats.9 An initial study by Gué et al.10 showed that intracerebroventricular (icv) CRF administration mimicked partial restraint-induced visceral hyperalgesia to rectal distension in rats, and that the effect of stress was blocked by icv injection of α-helical CRF9-41, a nonselective CRF antagonist.10 Million et al.11 reported that peripheral administration of a selective CRF1 receptor antagonist, NMI-35965, prevented WAS-induced hyperalgesia to colorectal distension in adult rats exposed to neonatal maternal separation, which is associated with higher brain CRF levels in adults.6 The findings of these pharmacological studies suggest that CRF/CRF1 receptor is involved in visceral hyperalgesia. A growing body of evidence about other proposed molecules responsible for hyperalgesia in IBS models has led to the following putative mechanisms underlying stress-induced increases in visceral sensitivity to colon distension: Stress-induced release of CRF activates the HPA axis and mast cells to release several chemical mediators (tryptase, serotonin), cytokines, and CRF. One of the important characteristics of CRF receptor subtypes is their distinct affinity for mammalian CRF family ligands. CRF has higher affinity (10to 40-fold) for CRF1 than for CRF2 receptor.
