My MI; here or there, I get great care: is tertiary-care hospital acute myocardial infarction management really better?
Cardiovascular specialists are often judged by the places they are from. When we are introduced to each other, almost invariably the first question asked is, “In which hospital do you work?” The answer to this question provides an immediate reference frame with respect to whether the individual works in a tertiary care institution, with an admirable and well-known reputation for research and clinical excellence, or whether he or she works in a nontertiary care environment, where the clinical expertise of the institution may be less documented and therefore less well known. However, with the ever-wider dissemination of fellowship-trained cardiologists in Canada, are these arguably arbitrary credentials still relevant to patient care outcomes? Clinicians in all disciplines pride themselves on the provision of excellent patient care. This is certainly the rule within cardiovascular medicine, but perhaps even more so given the fact that mortality and event-free patient survival are frequently the arbitrators of excellence by which we are all judged and compared. The use of major adverse cardiac events has allowed practitioners to compare themselves not only to their institutional colleagues but also to regional, national, and even international benchmarks. This careful examination and comparison of institutional outcomes is critical to the ongoing evolution of the knowledge translation cycles that help to improve patient care outcomes. Over the past 3 decades, cardiology communities around the world have generally come to consensus regarding the use of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or thrombolytic therapy (reflected in evidence-informed clinical practice guidelines) as the best means to improve outcomes in patients experiencing ST-segment elevated myocardial infarction (STEMI).
