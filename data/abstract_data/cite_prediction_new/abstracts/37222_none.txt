Neuroleptics in learning disability. Their use is sometimes justified.
ED1TOR,-J N Brown and A C Crosby describe a case of acute cervical cord compression in which plain radiographs were apparently normal.' Unfortunately these are not shown but instead there is a single sagittal T2 weighted slice from a magnetic resonance scan. In addition to the disc prolapse described, this shows very prominent chronic bony degenerative changes at the level of the C6-C7 disc, particularly posteriorly. I find it hard to believe that these were not visible on the original radiographs, thus changing the basic premise of this article. Acute cord injury in the presence of pre-existing degenerative change is of course a well recognised syndrome, particularly in elderly patients, and was described in a previous Lesson ofthe Week.23 The magnetic resonance scan illustrated is described as showing "acute prolapsed disc with 50% encroachment on the spinal cord," but the cord is not well seen on this image and in fact the 50% encroachment seems to be on the spinal canal. The authors go on to discuss computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, but no mention is made of myelography, combined when necessary with computed tomography. This still has a definite place in the investigation of such patients and is more likely than magnetic resonance to be easily available, particularly in the acute situation. This case is certainly of interest, but perhaps some radiological advice at all stages would have been advisable.
