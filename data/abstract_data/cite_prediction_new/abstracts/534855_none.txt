Postimatinib therapy emergence of a new JAK2V617F clone and subsequent development of overt polycythemia vera in a patient with chronic myelogenous leukaemia
To the Editor: A 67-year-old man was diagnosed with chronic-phase BCR-ABL1-positive chronic myelogenous leukaemia (CML) in December 1999 (FISH for BCR-ABL1 showed 98.2% fusion signal). Quantitative JAK2V617F mutation analysis (0.5% assay sensitivity) of archived bone marrow (BM), collected at the time of diagnosis, yielded negative results. The patient initially failed treatment with interferon-a, cytosine arabinoside and hydroxyurea but was later (December 2002) induced into partial FISH remission (17.2% fusion signal) by imatinib therapy (400 mg ⁄day). JAK2V617F mutation was still not detected at that time. In November 2003, FISH revealed 0% BCR-ABL1, but JAK2V617F was detected, for the first time, at 1% allele burden. There was no clinical evidence of polycythemia vera (PV) at that time. The patient continued to be in complete FISH remission of his CML in November 2004 and April 2008 during which time the JAK2V617F allele burden increased to 2% and 8%, respectively. In February 2007, a slight increase in platelet count was noted, but other blood counts were stable on imatinib: hemoglobin 15.4 g ⁄dL, white blood cell count (WBC) 6.1 · 10 ⁄L and platelet count 454 · 10 ⁄L. In November 2009, the patient experienced a minor stroke, and digital ischemia and blood counts around that time were: hemoglobin 16 g ⁄dL, WBC 9.8 · 10 ⁄L and platelet count 647 · 10 ⁄L. BM collected in March 2010 showed 0% BCR-ABL1 (by FISH analysis of 6000 nuclei) and 6% JAK2V617F allele burden: hemoglobin 17.2 g ⁄dL, WBC 11.9 · 10 ⁄L and platelet count 507 · 10 ⁄L. BM morphology was consistent with the clinical diagnosis of PV. The development of CML or the acquisition of BCR-ABL1 in patients with a previous history of PV (1–5), essential thrombocythemia (ET) (5) or primary myelofibrosis (6) has previously been published. Similarly, the concurrent occurrence of JAK2V617F-positive myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) and BCR-ABL1positive CML has been extensively reported1, (2, 4, 5, 7–13). In both instances, the BCR-ABL1 clone often displayed clonal dominance, and effective treatment of CML with imatinib often unmasked overt clinical manifestation of the JAK2V617F-positive disease (1, 13). The particular phenomenon suggested a pre-existing JAK2V617F clone giving rise to a BCR-ABL1 subclone, and this possibility was demonstrated in some patients whose myeloid colonies displayed JAK2V617F alone or together with BCR-ABL1 (2, 11). However, other cases of concurrent BCR-ABL1 and JAK2V617F have shown disappearance of both mutations during imatinib therapy, suggesting that JAK2V617F can occur as a subclone of BCR-ABL1 (8). A BCR-ABL1 clone has also been reported in JAK2V617F-positive refractory anemia with ring sideroblasts and thrombocytosis (RARS-T) (9), which, combined with the relatively high incidence of JAK2V617F in RARS-T, supports the alignment of RARS-T more with MPN (14) than with MDS (15). Our case is different from previous reports in that both the JAK2V617F clone and clinically overt PV developed after years of treatment for CML. Similar to what was observed in the above-mentioned studies, imatinibinduced suppression of the BCR-ABL1 clone in our patient might have facilitated JAK2V617F-associated clonal expansion. The particular scenario is consistent with the occurrence of mutually exclusive BCR-ABL1 and JAK2V617F clones that originated either from a common ancestral clone (or oligoclones) or independently arose from a polyclonal, but disease vulnerable (16, 17), stem cell pool (18–21). Either way, these observations validate Dameshek’s insight (22) regarding the pathogenesis of MPN and illustrate the complexity of clonal structure in MPN and the unpredictable nature of clonal evolution. The evolving concept of disease susceptibility and oligoclonal stem cell proliferation in MPN is relevant to both the prospect of targeted therapy and the feasibility of cure in these diseases.
