Making trials work in practice: please mind the gap.
Clinical decision making in cancer care is a complex process in which multiple patient-specific factors (eg, histopathology, staging information, comorbidity, patient wishes) are referenced against prior clinical experience to formulate a management plan that is considered most likely to result in the optimal overall outcome for the individual patient. Prior clinical experience is most reliably derived from phase 3 clinical trials, which have addressed the specific question of how best to treat patients with a given problem. However, we know that only a minority of cancer patients are enrolled in clinical trials (eg, 12% in the United Kingdom [1]). Although there are multiple reasons why patients do not enter trials, many fail to meet eligibility criteria, which can be stringent [2]. Thus the population who enter the trial is never truly representative of the entire population in need of treatment, and it is possible that the overall value of that intervention in the ‘‘real world’’ differs from the value seen in the trial. In this month’s issue of European Urology, Zargar and colleagues [3] report a very large, multicentre case series of patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by cystectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC). They conclude that real-world experience falls short of that gained in the pivotal trial that established this treatment as standard of care for patients newly diagnosed with MIBC [4]. In the trial, patients who had no pathologic evidence of cancer in the resected bladder had better overall survival than patients who had residual cancer at cystectomy. This survival advantage appeared to be maintained in patients who had a pathologic complete response (pCR) as a result of chemotherapy. Thus the proportion of patients experiencing pCR to chemotherapy is likely to be an important surrogate of clinical benefit in this context. In the series presented by Zargar et al, the pCR rate was 23% overall compared with 38% in the chemotherapy arm of the pivotal trial, and median overall survival was 5.8 yr compared with 6.4 yr in the pivotal trial. It is not unusual to find real-world outcomes that are less impressive than those seen in clinical trials. For example, survival times after initiating sunitinib as palliative treatment for advanced renal cell carcinoma were 14.4 mo in the ‘‘real world’’ compared with 26.4 mo in the pivotal trial [5,6]. This nonconcordance of effectiveness between trials and clinical practice may have important consequences. For example, it is likely that cost-effectiveness estimates derived from trial data are incorrect, and this may result in failure to prioritise scarce resources effectively. Moreover, some patients may not receive the optimal therapy for their specific circumstances. In the case of neoadjuvant chemotherapy for MIBC, it is possible that ineffective chemotherapy may delay or even prohibit radical cystectomy, compromising cure. The series by Zargar et al [3] offers some reassurance in this regard, as overall survival in the series is similar to that seen in the chemotherapy arms of the two key randomised trials and the pT0 rate is higher than that expected after transurethral resection of the bladder alone [4,7]. There are several possible explanations for the nonconcordance between real-world and clinical trial outcomes. First, the treatment regimens used in the real world often differ from those used in clinical trials. These may be differences in drugs, doses, and/or schedules. In the series by Zargar et al [3], 80% of patients received a drug combination that differed from those proven in clinical trials (65% receiving gemcitabine plus cisplatin [GC]), and16% did not receive cisplatin, which is widely considered to be the most active drug in bladder cancer [8].
