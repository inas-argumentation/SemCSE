Diverse effects in Friedreich's ataxia place PGC‐1α center‐stage
Friedreich ataxia (FRDA) is an autosomal recessive disorder that affects 1 in 50,000 Caucasians and is the most common of the early-onset hereditary ataxias (1). FRDA is caused by GAA triplet repeat expansion within the first intron of the frataxin gene (FXN ) resulting in profoundly reduced frataxin levels. This reduction in frataxin is associated with sensory neuronopathy and loss of sensory fibers in peripheral nerves and degeneration of posterior columns in the spinal cord. In addition to the progressive neurological disabilities, FRDA causes a hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (45–63%) and an increased risk of diabetes mellitus (14–19%). Skeletal muscle is only subclinically involved in FRDA. As in most neurodegenerative diseases no treatment is available to delay disease onset or reduce disease progression. Frataxin is a small, highly conserved and essential protein localized to mitochondria and involved in iron binding and iron metabolism (2). This likely explains its role in the prevention of oxidative stress. However, the exact mechanism that leads to neurodegeneration and metabolic disturbances in FRDA patients is unknown. A recent study by Coppola et al. provides evidence for a close link between frataxin deficiency and dysregulation of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ ) pathway and the transcriptional activity of PPARγ coactivator PGC-1α (3). PGC-1α belongs to a family of transcriptional coactivators that respond to a variety of environmental stimuli regulating metabolic and biological processes such as glucose, lipid and energy homeostasis in a tissue-specific manner (4). The authors suggest PGC-1α dysfunction in FRDA on the basis of changes in gene expression in heart and skeletal muscle from frataxin-deficient mice. These mice express 30–35% of normal frataxin and represent mildly affected FRDA patients showing no overt clinical abnormalities.
