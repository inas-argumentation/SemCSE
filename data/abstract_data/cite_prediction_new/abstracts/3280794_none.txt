Making Demography Astonishing: Lessons in the Politics of Population Science
In recent years, I have set aside the study of China’s population politics, which preoccupied me for some three decades, to work on other things. Now, however, I find myself pulled into a rather ugly, yet also revealing, dispute over China unfolding in the pages of this journal. Respectful debate of differing perspectives is always welcome, but the recent article in Demography (and a 2015 piece by the same author in Population Studies) are something else altogether. In language that both ridicules and impugns the motives of those he sets up as his opponents, the author seeks to undermine not just the arguments, but also the credibility of those with whom he disagrees. Indeed, he charges them with doing unsound science in the interest of policy advocacy, while implying that he alone conducts pure science outside of politics. Instead of seriously reading and engaging with the data and ideas, he misrepresents and distorts the work of scholars who have devoted their careers to understanding China’s complex population politics. More generally, his work lacks understanding of the complex social, cultural, and political dynamics of contemporary China. I am surprised and dismayed that the Editors of this journal did not seek the reactions of those targeted for such dismissive treatment before publishing the article. The printing of this kind of work raises important issues for the journal as well as for the field of population studies. What counts as sound population research? What kind of knowledge base gives someone the authority to present him/herself as an expert on a particular country? What constitutes responsible and ethical publication practice in demography? Most broadly, does population science lie outside politics, as the author claims, or is it inside politics, and what are the implications of the two locations?
