Recording industries, technologies and cultures in flux
Over the last decade and a half, digital technologies have upended many basic assumptions of how recording industries function. From the ground up, mp3s spreading across the internet and web occasioned vast changes in the production, promotion, circulation, distribution and retailing of recordings, resulting in a very different landscape than that of their late 1990s emergence. After ceding much of their economic power to Apple through iTunes Store licenses, the United States’ biggest recording companies reacted to the unceasing circulation of mp3s by having the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) file exorbitant lawsuits against peer-to-peer downloaders, and, via Digital Rights Management (DRM) attempting to enforce copyright laws through technologies themselves (Gillespie 2007), resulting in not much more than lost consumer goodwill. Over the past several years, record labels and technology companies have engineered a ‘topdown’ response to what Jonathan Sterne called the mp3 era’s ‘transnational swap-meet for recordings’, (Sterne 2012) licensing their music to streaming services like Pandora, Spotify and Apple Music and counting as the industry’s biggest technological initiative since introducing the compact disc. All the while, however, artists, labels, retailers and listeners themselves industriously (so to speak) have wrangled a wide range of digital technologies for their own creative purposes, using inexpensive digital software for production, free online platforms from MySpace to SoundCloud and YouTube for distribution, social media for promotion, and sites such as Kickstarter and Bandcamp for funding and retailing. While these transformations are profound, the ‘record business’ has been here before, of course. Over the past century-plus, various industries involved with recordings have been marked by a constant state of technological flux, in response to their own wants and needs as well as changes along the cultural circuit. Amid a variety of other socio-cultural dynamics, changing industrial approaches have commonly been associated with technological formats, including disks (78 rpm records, 33 LPs, 45 rpm singles, compact discs) and tapes (reel-to-reel, 8-track, analog and digital cassettes), along with a variety of now largely forgotten technologies (wire recording, anyone?). The nascent industry came close to failure as early as 1890, when phonograph companies were coming to terms with the technology’s failure as a business tool. This was before the emerging technology was recontextualized as public amusement via nickel-in-the-slot phonographs (Hatcher and Wile 1974). During the Great Depression, pundits declared the fall of the phonograph in the face of radio, with little or no sense that the emerging technology would be instrumental in reviving recording companies (Yorke 1932).
