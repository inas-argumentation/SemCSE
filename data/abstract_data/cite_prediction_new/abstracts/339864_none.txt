Molecular Modeling of the mGluR1 Metabotropic Glutamate Receptor Transmembrane Domain and Construction of the Model of Its Dimer
Metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR1) discovered a little more than a decade ago belong to the subclass of G-protein-conjugated glutamate receptors, which comprises, to date, eight subtypes (mGluR1– mGluR8). The complete structure of an mGluR receptor comprises an N-terminal domain (NTD) involved in binding competitive antagonists and agonists; a cysteine-rich domain; a transmembrane domain (TMD) containing a binding site for noncompetitive antagonists, which are more selective and effective than competitive ones; and a C-terminal domain, whose size varies in different subtypes. Only the structures of the mGluR1 NTD [1] and its complexes with the agonist [1, 2] and antagonists [2] have been determined experimentally. The purpose of this study was to construct the spatial structural models of the mGluR1 TMD and its dimer, as well as the modeling of the noncompetitiveantagonist binding site. Many methods have been developed for determining the boundaries of transmembrane regions. For amino acid alignment (Fig. 1), we used data obtained by the Memsat2, TMHMM, and HMMTOP methods. To model more accurately amino acid residues exposed at the outer surface, we used the results of the evolutionary-trace analysis of more than 700 amino acid sequences of the TMDs of G-protein-conjugated receptors [3], because a shift by only one position during amino acid alignment is equivalent to the turn of each α helix about its axis, which leads to incorrect identification of amino acids involved in ligand binding and interaction with neighboring α helices. When aligning amino acids, we also took into account the positions of loop regions I2, E2, and E3. The variation in the sizes of these regions in different subtypes of mGluR receptors is two, seven, and seven amino acid residues, respectively. After the alignment of the amino acid sequences of the mGluR1 TMD (code Q13255 in the SWISS-PROT database [4]) and the inactive form of rhodopsin [5] (code 1L9H in the Brookhaven Database [6]; spatial resolution, 2.6 Å; code P02699 in the SWISS-PROT database [4]), we used its results (Fig. 1) to construct the spatial structure of the TMD using the Sybyl 6.7.2 software [7] (the structural identity of different classes of G-protein-conjugated receptors remains an open question), optimized the geometry of the resulting model using the steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods, as well as molecular dynamic (MD) calculations (with a total duration of 1 ns). We used the modeled annealing method to improve the package of the side chains of amino acids using different variants of limitations imposed on different subsystems of atoms. When constructing the model, we also changed the bending of α helices, which occur at the positions of proline and glycine residues in rhodopsin, and varied the mutual positions of α helices.
