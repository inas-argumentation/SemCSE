The role of transesophageal echocardiography in patients with cardiac trauma.
n this issue of Anesthesia and Analgesia, Porembka and colleagues report a case in which an aorta-toI right-ventricle fistula occurring after a stab wound was missed at surgery and by transthoracic echocardiogram but was found with transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) (1). The authors recommend that Doppler echocardiography be performed ”as soon as clinically appropriate” in the postoperative course of such patients. Noting that there is “an increased awareness” of the value of TEE in critically ill patients, they seek to encourage further this trend. Indeed, I believe that this is a laudable goal. The author’s experience is not unusual. Patients admitted with cardiac trauma, who by virtue of their lifethreatening condition often undergo the briefest of preoperative evaluations, may have lesions missed even at surgery. Other authors have reported a ventricular septa1 defect discovered postoperatively only by TEE in a patient with multiple gunshot wounds to the heart (2). In a series of 204 patients presenting with cardiac injury (54% stab wounds, 41% gunshot wounds, 4% blunt trauma), only 128 left the hospital (3); 90 underwent operative cardiac repair, and, of these, 40 had suspected residual lesions. Eight went on to reoperation. Late complications after surgery for cardiac trauma are also well known as a result of retained bullet fragments and lesions missed at the time of initial presentation and treatment (4). Echocardiographic findings in penetrating trauma commonly include pericardial effusion, abnormal chamber enlargement, intracardiac thrombus, foreign bodies, abnormal wall motion, valvular abnormalities, and intracardiac shunts.
