Why the African renaissance is likely to fail: the case of Zimbabwe
This paper uses Zimbabwe as a case study to put forward the view that African economic and state failure can be linked to the ancient state cultures of the continent. The central thesis is that the postcolonial Zimbabwean state elite was embedded in the precolonial Shona state culture, and used it to reorganise the inherited Rhodesian state institutions and build a non-developmental state that catapulted the country into economic decline. It is in such situations that the presence of an indigenous non-developmental state is recognised. Using the Zimbabwean experience, the approach seeks to establish three things: (i) the kind of state that the precolonial state culture generated; (ii) the continuity or discontinuity of such indigenous state culture in the light of the colonialism that visited the precolonial Zimbabweans; and (iii) the kind of postcolonial state that emerged after independence. The paper also summarises an emerging African renaissance argument by a number of prominent pan-African scholars.
