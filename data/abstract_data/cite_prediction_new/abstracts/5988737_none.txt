Are there specific histologic abnormalities of skin in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis?
I read with great interest the paper by Ono et al. (1) on the “Delayed return phenomenon” (DRP) in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). The authors emphasize that the skin of ALS patients “feels supple, like tanned leather, ,and loses elasticity”, and,. when pulled out, it returns only sluggishly to the original position; this property of skin, seen only after 2.5 years from the onset of symptoms and mainly in the upper extremities, has been termed DRP. To clarify the relationship between DRP and its histologic findings, the authors also studied the reticular dermis in ALS patients and controls with or without DRP, respectively. They found in ALS patients with DRP, and not in controls with or without DRP: a) on light microscopy, collagen bundles reduced in amount, small and faintly stained, more loosely woven, and separated by wide spaces; b) on electron microscopy, a markedly large amount of amorphous material positive for ruthenium red, widely separating fragmented collagen fibrils and bundles. Ono et al. (1) wonder that “though it is thought skin symptoms, such as DRP and the absence of bedsore formation, even in the terminal stages of ALS, is characteristic of this disease, little attention has been paid to DRP”. Likewise they state that only two light microscopy studies concerning the skin in ALS have been previously carried out (2, 3). It is my duty to correct this statement. In 1966, Cerimele and I (4) also studied skin in ALS. Moreover, our study was preceded by the report of Winkelmann in 1963 (5). Our study (4) is briefly reviewed, as there is disagreement about skin biopsy results from ALS patients. We examined 10 ALS patients, from 38-64 years old, whose illness persisted from 2 months-3 years. Skin specimens were obtained by the use of a 6-mm key punch, after local anesthesia with 1 % novocain, from the abdomen (lower quadrant) and the extensor region of the hand (in the latter case, the skin overlying atrophied muscles). The specimens were fixed with 10 % formalin, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned 6 p thick. Several sections from each biopsy were stained with hematoxylin-eosin, elastica van Gieson, silver impregnation, alcian blue, colloidal iron-Mowry, Weigert, acid-orcein-Unna, paraldheide-fuchsin-Gomori, peracetic acid-aldheide fuchsin-Halmi, and Mowry-orcein-Kernechtrot’s methods. The specimens from abdominal skin did not show significant structural abnormalities throughout the dermis, except for a slightly increased amount of mucopolysaccharide in the superficial dermis. In the skin specimens obtained from the extensor region of the hand we found as follows: a) the epidermis was normal in all specimens examined; b) in 7 patients, a slightly increased amount of mucopolysaccharide in superficial dermis was observed; in these same patients, no significant abnormalities of collagen bundles and elastic fibers were seen; c) on the contrary, in 3 patients (age, 59,63 and 64 years; duration of illness, 3 years, 2 months and 2 years, respectively; all being farmers) a markedly increased amount of mucopolysaccharide and moderately fragmented collagen fibers were found in the middle dermis; in addition, severe morphologic and structural abnormalities of elastic fibers were seen in both superficial and middle dermis. With all proper reserves (we studied only 10 ALS patients and examined their skin only with light microscopy), in our ALS patients we found no evidence of the histologic findings seen in the dermis of ALS patients with DRP by Ono et al. (1) and by them considered a specific abnormality of ALS. Moreover, our findings also disagree with skin changes observed with the peracetic acid-aldheide fuchsin-Halmi staining in about 60% of ALS patients by Fullmer et al. (3), such as a distinctive elastosis, increase of mucopolysaccharide, degeneration and elastosis of errector pili muscle, focal areas of regeneration of connective tissue, and alteration of collagen.
