Dichoptic Reaction Times Open a New Window on Functional Loss of Vision.
Subjects presenting with loss of vision usually show objective corollaries. However, in some instances, visual loss not only lacks objective corollaries but also presents features that are incompatible with current pathophysiological concepts. Although long labeled “hysterical,” “psychogenic,” “nonorganic,” or the like, there is little evidence for causal associations with psychological stress or psychiatric disease. The neutral designation “functional loss of vision” (FLV) will be used here. Diagnosis of FLV is often problematic. The prominent fatigability and suggestibility features of many affected subjects argue against lengthy threshold assessments and tests whose outcomes may be susceptible to examiner influences. Capitalizing on the olden clinical impression that covert switching of the laterality of stimulation may be informative, the present study focused on unbiased dichoptic measurements of reaction times to strongly supraliminal targets. Subjects were recruited from the author’s neuro-ophthalmologic consultation service. Informed consent was obtained. All examinations adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the local institutional review board. The test group was formed by 12 subjects diagnosed with FLV on the basis of a lack of objective signs in full neuroophthalmologic examinations in combination with abnormal variations in space and time in examinations at the tangent screen and in repeated automated perimetry. Two control groups involved 15 subjects each: one composed of normal subjects and another composed of subjects with various visual pathway lesions. Demographic details are presented in Table 1 (available at www.aaojournal.org). Commercial digital video eyewear (iWear VR920, Vuzix Corporation, Rochester, NY) was programmed to present a white square subtending 4 4 centered on a 27 20 black background at an apparent viewing distance of 0.7 m. A black cross-shaped test target (line width 0.05 3 ) was presented in binocular or left or right monocular views, in random order, under software control. The test task was to click the computer mouse whenever a test target was seen. Presentations were spaced by randomly selected intervals in the 2to 4-second range. Reaction times and mouse click durations were recorded to the nearest 10 ms. Test duration was 1 or 2 minutes, depending on individual tolerance. An anaglyphic adaptation can be accessed at www.oft.gu.se/webdiagnos/flv/flv.html. The 2 control groups presented closely similar reaction times, of the order of 0.4 second (Fig 1, Table 1, available at www.aaojournal.org). To avoid possible statistical dependencies between eyes, only right eye results from the control groups were used in these analyses. Of the 12 FLV subjects, 2 had to be excluded: one indicated inability to see the test targets and one had to be repeatedly reminded to respond. One case only could be tested unilaterally because of deep amblyopia in the fellow eye. Self-reported visual loss was bilateral in 4 instances, left sided in 4, and right sided in 2. Because of the different lateralities, results were grouped on the basis of self-reported side involvement plus random side selections in bilaterally affected cases. The resulting F1 group comprised 6 left and 4 right eyes. The 9 fellow eyes formed the F2 group. The F1 group exhibited a considerably longer mean reaction time than the 2 control groups, 1.42 seconds (P 1⁄4 0.01; t test). The F2 group presented an intermediary level, 0.90 second (P 1⁄4 0.01). The mean latency with bilateral target presentations was 0.94 second (Fig 1), suggesting that the F2 eyes governed response latencies. Mean within-subject variability was approximately 4 times greater than in the control groups, but click durations were closely similar. Analysis of receiver-operating characteristics for normal and F1 reaction times revealed remarkably high sensitivity and specificity (Fig 2, available at www.aaojournal.org). All but one of the F2 eyes exceeded the F1 criterion of 0.48 second; 4 were normal by self-report. The various perimetry indices did not differ meaningfully between the study groups. The sole exception was the severity of field loss among the FLV subjects. However, individual instances of equally severe losses occurred in the true lesion group. Pointwise calculations of testeretest correlation coefficients were available in the FLV group and showed a median value of 0.48 (range, 0.21 to 0.69); 9 of 10 coefficients fell below 0.69, the previously reported normal limit. The finding of consistently and markedly prolonged reaction times in dichoptic testing of subjects with FLV is new and unexpected. Previous measurements of reaction times in the setting of automated perimetry have negated any differences between subjects with FLV and controls and no differences were found here. Prolonged dichoptic reaction times were recorded in FLV subjects only: both control groups presented reaction times closely similar to those observed in perimetry (Table 1, available at www.aaojournal.org). Therefore, the long latencies observed in the FLV group cannot be attributed to instrumentation faults. Instead, the discrepancies suggest that the FLV subjects somehow found the dichoptic test situation more demanding than the perimetric one. Clearly, there are many technical differences between the 2 tests, but these apparently were irrelevant for the control subjects. Because FLV subjects commonly are suspected of “malingering,” it is appropriate to consider the possibility that they use extra time to decide how to respond, for example, in case of unilateral involvement, to delay responses when targets are presented to the involved eye. The present observations cannot eliminate such an interpretation, but it is known that laterality of stimulation is very difficult to decide in secluded environments like the one used here. An alternative interpretation may be derived from studies made in other types of perceptual settings.
