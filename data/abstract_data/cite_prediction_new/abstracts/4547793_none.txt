Cholinesterase inhibitors were associated with increased risk for syncope and its consequences in older persons with dementia
Question In older persons with dementia, is use of cholinesterase inhibitors associated with increased risk for syncope and its consequences? Methods Design Retrospective cohort study with mean follow-up of approximately 10 months. Setting Population-based study in Ontario, Canada. Patients 81302 community-dwelling persons 66 years of age (mean age 80 y, 62% women) who had been diagnosed with dementia and had not been hospitalized for syncope in the previous year. 19803 patients (drug cohort) were new users of cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, galantamine, or rivastigmine), and 61499 patients (control cohort) had not received a prescription for a cholinesterase inhibitor in the previous year. The control cohort was matched to the drug cohort by year and quarter of cohort entry. Risk factor Use of a cholinesterase inhibitor. Patients were censored if they stopped using the drug (drug cohort) or started using the drug (control cohort). Outcomes First hospital visit (emergency department or admission) for syncope, bradycardia or complete atrioventricular block, permanent pacemaker insertion, or hip fracture. Main results All outcomes were more frequent in patients who received prescriptions for a cholinesterase inhibitor than in those who did not (Table). Conclusion In older persons with dementia, new use of cholinesterase inhibitors was associated with increased risk for syncope and its consequences. Commentary The modest but statistically significant association of cholinesterase inhibitors with increased risk for bradycardia and pacemaker insertion has inherent biological plausibility because of the drugs peripheral cholinergic side effects. The mechanisms by which cholinesterase inhibitors contribute to syncope and falls are less clear. Although neurocardiovascular instability (including orthostatic hypotension, carotid sinus syndrome, and vasovagal syndrome) may be as much as 6-fold more common in persons with dementia than in older adults without dementia, the prevalence and physiologic pathways may vary according to type of dementia. Neurocardiovascular instability in Alzheimer dementia, but not Lewy-body dementia, seems to be related to concurrent hypertension or ischemic heart disease (1). Patients with Parkinsonian dementia or Lewy-body dementia have considerably more autonomic dysfunction than those with either vascular dementia or Alzheimer disease (2). Clinicians should exercise caution in prescribing a cholinesterase inhibitor, especially to patients with a history of syncope, known cardiovascular disease, orthostatic hypotension, or bradycardia. They should also be alert to the potential additive effect of cholinesterase inhibitors on slowing heart rate in patients taking a -blocker and potential drugdrug interactions that may increase serum levels of the cholinesterase inhibitor. Although unproven, it is logical to assume that risks for syncope, bradycardia, and heart block are dose-dependent; however, the degree of symptomatic benefit from cholinesterase inhibitors is also dose-dependent.
