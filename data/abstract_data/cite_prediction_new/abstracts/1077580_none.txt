Dopa–responsive hemiparkinsonism due to midbrain Virchow–Robin spaces?
Sirs: Hemiparkinsonism due to focal midbrain lesions such as cerebral infarction [6], hemorrhage, or aneurysm [1, 9, 10] is rare. We report a patient with cystic lesions in the midbrain, probably VirchowRobin spaces (VRS) and a levodopa-responsive hemiparkinsonism on the contralateral side. VRS are intracerebral cystic dilatations which can occur along the collicular or accessory collicular arteries in the brainstem and midbrain. In contrast to other cystic lesions, VRS are stable over time, isointense with the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) on MRI, and do not show any contrast enhancement. VRS usually do not exert a mass effect and are clinically asymptomatic. Case report: A 63-year-old lefthanded woman presented with tremor of the right hand and difficulty in writing beginning in November 2002. Neurological examination revealed an intermittent 4–5 Hz tremor at rest in the right hand and leg which disappeared transiently on voluntary activation. She had bradykinesia on the right side and micrographia and rigidity under reinforcement. There was a moderate postural instability with recovery on the pull test. After a test dosage of 100 mg levodopa (plus 25 mg benserazid) symptoms improved within 45 minutes. She then was treated with pramipexole, starting with 0.088 mg 3 times per day. The medication was increased to a total daily dose of 2.1 mg within two months which led to a stable improvement of symptoms for 24 months. Tremor only occasionally recurred. There was no progression of disease and all parkinsonian symptoms remained strictly unilateral. The Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale part III (off-medication/on-medication) was 7/1 at baseline, 8/2 six months later, and again 7/2 points at 14 and 24 months follow-up. MRI in February 2003 revealed a segmented structure of 20 mm in diameter within the left cerebral peduncle and midbrain with a moderate mass effect on to the aqueduct and a subsequent mild enlargement of the third and lateral ventricles, but without expansion of the temporal horns (Fig. 1). The MRI signal of the cystic spaces within the structure was virtually identical to that of the CSF on all MR images. There was no evidence of perifocal edema or contrast enhancement. The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) was the same as for the CSF. Repeat MRI 14 months later revealed identical findings. Complete laboratory tests including markers for vasculitis and CSF analysis yielded normal results. Ioflupane 123I single photon emission computerized tomography scans (DaTSCAN®) demonstrated a reduced radiotracer uptake in the left caudate nucleus and putamen (Fig. 2). The course of the disease, the strictly lateralized symptoms, and the contralateral abnormalities on neuroimaging suggest a relationship between the cystic midbrain structure and the hemiparkinsonism. VRS are believed to be asymptomatic and without mass effect, though the pathogenesis is poorly understood. A correlation of VRS with age, arterial hypertension, and dementia has been reported [3]. Most patients with mesencephalic VRS had hydrocephalus. Two of them revealed extrapyramidal motor symptoms [7, 8]. Yet hydrocephalus is usually associated with generalized parkinsonism [1]. In our case chronic pressure exerted by the VRS or by the hydrocephalus onto the midbrain, or circulatory disturbances in the terminal branches of the supplying arteries might have led to a reduction of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. The natural loss of neurons with aging might have led to the present manifestation of parkinsonism.
