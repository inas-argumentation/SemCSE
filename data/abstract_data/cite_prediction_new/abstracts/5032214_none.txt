Where do we stand with targeted therapy of refractory thyroid cancer?--Utility of RECIST criteria.
Thyroid cancer of the follicular epithelium is one of the human malignancies showing a continuous increase in incidence (1). This increase is likely due to better detection as suggested by the finding that most of the new cases are small, intrathyroidal tumors, fortuitously discovered at neck ultrasonography. They usually have a very good long-term prognosis and very low risk of recurrence after initial standard treatment. Nevertheless, a small subgroup of patients present with locally advanced disease, frequently unresectable and=or distant metastases. Therapeutic options for these patients include TSH suppressive therapy, surgery for resectable lesions, radioiodine therapy provided that the disease retains a functional sodium=iodide symporter (NIS) gene, and external radiotherapy in selected cases. All of these therapies are rarely curative but may be effective in reducing the tumoral burden, improving the quality of life and stabilizing the disease, even for many years. When the above strategies fail and the disease progresses, the physician has to face the challenging question of what to do next. One option is systemic chemotherapy with doxorubicin as a single agent or combined with cisplatin (2). In general, the available experience has been disappointing, showing that the response rates are very low (below 20%), short lasting, and associated with high degree of toxicity. Considering this, it is evident that new therapeutic approaches are needed. The outstanding progress of the last 20 years in the genetics of thyroid cancer has led to the discovery of oncogenes having a pathogenic role in epithelial and medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) initiation and progression. All of them act through the RTK=RAS=RAF=MAPK pathway or the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)=Akt pathway (3), and all of them confer constitutive activation to the transformed cells. In addition, mutations of tumor suppressor genes have been discovered and associated with progression toward a more aggressive phenotype. As in several other human malignancies, the knowledge of the molecular alterations has prompted the search for new agents able to inhibit the function of specific oncoproteins, aiming to shut down the uncontrolled growth of neoplastic cells and, hopefully, have less toxicity in normal cells, the so called ‘‘targeted therapy.’’ In thyroid cancer the logical approach has been to develop molecules blocking the RTK=MAPK and the PI3K=AKT pathways, those activated by RET=PTC, RAS, and BRAF (3) mutations. The effects of some experimental drugs are not restricted to one single protein. Many also share the ability to inhibit other proteins crucial for the survival and expansion of neoplastic cells. Vascular endothelial cell growth factor receptor (VEGFR) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) are two examples. Inhibiting VEGFR blocks the growth of the tumor’s endothelial cells, and inhibiting EGFR may deprive the tumor of one important growth factor sustaining an aggressive phenotype. After promising in vitro experiments showing that these compounds are effective, some are currently being tested in clinical trials (3,4). Will they be able to meet the hope for more effective therapy? Based on preliminary results of phase I=II studies presented in abstracts and meetings, it is difficult to answer this question. But some optimism is justified. In this issue of Thyroid, Pennell et al. (5) offer an example of targeted therapy in advanced thyroid cancer. They conducted a phase II open-label trial using a small molecule inhibitor of the EGFR tyrosine kinase (Gefitinib, Iressa; AstraZeneca, London, UK). The drug has been effective in the treatment of nonsmall cell lung cancer (6), where an activating mutation of the EGFR gene is present. Although, EGFR mutations have not been found in thyroid cancer, EGFR is highly expressed in normal and malignant thyroid tissue (7), and its expression has been associated with a more aggressive phenotype in papillary thyroid cancer. Based on this rationale the authors treated 27 patients with locally advanced or metastatic thyroid cancer of different histotypes, including papillary, follicular, medullary, and Hurthle cell carcinoma. The drug was given by oral route at a dosage of 250mg=day. No objective responses were observed, although tumor reduction that did not meet the criteria for partial response was achieved in 32% of the patients. In addition, stabilization of the disease was obtained in 46%, 24%, and 12% of the patients after 3, 6, and 12months of treatment, respectively. The authors interpreted these results as modest but still suggestive of a biological effect of the drug. Similar results have been reported with another compound, motesanib diphosphate (AMG 706; Amgen, Thousand Oaks, CA), in a phase II trial in patients with advanced thyroid cancer (8,9). In this case, out of a total of 93 patients with follicular-cell-derived thyroid cancer, 12% experienced a complete or partial response and 69% had stable disease, while out of 91 patients with MTC 1% had complete or partial response and 80% had stable disease. As usual when dealing with thyroid carcinoma, the significance of disease stabilization is controversial.
