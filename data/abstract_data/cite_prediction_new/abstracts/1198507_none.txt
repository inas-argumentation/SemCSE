Pricing Automobile Insurance under Multivariate Classification of Risks: Additive versus Multiplicative
For most auto insurance coverages, insureds are cross-classified by at least two variables: driver class and territory. Traditional classification ratemaking has employed a multiplicative technique equivalent to the assumption of a completely interactive model. In this paper, the traditional method and the closely associated log-linear model are found, for recent Massachusetts experience, to lead to biased pricing for drivers in high-rate class-territory combinations. An additive model eliminates this bias and has slightly superior overall accuracy. Constraints are selected so that model parameters describe natural actuarial quantities. The different effects of dependency in exposure distributions and interaction between variables are brought into focus. Introduction For most major types of automobile insurance coverages, insureds are classified by at least two variables: (1) the place where the auto is principally garaged (territory), and (2) the driver characteristics (driver class). The price for a particular combination (cell) of territory and driver class has been traditionally determined as the product of the statewide average price, the territorial relativity, and the driver-class relativity. The relativities are determined separately by the territorial claims experience and the driver-class claims experience. This technique does not examine nor employ the actual claim experience for any particular driver class-territory cell. Instead, it assumes that the ratios of claim amounts between corresponding driver classes are the same in all territories and the ratios of claim amounts between corresponding territories are the same in all driver classes.
