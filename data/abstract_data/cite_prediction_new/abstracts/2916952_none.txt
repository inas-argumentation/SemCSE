Chromosome aberrations in leukocytes exposed in vitro to diagnostic levels of x-rays.
LEUKOCYTES of persons exposed either to therapeutic radiation or to industrial radiation have numerous chromosome aberrations. Tough et al. (1960) and Buckton et al. (1962) found both rings and dicentrics in persons undergoing radiation treatment for ankylosing spondylitis. In these patients, who received about 1,500 rads, one-fourth of the cells examined had rings, dicentrics, or acentric fragments. Treatment for cervical cancer with X rays (Goodlin, 1962; Moore et cl., 1964; Amarose and Baxter, 1965) and for thyrotoxicosis and adenocarcinoma with 1l1 I (Boyd et al., 1961; Macintyre and Dobyns, 1962; Nofal and Beierwaltes, 1964) caused large numbers of chromosome aberrations. Bender and Gooch (1982a, 1963) studied the aberrations in eight persons accidentally receiving from 22 to 365 rads and found that 2-11% of the cells contained chromosome abnormalities 42 months after exposure. Chromosomal changes are sometimes noted in leukocytes of persons undergoing diagnostic irradiation. Retrospective case reports by Stewart and Sanderson (1961) and by Conen et al. (1963) each showed one chromosome abnormality following less than 0.3 and 2.0 rads respectively. Norman, Sasaki, et ai. (1964) found about 0.77% dicentrics in leukocytes of radiation workers exposed to between 10 and 25 R as cumulatively measured over an employment lifetime. In a prospective study by Bloom and Tjio (1964), there were dicentrics, acentric fragments, or rings in 1-5% of the peripheral leukocytes of five patients who had received from 12 to 35 rads for gastrointestinal studies. However, these workers did not find these aberrations in over 3,000 leukocytes examined from seven persons who had received 20 to 80 mR from chest X rays. No rings or dicentrics were seen by Migeon and Merz (1964) in a total of 600 cells from three patients who had each received about 20 R during diagnostic procedures. Since each leukocyte is subject to a different exposure depending upon its location in the body during the exposure, radiation dosimetry for human in vivo studies is particularly inaccurate at low exposures.
