Both are positive results, but the magnitude of the findings is drastically different, with Chit and colleagues asserting net reductions in total healthcare expenditures. The results that Raviotta and colleagues reported were based heavily on mathematical modeling techniques. The quality of the structural assumptions (how various model variables relate to one another) and the limitations of the data defining the model parameters may limit such techniques. Minimizing the number of assumptions is a core advantage of the approach of Chit and colleagues. They achieve this by relying on randomization to define the causal relationship between the use of HD-IIV3 and outcomes of economic importance. A chief causal relationship important to the economic evaluation of HD-IIV3 is the effect of the vaccine on the number of hospital admissions. In the randomized trial, HD-IIV3 was significantly more effective than SD-IIV3 at preventing all-cause hospitalizations (relative vaccine efficacy (rVE) = 6.9%, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.5–12.8%). Further predefined analysis in the trial revealed that the driver of this treatment effect was reduction in cardiorespiratory hospital admissions (rVE = 17.7%, 95% CI = 6.6–27.4%) and, more specifically, serious pneumonia admission (rVE = 39.8%, 95% CI = 19.3–55.1%). Raviotta and colleagues overlooked these treatment effects. Consequently, Chit and colleagues estimated that HDIIV3 prevented 575 more cardiorespiratory hospital admissions plausibly related to influenza per 100,000 vaccinated population than IIV3 (data taken directly from the efficacy trial SD-IIV3), whereas in their model, Raviotta and colleagues estimated that HD-IIV3 would have prevented 5,185 more hospitalizations than IIV3 if is had been used in the entire older adult population of the United States in 2013. When one considers the 2013 U.S. older adult population of 44,704,074, HD-IIV3 would have been expected to prevent 11 more hospitalizations per 100,000 vaccinated population than IIV3. In conclusion, the modeling study of Raviotta and colleagues estimated a far smaller effect of HD-IIV3 on this outcome than the findings from the HD-IIV3 efficacy trial. During the 2-year FIM12 trial, HD-IIV3 demonstrated strong efficacy against serious pneumonia. Investigators propose that HD-IIV3 may have ancillary effects on the most common etiologies of pneumonia beyond its prevention or modulation of influenza, mediated through a cross-pathogen response or indirectly through alterations of the nasopharyngeal microbiome. Thus, HD-IIV3 may have vaccine-specific effects that reduce serious pneumonia rates, resulting in a hospitalization rate reduction beyond what would be expected from preventing influenza cases. The Raviotta and colleagues model does not explicitly account for this, but they are not unique in their omission. An early model that Chit and colleagues developed and published in Vaccine also failed to define a model capable of accounting for the results of the efficacy trial. This was the impetus behind Chit and colleagues pursuing the economic evaluation directly using the efficacy trial results rather than relying on disease modeling. Finally, despite the more positive evaluation by Chit and colleagues, it is plausible that their results are still conservative. For instance, because the FIM12 trial was an individual-based study, it does not account for any benefits attained through indirect protection. Furthermore, information was not collected on any posthospital-discharge costs of care or reductions in quality of life. Finally, the trial was not large enough to estimate a treatment effect against mortality. A better understanding is needed of the HD-IIV3 mechanism of action that results in protection against influenza, pneumonia, and hospitalizations. Without this, it will remain difficult to use models to appropriately predict the full public health effect of HD-IIV3.
