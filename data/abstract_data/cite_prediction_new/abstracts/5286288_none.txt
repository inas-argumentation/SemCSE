Tailoring Nd-doped glasses for laser applications: Dependence of induced-emission cross sections on composition
Ho:YLF is easily and rapidly grown using a reactive atmosphere crystal growth process. A 6-hr growth cycle Czochralski  ingot yields several laser rods of glass-like optical quality. Because of easy growth, systematic variation of ion concentrations x-y-z has been possible and allowed study of effects on laser performance. Horizontal Bridgman crystal growth method can be used for large scale, inexpensive rod production when x-y-z values are fixed. Thermal and elastic properties of birefringent Ho:YLF are satisfactory. Maximum power dissipation of fracture stress limit is 60% of the Nd:YAG value. New laser design options result from significant changes in laser performance occurring with variation of Er-Tm sensitizer concentrations x-y and Ho activator  concentration z. Trade-offs can be made for pump-band optical densities, laser threshold, slope efficiency, and output pulse power. Design options therefore occur for choice of pump lamp and lamp parameters, resonator configuration, rod dimensions, and cooling system. The options are generally interdependent. Optimum Er-Tm-Ho concentrations exist for particular laser applications of Ho :YLF. Rod colling requirements have more flexibility for 2.06 pm laser design than for similar performance Nd :YAG systems. The Ho :YLF laser transition terminal level is 300 cmabove ground and is thermally populated at 300°K. For ambient temperature operation the minimum possible metastable level excitation necessary for laser gain requires 2.4 J per unit rod volume (cm3) per atomic percent Ho concentration. For light weight, portable sources where minimum laser threshold is an important system requirement, a small diameter laser rod will also minimize rod thermal gradients. With simple lamprod imaging optics, rod and lamp dimensions are 1-to-1 and reliable lamp performance requires about a 1 mm bore lamp, implying a minimum 1 mm rod diameter. Selective variation of rod diameter depends on efficiency of rod pump light absorption. Consequently, pump level optical densities and efficient pump-tometastable level energy transfer requirements provide additional design constraints. The Ho:YLF pump band spectral distribution covers 90% of a pulsed xenon lamp spectral output. RespectiveEr3+, Tm3+ and Ho3+ levels account for 45, 13, and 42 % of the spectral distribution of pump levels. Threshold consideration  requires  modest Ho concentration, indicating  variation of Er  concentration for control of pump band optical density. Although Tm3+ levels weakly contribute to pump light absorption, Tm concentration is necessary for fast pump-to-metastable energy transfer rate. Extensive recorded data on Ho:YLF concentration effects indicate an Er-Tm-Ho energy transfer interaction  contrasting with results reported for other ion interaction systems. However, the data  do provide  empirical  criteria satisfactory for laser design for specific application requirements. For a heavy system with low duty-cycle requirements, such as high output energy rangefinders, a design option different from threshold minimization occurs. This represents a nearly ten-fold increase compared with standard disk designs. Such a system is very promising for large beam amplification since it is scalable. Several problems  related to mirror  deformation, induced birefringence, cooling and pump light filtering have been solved, and will be discussed during the presentation of this paper.
