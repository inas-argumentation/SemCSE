New Ventures in Hungary: The Impact of U.S. Partners
Choices made by new businesses in emerging economies such as Hungary are complex. Decisions must be made about how to gain access to resources, the appropriate founding strategy, and the vision guiding future plans and intentions. Competitive strategies which encompass these choices are crucial since start-ups in such situations must succeed quickly, often with little support from financial institutions. The effective formulation of initial strategies by new small business in emerging economies may be greatly influenced by the presence of partners or a start-up team. Partners can provide access to capital, to deep management capabilities, and to customers and distribution channels (McCann 1991). The presence or absence of these partner capabilities affect the firm's resources and enable choices about pricing and firm positioning. To the extent that partnering choices mitigate pressures on the venture's viability, understanding partnership arrangements and their influence seems essential to understanding start-up strategies in developing economies. Entrepreneurs in countries like Hungary face the critical issue of whether to partner with foreign principals, or whether to establish alliances with other nationals. Foreign companies seeking access to the Hungarian market offer numerous resources, not the least of which is financial. U.S. businesses alone have invested $1.8 billion in Hungary which is more than half of the country's foreign direct investment (Warson 1993). Projections are that foreign investment will continue since foreign companies enjoy favorable tax holidays and government subsidies for investing equity capital. Partnerships with U.S. principals may prove especially beneficial since they offer exposure to Western style management techniques, current technologies access to export markets, and marketing management. Alternatively, in countries like Hungary where the economy is making the transition from controlled to free market status, the supposed advantage associated with foreign partnerships may prove ineffective, or at a minimum, premature. Partnerships with regional principals who are familiar with the history and the culture of the area may prove more beneficial. Teaming up with partners informed about the local market can greatly reduce uncertainty in decision making which in turn may enhance the probability of firm success. Teaming up with uninformed partners, however, may only increase uncertainty. In this paper the ownership structure of start-ups in Hungary is examined to ascertain whether partnerships provide advantages over sole proprietorships, and whether partnerships with U.S. principals are more beneficial than partnerships with other regional principals. While alliances with U.S. principals can take many forms ranging from contractual (e.g., licensing and franchising) to jointly owned subsidiaries, this study is limited to partnerships where U.S. principals have a direct equity investment. We examine whether differences in the three ownership structures (U.S.-Hungarian partnerships; regional partnerships; sole proprietorships) are associated with differences in two aspects of start-up experiences: access to resources and founding strategy. Theoretical Framework Importance of Team at Inception Although entrepreneurship is often associated with the image of the sole proprietor "going-it-alone", new ventures are just as apt to be started by teams. The team approach is more effective for amassing resources at the firm's inception. In addition to financial resources, partners can contribute much needed expertise to the start-up process. Kazanjian (1988) found that one of the most important issues for new firms is "achieving management depth and finding talent." Kamm and Nurick (1993) suggest that this problem can be solved through establishing teams of partners.
