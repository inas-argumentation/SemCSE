Theories of Judicial Behavior and the Law: Taking Stock and Looking Ahead
Theories of judicial behaviour have been put forward by political scientists to make sense of how the adjudication process unfolds and law is put into practice by courts. These positive theories constitute a powerful tool to assess judicial activism by looking into the several types of motivations and incentives that judges have to decide the cases brought before them. By shedding light in how these cognitive processes take place and framing courts as (just another) political actor, they have exposed several insufficiencies and flawed assumptions of the normative theories of judicial review. To that extent, they pose a standing challenge for normative theories to get fine-tuned. At the same time, however, they exhibit several weaknesses. On the one hand, the fact that they are strongly conditioned by the institutional environment of origin (the US federal court system) limits their transferability. On the other hand, the obsession with quantifiable data and with outcomes has led their authors to disregard the actual judicial opinions. All things considered, similarly to what happens with normative theories, positive theories of judicial review only tell part of the story. This article revisits the topic and provides an overview of attitudinal and (both internal and external) strategic theories, what draws them together and apart, as well as of their limitations.
