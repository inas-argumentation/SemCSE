Risk perception in toxicology--part II: toxicology must be the solution not the problem.
Toxicology is a broad-based discipline that is changing and expanding continually in order to meet the challenges posed by the daily human and ecological exposure to chemicals and other toxicants. Paracelsus (1493–1541) was the first person to recognize that everything, all treatments, therapies, and chemicals, have the potential to be poisonous and that it is only the dose that makes the difference between benefit and harm. Modern-day toxicologists recognize that all substances have the intrinsic potential to be hazardous and that under conditions of sufficient exposure will be a risk to health. Historically, mandates such as the Delaney Clause, which prohibited the addition of substances found to cause cancer in animals or humans into the food supply (1958) laid the foundation for assessing whether a chemical was ‘‘safe.’’ The landmark publication of the ‘‘Red Book’’ (National Research Council, 1983) detailed the fundamental steps in the risk assessment process, including hazard identification, doseresponse assessment, exposure analysis, and risk characterization and provided the foundation for consistency in the risk assessment process. More recently, the National Academy of Sciences reported on Science and Judgement in Risk Assessment (National Research Council, 1994) which advanced approaches to incorporating new scientific data into the risk assessment process and how to deal with uncertainty in decisions when sufficient scientific information was lacking.
