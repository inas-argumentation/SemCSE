Anti-“negative-doping” testing: a new perspective in anti-doping research?
The goalkeeper of an Italian first division soccer team has recently been accused of spiking his team-mates’ beverages with psychoactive drugs (i.e., benzodiazepines), to influence the results of several games and place his bets for losing the matches. This circumstance became evident when five team-mates suffered from various degrees of sickness, and the subsequent laboratory testing revealed the presence of Lormetazepam in their urine (La Gazzetta dello Sport 2011). This scandal, which is suspected to involve several soccer players in Italy, is neither likely to be the first episode of this kind of ‘‘negative-doping’’ nor is it likely to be the last. Basically, doping in sports has two primary objectives: the traditional ‘‘doping to win’’, which is aimed to enhance physiological performance, or the less usual ‘‘doping to lose’’. The latter circumstance has been widely practiced in horse-racing since ancient times (e.g., in early Greek mythology, there is a reference to Medea using an opiate to stupefy the dragon that guarded the Golden Fleece), where large doses of opioids, neuroleptics or tranquilisers (typically acepromazine or reserpine) are illicitly administered to race-horses to decrease their athletic performance and thereby to gain an unfair advantage for others (Clarke and Moss 1976). Now, there is evidence that negative doping might not be limited to dogor horseracing, so that a new perspective is challenging the already complicated setting of anti-doping testing, i.e., so-called anti-‘‘negative-doping’’ testing. The World-Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) is the independent foundation that promotes, coordinates and monitors the fight against drug abuse in sport worldwide. WADA currently classifies doping agents and methods among several classes according to their biological action, i.e., non-approved substances: drugs or molecules not addressed by any of the subsequent sections (S0); anabolic agents (S1); peptide hormones, growth factors and related substances (S2); beta-2 agonists (S3); hormone antagonists and modulators (S4); and diuretics and other masking agents (S5) (World Anti-Doping Agency 2011). Although several methods such as enhancement of oxygen transfer and/or chemical and physical manipulation are also included in the prohibited list, sophisticated strategies such as ‘‘adding performance-decreasing substances to others’’ are not regulated. According to the World Anti-Doping Code, which was first adopted in 2003 and became effective in 2004, the anti-doping program is aimed to protect an athlete’s fundamental right to participate in doping-free sport, and thus to promote health and—of particular relevance to this issue—fairness of competition (World Anti-Doping Agency 2011).
