The cellular component of the mucinous nevus consists of CD34‐positive fibroblasts
To the Editor, Mucinous nevus is a rare variant of connective tissue nevus, proteoglycan type, that was first described by Redondo Bellón et al. in 1993.1 Since then, only 10 other patients with this kind of lesion have been reported in the literature, all of them as case reports.2– 8 Mucinous nevus is composed of grouped papules and nodules forming a plaque or arranged in a linear dermatomal distribution. It can be present since birth or develop during childhood or early adulthood. It is usually located on the trunk. Histologically, it consists of a bandlike deposit of mucin in the superficial dermis that displaces the collagen and elastic fibers. Epidermal changes consisting of acanthosis with elongated rete ridges and a variable grade of hyperkeratosis have been reported in about one half of the cases.3,5– 7 These alterations are considered part of the hamartomatous process. Mucinous nevi with or without abnormalities of the epidermis share all the remaining clinicopathologic features. Some authors think that the cases with epidermal changes may represent a combined mucinous and epidermal nevus.3 Within the dermal mucinous substance, there is a scarce cellular component composed of spindle or stellate mesenchymal cells without atypia. The immunoprofile of these cells, probably implicated in the production of the mucinous material that names the lesion, is presently unknown, with none of the previous reports having addressed this issue. We have recently studied a case of a mucinous nevus in a 27-year-old male, who presented multiple confluent, purplish papules forming a plaque in the lumbar region. The lesion had appeared several years before. A biopsy of one of the papules was performed. Microscopic findings consisted of a subepidermal band of mucin that stained with alcian blue at pH 2.5 (Figs. 1 and 2).
