Skin Necrosis Following Subcutaneous Immunoglobulin (SCIg)
To the Editor, Subcutaneous immunoglobulin is an increasingly popular mode of immunoglobulin replacement, owing to ease of selfadministration, stable IgG trough levels, and superior side effect profile compared to intravenous therapy [1]. The introduction of recombinant human hyaluronidase-facilitated (fSCIG) and rapid push has opened up further options to individualize therapy. Skin necrosis in association with subcutaneous immunoglobulin administration has recently been reported in two pediatric patients with primary immunodeficiency by Datta et al. [2]. We describe two adult patients with similar localized injection site reactions, one following conventional subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIg) and one after recombinant hyaluronidase-facilitated subcutaneous immunoglobulin (fSCIg) therapy. Patient one was a 48-year-oldmale with common variable immunodeficiency for which he had received replacement fSCIg for 1 year with 400 ml of 10 % immunoglobulin with hyaluronidase pre-treatment, described previously [3]. Patient preference for reduced needle frequency due to mild essential tremor had driven the change from SCIg and directed use of the anterior thigh as an infusion site. Following his regular infusion using a blue butterfly needle (23 G, 19 mm length), the patient noted a red discoloration, which evolved over 48 h into a tender area with central Bscab,^ and dermal atrophy consistent with skin necrosis. He was treated with topical mupirocin (Bactroban) in addition to oral flucloxacillin to prevent secondary infection and subsequently healing and scar formation occurred over a 3-week period (Fig. 1). Patient two was a 43-year-old female, who received high-dose immunodulation for multifocal motor neuropathy. Weekly SCIg was administered as a 32-g dose (supplied as 160 ml, 20 %) of an approved subcutaneous product infused via four anterior abdominal sites at 27 ml/h. Infusions were administered via the Bbutton^ style Neria needle (9 mm length, 27 G). As with patient one, there was no prior history of allergic reaction, skin pathology, or staphylococcal skin carriage; SCIg had been well tolerated over the preceding 2 years. At needle withdrawal, she noted a Bclearly demarcated blanched white disc of skin^ roughly the size of a ten-pence coin (∼24 mm diameter) at one of the four sites. The site did not itch and was not painful; a scab subsequently developed of approximately 5 mm diameter with surrounding elliptical erythema and dermal atrophy. Treatment with topical bactroban and oral flucloxacillin was followed by scarring without systemic complications (Fig. 1). A home visit was arranged to re-assess infusion technique; in the first case, techniques to overcome fear of needle insertion and correct insertion angle were reinforced. In case 2, the needle was changed from the Neria needle to the longer blue butterfly. Neither patient has experienced further skin necrosis. To our knowledge, this is the first description of skin necrosis in adults following conventional or facilitated SCIg. This is a rare event; however, informal discussion with colleagues suggests at least ten similar episodes across the UK.
