Changing societal‐military relations in Israel: The operational implications
It has long been a convention amongst students of strategy to acknowledge the contribution of societal factors to the outcome of military conflicts. This theme appeared in the writings of Sun Tzu and was reiterated by Machiavelli and Clausewitz. It is in the twentieth century, however, that the emphasis on the societal determinants of war outcomes has become particularly pronounced so much so that 'soft' domestic dimensions of security now often assume much of the primacy once reserved for such 'hard' attributes as size, technology, battlefield command and productive capacity. In the 1970s, for instance, Rosen and Howard persuasively argued that victory in war ultimately goes to the belligerent in possession of greater domestic resilience and higher public morale. Responding to current redefinitions of security concepts, recent research has begun to investigate some of the corollaries of that thesis. In the process, it has also adjusted the co-ordinates of analytical attention. Instead of focusing on the relative contribution of societal circumstances to the results of armed conflict, scholars now also examine their impact on the manner in which wars are waged. This shift of emphasis has opened up several new avenues of inquiry, theoretical as well as historical, whose thrust is to concentrate on the intrastate context of force mobilization rather than the interstate dimensions of its application. Principal among the questions raised are: can differences between societies account for variations in their force structures? Is it possible to identify domestic determinants of national predilections for specific military doctrines and their applications? Increasingly, the answers appear to be affirmative. Contrary to some conventional assumptions, the dictates of international competition and survival do not necessarily compel all societies to organize their militaries in basically similar functional ways.
