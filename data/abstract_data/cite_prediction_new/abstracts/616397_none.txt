Prevention of bronchiolitis obliterans-the realization of a hope?
See Related Article, page 138 Bronchiolitis obliterans is a small airway disease triggered by injury of small airway epithelial cells and sub-epithelial structures, followed by an abnormal healing response with excessive fibrosis that constricts the airways. Bronchiolitis obliterans is a frequent and disabling complication after lung transplantation, occurring in 450% of lung transplant recipients within 5 years of the transplant. Current treatments are only partially effective and include increasing immunosuppression and administering low-dose macrolide antibiotics, leukotriene-receptor antagonists and combinations of inhaled bronchodilators and glucocorticoids. Because of the multiplicity of mechanisms causing the initial lesion to the epithelial surfaces of the bronchi, involving both innate and adaptive immunity responding either to allo-antigens or infectious/toxic insults, prevention of the initial lesions may not be possible or feasible in time to prevent the fibrotic response. Thus, bronchiolitis obliterans remains a major cause of graft failure. In this issue, Watanabe et al report that imatinib, a specific tyrosine kinase inhibitor, prevents obstruction of the airways in the heterotopic tracheal transplantation animal model of bronchiolitis obliterans. The anti-fibrotic properties of imatinib, were first described by Daniels et al and Aono et al more than 10 years ago. These investigators showed that imatinib decreased fibrosis and fibroblast proliferation through inhibition of platelet-derived growth factor tyrosine kinase and transforming growth factor-beta. A year later, Majhail et al showed that administration of imatinib ameliorated bronchiolitis obliterans in a patient with chronic myeloid leukemia relapse after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.
