Mental retardation, keratoconus, febrile seizures and sinoatrial block: a previously undescribed autosomal recessive disorder
To the Editor: We present three non-dysmorphic siblings (one female and two males) from a consanguineous Pakistani family with severe mental retardation and keratoconus, resulting in significant visual failure. All three suffered recurrent febrile seizures throughout the first few years of life. Two of these individuals subsequently developed sinoatrial heart block, which in one necessitated the insertion of a cardiac pacemaker. This combination of features constitutes a previously undescribed syndrome inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. These three individuals, from a sibship of seven, were born at term following normal pregnancies. Birth weights were 2.7 (<10th centile), 2.7 and 3.2 kg (<50th centile). All three experienced recurrent febrile convulsions between 6 and 13 months of age and demonstrated significant developmental delay, resulting in investigation as young children. Immunological (IgG, IgA and IgM), white cell enzyme, metabolic (pyruvate and lactate), cerebrospinal fluid (cells, protein, immunoglobulin, glucose and viral screen) cholesterol and microbiological (TORCH screen) investigations all showed normal results. EEGs demonstrated diffuse slowing of background rhythm, but no seizure activity. Chromosome and Fragile X-testing was normal. Cranial CT imaging was noncontributory. Audiometry revealed normal hearing. As adults, all three had head sizes on or above the 97th centile. Parental measurements were unavailable. Two of the siblings were diagnosed with sick sinus syndrome (aged 9 and 19 years) following 24-h ECG demonstrating frequent pauses of up to 4.8 s. One individual required insertion of a permanent pacemaker at age 20 years. Between the ages of 15 and 34, ophthalmological investigation revealed all three siblings to have bilateral keratoconus associated with a significant reduction in visual acuity. Electroretinography and visual evoked responses were unremarkable. Examination as adults demonstrated mental retardation, worse in the female. No focal neurological abnormalities were detected. All three required assistance with activities of daily living, including dressing and washing. All had severe speech delay, using gesticulation and grunts for communication. None of the siblings can read. The three individuals reported here have a very similar combination of features. All suffered repeated febrile convulsions, severe developmental delay and keratoconus with poor vision. Two of the three have sinoatrial block for which one required pacemaker insertion. All three had head circumferences at or around the 97th centile. The observation of the three affected siblings, one female and two males, born to healthy related parents suggests that this is an autosomal recessive condition, although the possibility of a microdeletion can not be discounted. The association of keratoconus with mental retardation is a common one (1). However, this combination together with a cardiac conduction defect and febrile seizures is unusual. Mental retardation, keratoconus and cardiac conduction defects are seen in Costello syndrome (2), but the other features of that disease were not present in our cases. Seizures in early life and cardiac conduction defects suggest the possibility of an ion channel disorder (3, 4). Of note, Ludwig et al. (5) described homozygous HCN2-null mice, who, like the cases reported here, demonstrate sinoatrial node dysfunction and seizures. Thus, HCN2 would represent a candidate gene for this disorder. A syndrome including febrile seizures, generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (GEFSþ), involves mutations in either sodium (SCN1A, SCN1B and SCN2A) (6) or chloride (GABRG2) (7), ion channels.
