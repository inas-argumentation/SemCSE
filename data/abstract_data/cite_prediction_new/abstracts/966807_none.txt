The Political Economy of Publicly Funded Biomedical Research: Evidence from NIH Funding for Rare Diseases
How do the scientific community, politicians, and special interest groups influence the allocation of publicly funded research? We investigate this question by analyzing novel data on the lobbying expenditures of disease interest groups, Congressional earmarks, and funding by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) for 955 rare diseases, during the 1998‐2008 period. We show that Congressional earmarking for the rare diseases is responsive to the lobbying expenditures of interest groups associated with the diseases, but not to our measures of scientific opportunity and disease burden. NIH funding for the diseases is not responsive to earmarking overall but only to earmarking associated with lobbying expenditures. Overall, we estimate that between 0.52%‐2.2% of NIH’s allocations for rare diseases each year are related to lobbying by interest groups. We also provide suggestive evidence that lobbying has an informational role, helping focus Congressional attention on diseases with higher burden or scientific opportunity. Our findings have implications not only for the U.S. public research enterprise, but also for the political economy literature on how interest groups influence policy.
