Flipping a Sport Finance and Economics Class — Lessons Learned
2015-100 10:35 AM (Bytowne) Statement of Purpose Many students struggle to learn, understand, and apply the breadth and depth of the financial and economic principles comprising the business of intercollegiate athletics and professional sports. Because of the extensive content in sport finance and economics and desire to more actively engage students during class to enhance learning, the professor decided to flip this class. A flipped, hybrid, or blended class provides a learning environment combining out-of-class learning of content so students could engage in participatory learning activities during class. The student-centered learning environment of a flipped class becomes more dynamic and interactive as students work together to solve problems, grapple with the content, and answer questions, rather than listen passively. Review of Literature The blending of face-to-face instruction with technology-mediated delivery of information has been practiced for decades (Dziuban, Hartman, & Moskal, 2004). Based on increased evidence, well-designed flipped, hybrid, or blended classes have been shown to enhance student learning and retention (Amaral & Shank, 2010). One major contributing factor occurred when traditional classes were liberated from lectures and replaced by higher-value experiential activities (Wallace & Weiner, 1998), such as problem solving, small-group discussions, peer feedback on drafts of papers, exploratory writing, and checks for understanding in a variety of forms (Blended and Flipped, 2014). Amaral and Shank (2010) posited how using digital delivery of multi-media tutorials promoted student success. That is, not only were students engaging more with course content out-of-class by being required to view and listen to lectures, but their learning was reinforced during class as they expanded the amount of time spent engaged with the content through a variety of learning activities. Being able to access learning management systems 24/7 enabled students to embrace a paradigm shift to a new learned-centered model (Dziuban, Hartman, & Moskal, 2004). Conceptual Design of this Flipped Class The professor prepared 24 audio-visual lectures that presented key content via the course learning management system (i.e., Blackboard) that students were required to view and listen to prior to when each topic was to be discussed in class. Benefits of providing electronically developed and delivered audio-visual lectures of unfamiliar, detailed, and challenging content abounded. First, students through Blackboard and convenient devices such as tablets and smart phones viewed and listened to these lectures (6:07 to 27:08 minutes each) that were then used as starting points for whole and small-group class discussions. Second, these lectures helped students become familiar with key concepts prior to class discussions, in-class writing activities, or lectures building upon essential information and concepts. Third, students who may have missed classes were able to partially catch up by viewing these lectures. Fourth, students used these lectures to review and study for tests. The content in these lectures was not re-taught during class. To further reinforce their importance, each topic had one or more SoftChalk review exercises provided on Blackboard so students could check their understanding of what was presented. For example, lectures on revenues and expenses and the arms race in intercollegiate athletics were presented along with SoftChalk self-checks, a sorting activity, and a crossword puzzle to check for understanding. Out-of-class lectures about lawsuits and free agency, revenue sharing, and the collective bargaining agreement in the National Football League included SoftChalk exercises of a flash card activity, quiz group, crossword puzzle and sorting activity. Lessons Learned In responding to a Qualtrics survey conducted after the semester ended, 72% of the students (N = 46) reported that out-of-class lectures often positively impacted their learning, while the remaining 28% responded these sometimes did. Students also were asked to assess whether a variety of active learning activities, such as group blogs, Jeopardy 2015 North American Society for Sport Management Conference (NASSM 2015) Ottawa, ON June 2 – 6, 2015 Page 485 review games, minute papers, and the audio-visual lectures was most helpful to their learning. The 24 audio-visual lectures were rated by 44% of the students as the most helpful to their learning; 0% rated the out-of-class lectures as the least helpful. These positive results suggested that use of the 24 audio-visual lectures was a constructive addition to this course. The professor’s personal reflection was highly positive because it facilitated expanding class discussions and incorporating numerous active learning activities into each class. In addition to these instructional strategies helping to increase student learning, students’ grades and end-of-course anonymous feedback suggested they learned more possibly because I flipped this class. Importance to NASSM Attendees One essential goal of flipping a class is to move away from the heavy reliance on lectures to deliver information and structure a class (Honeycutt & Garrett, 2014). Instead, every instructor can use a variety of active learning strategies that place students at the center of the learning experience, such as by using “think-pair-share,” games likes Jeopardy, Poll Everywhere for online polling, minute papers, and much more.
