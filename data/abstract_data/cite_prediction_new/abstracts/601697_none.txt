Are interactions between organic compounds and nanoscale weathering minerals the key drivers of carbon storage in soils?
L use disrupts the natural functioning of soils, leading to soil resource degradation. Forecasts also estimate that agricultural production has to be increased by 1.85-fold to meet the food demand of 9 billion people by 2050. Agro-ecological practices thus have to fulfill two main objectivesminimize soil degradation while improving ecosystem services. Agroecological strategies for restoring soil functioning mainly strive to enhance the soil organic matter pool. In this Viewpoint, we argue that future agro-ecological techniques should also be geared toward increasing the residence time of organic matter in soil. A better understanding of organic matter stabilization mechanisms is therefore needed. We propose an alternative model of organo-mineral interactions that no longer considers soil minerals as stable surfaces but instead take nanoscale weathering mineral dynamics into account. The soil organic matter (OM) pool is one of the main targets of agro-ecological strategies for three reasons: (i) soil OM plays key roles in soil system functioning (plant nutrient, soil structure, energy supply for microbes and pollutant adsorption), (ii) soil OM represents the largest continental carbon (C) stock, and (iii) intensive use of arable land has led to the destabilization of significant amounts of soil OM. Restoring the OM pool in cultivated soils could thus address two major issues: food security and climate change mitigation. The C-stock in a soil (t.ha−1) results from a combination of flux (t·ha−1·yr−1) and residence time (yr). It has been demonstrated that a severe decrease in OM content following intensive agricultural practices is not solely caused by a decrease in the input flux, but rather by a decrease in the carbon residence time. These two levers can be used to increase OM stabilization and thus enhance the C-stock. Agro-ecological techniques developed so far have mainly been focused on the first lever. However, greater insight into the mechanisms of soil OM stabilization and destabilization is required to act on the Cresidence time lever. To understand stabilization mechanisms, it should be kept in mind that the average mass proportion of minerals and OM in soil is roughly 95% versus 5%. Mineral phases include both primary minerals from parent rocks and secondary minerals formed via primary mineral weathering. Soil OM includes about 15% living biomass and 85% dead biomass. Dead OM is formed by an assembly of low molecular weight compounds. These organic compounds have their own dynamics but it remains unknown why some OM persists for millennia whereas other OM decomposes readily. Among OM stabilization mechanisms, mineral surfaces are suspected to govern organic compound stabilization through organo-mineral interactions at the molecular scale. Physical-chemical protection by mineral phases involves intermolecular interactions between organic compounds and inorganic phases (mineral surfaces, secondary nanosized phases or metal ions). A generally recognized conceptual model of the organo-mineral interface in soil proposes that organic compounds form a ∼4 nm thick discrete zonal sequence dependent on the hydrophobicity of the molecules (Figure 1). In this model, mineral phases are considered to be (i) stable, (ii) of much larger size than that of the organic compounds, and (iii) formed prior to the occurrence of organic compounds that form complexes on their surface. However, this pattern is probably too simplistic because the fate of the mineral phases closely depends on the soil biophysical−chemical fluctuations. Indeed, primary rock minerals are subjected to weathering conditions that upset their stable thermodynamic conditions. Weathering is especially enhanced at points of contact with bioactive zones, such as plant roots and in the vicinity of microorganisms. The formation of secondary weathered phases in soils has been challenged over the last 10−15 years, particularly in soils developed on volcanic rocks. Weathering of the latter rocks rapidly produces poorly crystallized nanometric-sized secondary phases (allophane type).
