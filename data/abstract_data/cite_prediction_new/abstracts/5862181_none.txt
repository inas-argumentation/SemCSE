Defining Response and Defining How We Measure Response to CRT
It is generally accepted that chronic cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) produces benefit through a variety of salutary actions. Therapy efficacy has been demonstrated utilizing established methods of measuring response to therapy in heart failure subjects.1,2 After the completion of the pivotal clinical trials, the therapy has been applied to thousands of patients, most of whom have very advanced disease. The study by Cha et al., published in this issue of JCE, provides additional and naturalistic single-center data relating various response parameters to the event-driven outcome, survival.3-8 Looking back 12 years, it is apparent that the initial controlled clinical trials of CRT had rather humble aspirations. Since it was unknown if the therapy would achieve benefit and considering the severity of heart failure in the targeted population, the goals were to demonstrate safety of the implanted hardware and improve relatively simple clinical or so-called functional endpoints. This type of study design was also the most expeditious path to regulatory approval.2 A composite of functional measures such as New York Heart Functional Class, quality-of-life assessment, 6-minute walk distance, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and LVEF demonstrated improvement at 6 months using CRT, compared with no CRT. A comparison of mean values across patients with and without CRT was used to demonstrate statistical response. Additionally, measures of disease progression were also collected and showed improvement by the same statistical method. These included echocardiographic assessment of progression of disease and neurohormonal measures.4-6 Given the cost of the hardware and the magnitude of the treatment effect (a lot of patients felt a lot better), there was pressure from many to complete a study looking at important event-driven outcomes for this population, such as the composite endpoint of hospitalization and mortality.9 At that juncture, it was unclear if CRT was just a “feel good” therapy that might have the potential actually to reduce longevity, such as some of the pharmacologic inotropes.10 Two well-conducted mortality trials involving nearly 3,000 patients and over $200,000,000 dollars demonstrated a 36% reduction in hospitalization and mortality and a 20% reduction in mortality. The magnitude of benefit, regardless of the response measure utilized, identified CRT as a very important heart failure therapy. However, this evolution of response measures also led to a fair amount of confusion that the Cha study3 helps to clarify.
