Defining outcomes for high-risk infants: Problems and possibilities.
The title of this issue of Seminars in Perinatology, “Outcomes of High-Risk Infants,” naturally leads to two questions: what is meant by “high-risk infant,” and what is meant by “outcome?” The answers are not straightforward, and have evolved and expanded over the decades as perinatal and neonatal care have advanced. The term “high-risk infant” has been defined in many ways, and unfortunately has been encumbered by many negative overtones. In general, the goal of delineating infants as high-risk has been to heighten focus on those children who may benefit from increased surveillance and early intervention across a number of domains. According to a policy statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics, high-risk infants are defined broadly as including (1) the preterm infant, (2) the infant with special health care needs or dependence on technology, (3) the infant at risk because of family issues, and (4) the infant with anticipated early death. Many clinical conditions and risk factors seen in term-born infants, including congenital heart disease, need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, place them at high risk for neurologic, developmental, functional, and health outcomes challenges in early childhood and beyond. Yet, the neurodevelopmental and functional needs of many of these term-born infants may be overlooked at hospital discharge, and they may not be appropriately referred to early intervention services even when they are identified as eligible in follow-up. Conversely, preterm infants, particularly those born very preterm (o32 weeks estimated gestational age) and extremely preterm (o28 weeks estimated gestational age), are easily the most identifiable and targeted population of high-risk infants. Very preterm and extremely preterm births are rare, representing only 1.92% and 0.73% of births, respectively, in 2013 in the United States. But with nearly 4 million births annually, this means that 75,000 very preterm infants and 30,000 extremely preterm were born in 2013 alone in the U.S. And even among very low birth weight infants, referral to high-risk outpatient programs may be missed at discharge, a finding that is associated with social and demographic disparities.
