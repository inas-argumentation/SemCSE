Sir, T. M. Reynolds raises the question of whether it is necessary to calculate different medians for local and distant samples if free P hCG is used in Down’s screening. Taking our results (Zimmermann et al. 1994), we were concerned about stability of free p hCG and wanted to know about practical problems before introducing it into daily routine. In a retrospective study we compared multiples of median (MOM) of 337 in-house samples with 306 distant samples sent to our laboratory by post. Most samples reached the laboratory unseparated within one day of venepuncture, whereas the inhouse samples were separated within three hours and kept frozen until determination. The median in the in-house group was 0.975 MOM compared with 099 in the distant group. Mann-Whitney U test showed no difference between the groups (Fig. 1). In conclusion, in our region it does not seem necessary to adjust free P hCG values for the delay in posting. However, according to our experience, we will recommend our clients to post blood samples after separation as quickly as possible and to keep them in the refrigerator at weekends and public holidays until the next working day. If delivery of the samples is not guaranteed within 24 hours, values could be corrected using correction factors calculated by Spencer et al. (1993). As shown in the latest paper concerning free p hCG (Macri et al. 1994), the median for a Down’s population syndrome is 264 times the median of a normal population, meaning that determination of the free P-subunit is superior to the whole p hCG under the assumption that the standard deviation is at least as narrow. Unfortunately, the standard deviation in this paper is omitted. Before the medical community begins to use free P hCG, the authors should provide proof that it is better.
