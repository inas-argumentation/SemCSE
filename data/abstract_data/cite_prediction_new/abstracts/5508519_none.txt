Ethnic-Specific In Vitro–In Vivo Extrapolation and Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Approaches to Predict Cytochrome P450-Mediated Pharmacokinetics in the Chinese Population: Opportunities and Challenges
The past decade has witnessed rapid progress of China’s new drug research and development and healthcare. By 2015, China is expected to be the world’s second largest pharmaceutical market after the USA [1]. Thus, domestic and international pharmaceutical industrial organizations are working to satisfy the medical needs of China, which is one of the key emerging markets. It is not surprising that large-cap pharmaceutical companies are now taking steps to reduce the delay between registration of new intellectual property-protected medicines in China relative to the USA and Europe. Since race diversity might lead to differences in dose–response relationships with respect to safety and efficacy, clinical pharmacologic evaluations should include volunteers from relevant countries that are representative of the target therapeutic populations [2]. For many drugs, ethnic sensitivity of drug pharmacokinetics may account for much of the variability in the dose–response relationship [3]. Physiologically based pharmacokinetics (PBPK) combined with in vitro–in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) offer a unique opportunity to integrate ethnic-specific demographic, physiological, biological and genetic knowledge into a mechanistic framework to predict drug exposure and disposition in different ethnic populations. Recently, Barter et al. [4] developed an ethnic-specific PBPK–IVIVE model using their Chinese population database, and they systematically evaluated its performance in predicting cytochrome P450 (CYP)-mediated pharmacokinetics in the Chinese population. Another work by Lu et al. [5], recently published in the same journal, reported the first attempt to incorporate demographic information on a Chinese population into an IVIVE pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic model to project the maintenance dosage of warfarin (a substrate of CYP2C9) in Han Chinese patients. These approaches provided novel insights into how to predict the impact of ethnic differences between Chinese and Caucasian subjects on CYP-mediated pharmacokinetics. It must be noted, however, that these models are based on the authors’ current knowledge and experience; therefore, they should be considered as evolving models, which may be expected to be re-evaluated and modified in the light of available updated knowledge. As new data concerning critical physiological and genetic knowledge arise, updates of PBPK and IVIVE models should rapidly follow. Here, we summarize several key anatomical, physiological and genetic information on Chinese individuals from the Chinese-language literature (Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure, 1979–2013), which we believe at this time would be useful to those interested in applying the models mentioned above. Furthermore, we discuss several challenges and assumptions behind these models that are well worth bringing to readers’ attention.
