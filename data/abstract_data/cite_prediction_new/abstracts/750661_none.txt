Mechanical and geometric stability of shape-adapted bioabsorbable plates.
B ioabsorbable plates, mesh panels, and sheets are typically provided by manufacturers in a flat, planar, configuration. However, part of the clinical utility of these devices lies in their ability to be three-dimensionally shaped, intraoperatively, to fit the anatomy. Often, this involves heating to soften them, then manually shaping, and holding them in this configuration until cool. For some types of devices, namely, self-reinforced or oriented, cold bending can be performed, although this has limitations. The process of three-dimensional adaptation of bioabsorbable devices, especially via heating, was reviewed by Pietrzak. Two phenomena are well known to exist after adaptation via the heating method, namely, (1) a memory effect in which the devices tend to revert, at least to some degree, back to their original planar state over time when submersed, unconstrained, in buffer at 37-C, and (2) an initial reduction in modulus which quickly recovers to its initial value within hours of placement in this environment. The molecular events that contribute to these phenomena have been described. Recently, Varghai et al examined the geometric stability of a variety of bioabsorbable plates of varying thickness and polymer formulation from several manufacturers. Their motivation was the need to better understand the three-dimensional stability of bioabsorbable devices for potential application in craniofacial cartilage molding procedures. Briefly, the plates were cut into 2 2-cm squares, then symmetrically bent over a right-angle aluminum template to create an angle height of 7 mm. The manufacturer’s bending instructions regarding the use of heat (or not) were followed. The bent plates were then placed in a 37-C water bath with the apex of the bend facing upward. The apex of the right-angle fold was loaded with an 80-g weight, and the angle height was measured during a 2-week interval to follow its change with time. For 1 type of plate, the same previously tested plates were similarly bent again and tested for 2 additional weeks. This cycle was then repeated for a third, and final, 2-week interval. The results were as follows: (1) during the first 2-week interval, each plate lost some, or all, of its initial 7-mm angle height because of flattening; (2) the tendency to lose the angle height was more pronounced as the number of cycles increased; and (3) there was a trend toward better shape stability with increased plate thickness. The authors concluded that further research and development by industry may lead to implants with mechanical properties better suited to soft tissue molding procedures. This study was the first to investigate the effects of a static load on the geometric stability of three-dimensionally shape-adapted bioabsorbable plates, which has important implications regarding their use for soft tissue molding procedures. While the results speak for themselves, it is instructive to interpret them in a theoretical context. There are several mechanisms that undoubtedly contributed to the loss of angle height. First, polymers, in general, exhibit the phenomenon of creep in which they will deform, over time, in response to an applied static load. This is due to the force-induced slippage of polymer chains past each other. Second, after hot bending, the modulus of bioabsorbable plates is initially reduced but may recover in as little as a few hours in water at 37-C. This temporary reduction in stiffness could have contributed to the early reduction in angle height observed for some of the plates. Third, bioabsorbable plates that are bent, especially those that have heat applied in the process, tend to revert at least partially back to their original planar configuration if placed unconstrained in water at 37-C. This is due, at least in part, as a response to the tensile and compressive forces that reside in the device on the convex and concave sides of the bend, respectively. Fourth, during the 2-week interval in water, hydrolysis of the polymer chains would have resulted in a reduction in the polymer molecular weight, facilitating chain mobility. Also, infiltration of water into the polymer would have provided lubrication to the polymer chains, enhancing mobility. Some combination of these mechanisms contributed to the loss of angle height for each of the plates. For the one type of heat-adapted plate that underwent 3 successive hot bending and 2-week test cycles, it was found that the decrease in angle height was more rapid as the number of cycles increased. Heat adaptation is generally a reversible process, so this was likely due to the progressive degradation of the polymer that occurred, reflective of 2, 4, and 6 weeks of hydrolysis for the 3 cycles, respectively. However, it also must be remembered that when the plates were hot bent for the second and third time, they had already undergone some measure of hydrolysis and were not in their initial, as-manufactured state. Although this was an interesting in vitro experiment to have performed, it does not necessarily correlate with clinical practice because once an adapted plate is placed in the patient, it is not typically removed at a later date and readapted. The observation that thicker plates tended to exhibit better shape stability was described as a trend. Because the results were not statistically analyzed, this is at best a preliminary finding. That said, it is reasonable that thicker plates would be generally stronger than thinner plates and would better withstand external, static loads. When affixed to bone with screws or rivets, an anatomically bent bioabsorbable plate will be under significant constraint to prevent it from reverting back to its original shape. However, with less constraint as might occur for soft tissue molding procedures, this could be a concern.
