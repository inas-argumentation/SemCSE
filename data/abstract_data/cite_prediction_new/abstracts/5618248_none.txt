The metabolic syndrome, testosterone deficiency and erectile dysfunction never come alone
In their paper, Yassin et al. (2008) present a broader concept, the ‘metabolic syndrome, testosterone deficiency and erectile dysfunction never come alone’. The understanding that the male hypogonadal state is associated with other related disease components is important because it leads to a better appreciation and treatment of the whole patient. This can be viewed as the hypogonadal-metabolic-atherogenic disease and ageing connection (Cohen, 2001). Furthermore, it supports the concept that the hypogonadal state participates in both the genesis and evolution of much of the spectrum of obesity and metabolic related cardiovascular states in men. While these explanations are adequate, there are other issues to be considered as part of the extended panorama of the testosterone deficiency state. A constant finding that occurs with both the obesity related conditions and ageing is the relative increase of adipose tissue compared to lean body mass. The increased fat accumulation and storage is fuelled by caloric excess (and also by decreased energy expenditure) and results in abnormalities of metabolic regulation. Visceral adiposity, along with an expanded total body fat mass, is associated with increased aromatase activity, which results in a greater conversion of testosterone to oestradiol. The decreased testosterone concentration then allows for the preferential deposition of abdominal/visceral fat (Marin et al., 1995), whereas the increased oestradiol production eventually leads to the feedback inhibition of LH. This results in a hypogonadal-obesity cycle (Cohen, 1999) and, over time, to a progressive testosterone deficiency state. The metabolic and cardiovascular effects of testosterone deficiency have been clearly defined in two disease states: Klinefelter’s syndrome and androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) for prostate cancer. It is reported that 44% of patients with Klinefelter’s syndrome (Bojesen et al., 2006) and estimated that 50% of men receiving ADT (Braga-Basaria et al., 2006) develop the metabolic syndrome. Furthermore, lesser degrees of hypotestosteronaemia might be considered to be a risk factor for the metabolic syndrome, even when fewer diagnostic components are found among the clinical and metabolic profiles of male patients. This strongly suggests that many of the clinical manifestations of the metabolic syndrome in men are, in part, the result of deficient sex hormonal activity rather than of insulin resistance alone. Finally, many of the obesity associated states can present with symptoms of erectile dysfunction, infertility, coronary artery disease as well as metabolic abnormalities such as glucose intolerance, with or without the metabolic syndrome, and all require evaluation for the hypogonadal state. It is now apparent that there is a spectrum of metabolic conditions and that hypotestosteronaemia may be just one component of a hypoandrogenic-anabolic-deficiency state (Cohen, 2008). Therefore, Yassin et al. (2008) are correct in stating that the ‘metabolic syndrome, testosterone deficiency and erectile dysfunction never come alone’.
