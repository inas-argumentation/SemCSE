Optimizing Injection Technique to Minimize Ecchymosis in Blepharoplasty—Reply
Optimizing Injection Technique to Minimize Ecchymosis in Upper Blepharoplasty To the Editor We read with interest the article by Yu et al1 titled “Occurrence of Bruise, Hematoma, and Pain in Upper Blepharoplasty Using Blunt-Needle vs Sharp-Needle Anesthetic Injection in Upper Blepharoplasty: A Randomized Clinical Trial.” In the article, the authors describe the rate of ecchymosis, hematoma, and pain when using a 27-gauge, 1.4-in (35-mm) needle compared with the use of a 27-gauge, 2-in cannula. They report lower rates of hematoma formation when using the cannula compared with their standard needle injection technique. We submit that their control injection technique was not optimized for reducing the critical outcome studied. In 2015, our group performed more than 4000 blepharoplasties. Our typical anesthesia protocol differs substantially from the control technique used by Yu et al.1 Ten minutes prior to the injection we start by applying a topical anesthetic composed of benzocaine, lidocaine, and tetracaine. We then inject our anesthetic mixture using a 30-gauge needle on a 3-mL syringe. The mixture is prepared in advance and consists of 1 mL of hyaluronidase and 5 mL of 2.4 mEQ sodium bicarbonate mixed into 50 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100 000 epinephrine, which is combined 1:1 with 0.5% bupivacaine. When injecting, we position the needle with the bevel directed anteriorly, placing the tip of the needle into the thin eyelid skin in the immediate subcutaneous plane superficial to the orbicularis muscle, taking care to avoid visible subcutaneous vessels. The skin in this area is less than 1 mm thick,2-4 but there is a relatively avascular potential space between the epithelium and orbicularis muscle. The anesthetic is delivered slowly keeping the needle tip visible using a single, central, entry site immediately cephalad to the eyelid crease. The mechanism of hematoma and bruise formation in upper eyelid injection is typically orbicularis and/or subcutaneous vessel injury from the needle. Other possible causes of bleeding are injury to small blood vessels in the skin and subcutaneous tissues. We believe that our technique is an effective means of minimizing ecchymosis for the following reasons. The small-caliber needle (30-gauge) with a short length (0.5-in) allows for exquisite control of the tip and direction of the needle, avoiding injury to the orbicularis muscle. We have found that a longer and larger-bore needle, such as the 27-gauge, 1.4-in needle used by Yu et al1 is more difficult to control, with higher risk of deeper penetration that is more likely to injure the orbicularis and/or vessels. Furthermore, supplementing the anesthetic with hyaluronidase allows for a single injection site to facilitate delivery of anesthetic throughout the tissues to points distant from the initial injection site. Minimizing multiple injection sites decreases the overall risk of ecchymosis. The specific control injection technique was not discussed by Yu et al,1 and it is possible that their technique involved the more typical multiple injection technique. We thank the authors for an interesting and clinically relevant study. We hope that the collective experience offered by our practice expressed in this letter may aid blepharoplasty surgeons in decreasing postoperative ecchymosis.
