Think precarity, not frailty in care for older people.
I’ve recently returned from attending the European Sociological Association’s “Ageing in Europe”workshop held at the University of Chester, United Kingdom (U.K.) in synch with the University of Chester College of Nursing’s Global Healthcare Conference: “Reframing Healthcare for an Ageing Population.” I had the good fortune to provide keynote addresses at both conferences and thought to share some transatlantic insights in this column. Engaging with gerontology colleagues in the U.K. reinforced that the challenges we face in the United States in providing consistently high quality, individualized, late life and end-of-life care are very similar in the U.K., although our systems of care differ. Our European colleagues are deeply concerned about regional variation in care accessibility and quality, communication impairments, family caregiving, and an aging workforce. They also feel the tension between a desire to live fully and preparedness at end-of-life for older adults and their family caregivers. Three recent commissioned studies provide data on family caregiving in the USA and the U.K. Approximately, 34.2 million Americans provided unpaid care to an adult 50 years of age.1 The caregivers are both women (60%) and men (40%). Spouses are more likely to be higher-hour caregivers and over half (57%) report assisting in “medical/nursing tasks,” (e.g., injections, tube feedings, catheter/colostomy care, etc) and coordinating care (53%).1,2 In England, 22% of woman aged 55e59 years of age provide family caregiving with the most common recipient in the ‘oldest old’ (80 þ years) age category.3 A majority of care recipients (66% of women & 52% of men) receive help with mobility, self-care, or instrumental activities of daily living.
