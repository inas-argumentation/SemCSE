Tissue cells in vivo are constantly immersed in protein-rich fluids, and a dynamic balance may exist between extracellular and intracellular pro tein (18, 19). In view of these facts, it is perhaps surprising that the role of proteins in the main tenance and growth of cells in tissue culture has received relatively little attention in recent years. A major trend has, in fact, been the development of protein-free synthetic media (3, 9, 13, 20) or of semisynthetic nutrients with minimal amounts of added protein (1). The object of these studies has not been to avoid the study of proteins as such but rather to eliminate all components of the culture media which could not be completely defined. The utility of this approach has been amply demonstrated (1, 3), and a considerable array of information now exists on the role of crystalloidal factors for cell growth in media of minimal protein content (1). It should be emphasized, however, that cells in tissue culture will not in general grow progres sively in the absence of protein. In rare instances when this goal has been achieved (3), the cell strains concerned have been carried for long pe riods in vitro, subject to mutation and/or selection of a special cell type finally able to proliferate without added protein. Although the mechanics of this change remain to be worked out, the appearance of nutritional variants is consistent with other lines of evidence for variation in es tablished cell strains.
