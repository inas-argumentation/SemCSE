Mixing studies for abnormal coagulation screen – the current trend
Mixing studies are performed when the result of a patient’s coagulation screen is abnormal. It is useful as a screening test to identify the cause of an abnormal screen and is performed by mixing the plasma of the patient with pooled normal plasma, and then repeating the prothrombin time (PT) and/or an activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). If the mixing test results normalize, it is likely that the patient has a clotting factor deficiency. If the mixing test results do not normalize, the patient is likely to have an inhibitor (whether it is specific to a certain factor such as FVIII in acquired hemophilia, or nonspecific like lupus anticoagulants). Further investigation is needed in both cases. In that sense, the mixing test is considered a firstline investigation of prolonged clotting time [1]. In this study, we studied all the mixing test requests in a tertiary referral center and noted the reasons for requesting the clotting screen, and the impact the mixing study had on the patient management. The institution’s laboratory protocol is that any patient noted to have an abnormal PT and/or APTT for the first time during a hospital admission or an outpatient attendance would automatically have the mixing study performed without the need for an additional request. In these cases, the results are reported with the comment about the need for the next appropriate test based on the clinical situation of the patient (factor assays or inhibitor screen for patients with bleeding and lupus anticoagulant for patients with thrombosis). Our laboratory uses a lupus-insensitive reagent for both PT and APTT tests. Records of all mixing tests that were performed in a 3-month period were collected which included 912 tests. Detailed information was obtained on the first 200 patients that had one (PT or APTT) or both mixing tests done. The study was approved by the hospital audit committee as a service improvement project while all data were anonymised. Two hundred patients had a total of 279 mixing tests done. One hundred and fourteen were PT mixing tests, while 165 were APTT mixing tests (some patients had both tests done). Out of the 114 PT mixing tests, 17 (14.9%) corrected fully, 95 (83.3%) corrected partially while two (1.8%) did not correct. Out of the 165 APTT mixing tests, 83 (50.3%) corrected fully, 80 (48.5%) corrected partially while two did not correct. A very large number of tests requested had “routine checkup”, “unwell patient”, or in some cases, no specific reasons for the test documented. A pre-operative check was the documented reason for 46 tests done in 33 patients. Among the 46 tests, 94.1% of the patients had their PT mixing study partially corrected while the rest corrected fully while the percentages were 58.6% and 41.4% for full and partial correction for APTT mixing study. In the case of sepsis, the commonest identifiable reason in 61 tests performed in 44 patients, the partial, full and no correction for PT and APTT mixing studies were 78.3%, 19.5% and 2.2% and 76.3%, 22.2% and 1.5%, respectively. As the mixing study is only a screening test, ideally it should be followed by further testing as mentioned before. However, only four of the 200 patients had the lupus anticoagulant tested and three were confirmed as having a positive result. The rest of the patients did not have any follow-up testing i.e. factor assays or lupus anticoagulant or inhibitors, which brings up the concerning situation of how the results of clotting screen (and mixing studies) were interpreted.
