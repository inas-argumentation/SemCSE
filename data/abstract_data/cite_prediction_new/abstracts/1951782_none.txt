Rethinking “Out of Control” As “New Rules” for the New Economy
A s the generation that heralded the Age of Aquarius matures into the people who manage, own, and use America Online, it perhaps is no sur prise that someone like Kevin Kelly has been anointed as the voice of today’s new informational economy. A former editor of The Whole Earth Review ; a cofounder of the Bay area’s first virtual community, the WELL; an impresario of net-centered happenings; and now the editor-at-large of Wiredmagazine; Kelly staked out this high-profile position in two middle-brow treatises: Out of Control: The New Biology of Machines, Social Systems, and the Economic World (1994) andNew Rules for the New Economy (1998). Best and Kellner adroitly position Kelly’s work in several contexts with their article in Organization & Environment (1999). By questioning many of his intellectual assumptions and challenging most of his economic conclusions, they correctly characterize Kelly as the prime exponent of Silicon Valley’s hi-tech, “soft capitalism.” I want to refine their critique by amplifying and adding to doubts they express about Kelly’s analyses of the present moment. Most importantly, Kelly’s unique position at the conjunction of many influential networks in California’s information-based economy, as well as his celebrated status as the networked economy’s greatest prophet, allows me to see his work as a mass-market version of today’s key ruling ideas. As Marx and Engels (1947) suggest in The German Ideol ogy, such ideas are “nothing more than the ideal expression of the dominant mate rial relationships,” and Kelly’s ideas about the new economy aptly capture the dominant material relationships of soft capitalism seeking “to represent its interest as the common interest of all members of society, put in an ideal form” and thereby giving “its ideas the form of universality” represented as “the only rational, univer sally valid ones” (pp. 39, 41). These tendencies appear particularly pronounced in Kelly’s celebration of neobiological controllessness, his naturalizing transforma tion of technology to an all-knowing, always moving, identical subject-object, and his posthumanist celebration of chaos as an equalizing form of organization. Like Alvin Toffler (1980), John Naisbitt (1982), George Gilder (1989), and Francis Fukuyama (1992), Kelly’s work spins out an end-of-history narrative, and it touts the world-historical significance of Joseph Schumpeter’s vision of markets as engines for creative destruction. The most destructive creations of soft capital ism, for Kelly, are informational networks, and allegedly their biologics are rewrit ing, for all of us, a new history of our collective economic, moral, and political ends.
