Infections in Splenectomised Patients — How to Prevent
THE PAPER by Hassan and colleagues I in this issue of the Scottish Medical Journal renews the debate about the merits oflong-term lifelong prophylactic penicllin in splenectomised patients. For many years the risk of overwhelming infection and potential death following splenectomy has been well recognised. A study of 1490 splenectomised patients in Western Australiarevealed a 12.6 fold increased risk of severe late post-splenectomy infection compared with the general population. The risk ofinfection is greatest in children.v' when spelencetomy is performed for underlying pathology as opposed to removal ofa normal spleen for example following a road traffic accident" and, within the first few years of splenectomy.' However, many published studies confirm that there exists a small but significant lifelong long susceptibility to sepsis" even following removal of a normal spleen."Ifwe know that patients risk of infection persists into adulthood, albeit at a lower grade, then we need to identify what range of pathogens are they susceptible to, how best may we protect the patients from these infections and importantly for what duration should some ofthese protective measures be recommended given that we have very little good scientific data to support routine measures such as life long penicillin chemoprophylaxis and how we can usefully measure the long term immunity afforded by pneumococcal and other vaccines. Fifty to sixty per cent of infections are due to Streptococcus pneumoniae followed by Haemophilus influenzae' (particularly in children). Infections with Neisseria meninigitidis, Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are the next most prevalent; the latter two, though less common, are associated with the highest mortality.? Other infections reported in splenectomised individuals include Capnocytophaga canimorsus (DF2) infections following trivial scratches by dogs,"malaria and tick borne babesiosis, particularly the strain that is endemic in certain European countries. Ideally preservation of the spleen by conservative management of splenic injuries or salvaging some part of the spleen (partial splencetomy) during an operation is the best hope of trying to offer some long term protection against subsequent serious sepsis as heterotropic autotransplantation of splenic tissue still requires subsequent vaccination and antibiotic chemoprophylaxis.'? Unfortunately, in a majority ofcases splenectomy is unavoidable and we are reliant on vaccination and antibiotic chemoprophylaxis. Despite the availability of pneumococcal vaccination a recent study by Kinnersley et al" revealed that a large group ofpatients who have had a splenectomy remain unvaccinated. Furthermore although there is general acceptance that immunisation works there are some inconsistencies regarding the frequency of routine pneumococcal reimmunisation, the sub-group of patients who are most likely to benefit from H injl.uenzae vaccine and above all the use and duration of penicillin chemoprophylaxis.
