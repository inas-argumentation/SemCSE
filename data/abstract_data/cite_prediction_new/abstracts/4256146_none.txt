Impact of insulin delivery devices in diabetes care.
The recent developments in technologies and new therapeutic options have significantly improved diabetes control. Studies have shown that reducing A1c levels can delay and=or reduce the overall risk of both microand macrovascular complications associated with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. The improved delivery devices for insulin treatment, using continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) (insulin pumps) and insulin pens, have increased patient compliance and acceptance of an intensive insulin strategy in the majority of the patients. Implementation of intensive insulin treatment using these devices is expensive, although there is a significant reduction in the risk of long-term complications and thus the cost. The basal-bolus treatment strategy can be implemented using multiple daily injections (MDI) per day using insulin pens. This has been further elaborated by Yoeri Luijf and Jans DeVries in this supplement. Both insulin glargine and detemir are available as insulin pens (disposable and=or cartridges) and are easy to teach to patients to deliver insulin as discussed by Virginia Valentine and Davida Kruger. In fact, the majority of Western Europe’s insulin-requiring patients with both type 1 and 2 diabetes use insulin pens. However, the use of insulin pens in the United States is not that widely accepted, and this may, in part, be due to lack of health maintenance organization (HMO)=insurance coverage or coverage with a higher co-pay for patients. For example, Kaiser Permanente, a large HMO, Medicare, and Medicaid do not reimburse for insulin pens. All three rapid-acting insulin analogs—insulin lispro, aspart, and glulisine—are also available in disposable pens with different memory features in some pens. The barrier of higher cost to patients and=or lack of reimbursements for pens needs to be eliminated so that more patients could reach their target A1c values by accepting intensification of diabetes treatment. These cost barriers have been discussed at length by Carl Asche, Laura ShaneMcWhorter, and Swetha Raparla in this supplement. The ideal basal-bolus treatment can be best implemented using insulin pumps. Several independent studies and meta-analyses using insulin pumps in patients with type 1 diabetes have shown reductions in total insulin dose, decreased numbers of hypoglycemic episodes, decreased glucose variability, and significantly better glucose control (A1c) compared to MDI. A majority of HMOs and other payors in the United States reimburse pump therapy for patients with type 1 diabetes in the United States. In total, about 700,000 patients worldwide use insulin pumps, with a majority of the users in the United States (16–20% of patients with type 1 diabetes). Insulin pump use in pregnancy and in children is discussed in detail by Adrienne Wollitzer and co-workers and David Maahs and colleagues, respectively. CSII=pump use is also gradually increasing significantly in Europe. The use of insulin pumps in type 2 diabetes has been very limited. There are about 4.5 million patients with type 2 diabetes in the United States who require insulin treatment for their diabetes. However, only about 37,000 patients with type 2 diabetes are using an insulin pump, partly due to poor reimbursement. For example, even in insulin-requiring patients with type 2 diabetes on MDI, Medicare and Medicaid require that patients be C-peptide negative, which is rarely the case in type 2 diabetes. The significant reduction in hypoglycemia with the use of CSII is likely to offset the increased upfront cost of initiating pump treatment as discussed by Bruce Bode in this supplement. It is important to consider increased upfront costs and risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) if patients do not monitor their glucose regularly at home and=or use a continuous glucose monitor (CGM). The proper education of patients is essential while initiating pump therapy and is the key to a successful outcome. This area has been discussed by Irl Hirsch in this supplement. The education must include not only going over pump features (‘‘bells and whistles’’), but must also include education about CSII and how to adjust insulin dose when sick (sick-day management on CSII), the risk of DKA, and the need for regular blood glucose monitoring at home. Most patients who use CGMs all of the time (at least 6 days=week) significantly improve A1c values with less time spent in hypoglycemia. In the near future (5–10 years) we may start seeing the use of semior closed-loop systems for insulin-requiring patients. The combination insulin pumps and CGM-guided auto-shutoff pumps (in case of hypoglycemia) are already approved in Europe, and many recent studies have shown a significant reduction of nocturnal hypoglycemia. The use of these pumps is not approved for use in the United States by the Food and Drug Administration; however, several studies are underway to get such approval. For a near perfect closed-loop to happen sooner, the CGM technology must improve its accuracy, and new algorithms need to be developed along with the need for developing ultra-fast-acting insulins.
