The Political Economy of Inflation and Currency Depreciation in Lebanon, 1984-92
Lebanon experienced recurrent currency crises and inflationary episodes in the 1980s and early 1990s. The extent of the crises can be seen by the trend and volatility of inflation and the value of the Lebanese pound during the period 1984–92. Inflation increased from 6.6 per cent in 1983 to 120 per cent in 1992, with very high inflation registered in the years 1987 and 1988. The exchange rate collapsed from 4.53 Lebanese pounds per dollar in 1984 to 1,838 Lebanese pounds per dollar at the end of 1992. These events have been distressing in a country that traditionally considered monetary and financial stability as an essential element of its economic development policy and has a deeply entrenched culture of an independent central bank, a free financial system and anti-inflationary bias. The crisis in effect destroyed the major pillars of the pre-civil war Lebanese ancien régime: a strong currency and price stability. The existing literature has attributed Lebanon’s currency crisis to the following factors: civil war, inflationary finance practices of the state and currency substitution. These single-event explanations fall short of investigating the political-economic factors that made inflation more than a transitory phenomena in an economy highly committed to deflationary bias and financial stability. The major thesis of this article is that the crises were generated by a political process engendered by the role of finance, industry, the state and the central bank in creating an environment conducive to inflation and currency collapse. This environment was created by the interplay of inflationary state finance forced by the fiscal crisis of the state, the interest of the financial and industrial sectors in a depreciated currency and the inability of the central bank to defend the currency. This article briefly reviews the literature on the new political economy models of inflation, exchange rate determination and central banking.
