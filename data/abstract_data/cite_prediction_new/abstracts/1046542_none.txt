Enzootic nasal tumour virus demonstrated in sheep in Senegal by direct detection of provirus from tumour DNA
IN sheep and goats, enzootic intranasal adenocarcinoma (EIA) has been shown to be associated with the infectious betaretrovirus enzootic nasal tumour virus (ENTV) (De las Heras and others 1991, 1995, Ortin and others 2003). The aetiological diagnosis of ENTV infection is hampered by three major issues, but findings concerning the jaagsiekte sheep retrovirus (JSRV), a closely related sheep betaretrovirus responsible for lung adenocarcinoma, may be relevant to ENTV. First, these natural ovine betaretroviruses do not propagate in vitro (Palmarini and others 1999). Secondly, seroconversion does not occur after infection, possibly because of the partial expression of sheep endogenous retroviruses (SERVs) (Palmarini and others 2001) or because of a virus-induced impairment of immune functions (Kwang and others 1995, Rosati and others 1996, Ortin and others 1998, Summers and others 2002). Thirdly, the identification of the JRSV and ENTV genomes is made difficult because of the numerous SERV sequences, which interfere with molecular detection assays. The SERV subgroup which is transmitted genetically, consists of a set of 15 to 20 copies of proviral DNA sequences which inserted into the ovine genome an estimated two million years ago (Palmarini and others 2000, 2001). This short communication describes the demonstration of ENTV in sheep in Senegal by the direct detection of provirus from tumour DNA. During 2002, veterinarians in the suburb of Dakar, Senegal, followed eight fatal cases of progressive respiratory distress in small-sized flocks of highly inbred local sheep of high value. Details of the flocks and cases are summarised in Table 1. The age of the affected animals ranged from 15 months to four years. Both Sahelian breeds (Laadoum and Touabir) were affected. Initial manifestations were abundant bilateral nasal discharge accompanied by dyspnoea. In some cases, extension to the frontal sinus was observed, culminating in leakage of mucopurulent matter through a fistula in the frontal bone. Although the progression of the disease was chronic and apyretic, possible bacterial and parasitic infections were considered as the cause. The animals were treated with dexamethasone (Dexadreson; Intervet), phenylbutasone (Phenylarthrite Injectable; Vetoquinol), ivermectin (Ivomec Ovin; Merial), spiramycin (Suanovil 20, Merial) or oxytetracycline (Oxytétracycline 10 per cent; Vetoquinol) without any effect. The sheep progressively lost weight, and all eventually died or were culled within three to four months. At postmortem examination, longitudinal sections of the heads revealed tumours growing in the nasal cavities and restricted to the ethmoturbinate region. An abundant seromucosal exudate was invading the whole nasal cavity. There was no evidence of metastases either locally or in distant areas. The singular mass of nasal tumour tissue grew bilaterally, originating from the ethmoturbinates and invading the nostrils. The tumour tissue was grey, soft and friable. Histopathological examination of sections of neoplastic tissue revealed a tubulopapillary adenocarcinoma. Based on the macroscopic and histological findings, EIA was suspected. Fragments of tissues (10 to 20 mg) were collected from two culled animals, sheep 1 and sheep 2, using separate sterile instruments, in the following order: lung, retro-orbital lymph nodes (RLN) and nasal tumour tissues; the samples were all snap-frozen. DNA was prepared by digestion of the tissues with proteinase K according to the method of Lena and Luciw (2001). The presence of endogenous betaretrovirus sequences in the genome of Sahelian sheep was assessed by PCR amplification using the published universal primers P1 and P2 (Cousens and others 1996, Palmarini and others 1996) based on a conserved motif in the gag gene. The primers are described in Table 2. Amplicons of the expected size (229 base pairs) were obtained from each tissue sample analysed, including buffy coat cells from a control Touabir sheep originating from an unaffected herd (Table 2). Additionally, PCR products were resistant to digestion with PstI and AatII endonucleases (Cousens and others 1996). The detection of amplicons from each tissue examined, together with PstI and AatII resistance, demonstrated the presence of SERVs integrated into the genome of these Sahelian ovine breeds, and the availability of each DNA extract for PCR. To investigate the presence of ENTV DNA within the tissues obtained from affected animals, the primers TM-1S and U3A1 were designed (Table 2) on the basis of marked differences between ENTV, JSRV and SERVs in the region of the viral genome spanning from the 3′ end of env gene to the long terminal repeats (Cousens and others 1999, Palmarini and others 2000). This set of primers was used to amplify the ENTV target sequence directly from DNA extracted from the tissues. Amplicons of the expected size were obtained from the DNA extracted from tumour tissues from both affected sheep examined, but not from each of the other samples (Table 2). These distribution data agree with those published by Ortin and others (2003), where a different ovine ENTV-specific PCR was employed. The amplicons were sequenced with the PCR primers; the sequences have been assigned the GenBank accession numbers AY271312 and AY271313.
