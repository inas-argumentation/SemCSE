Anaemia prevention in the community: students as change agents
What problems were addressed? This project addressed the questions: how can we teach medical students key skills of professionalism in a meaningful, clinically relevant and lasting way? How can we teach medical students to be effective communicators, to be advocates for their patients, and to respect and consider patient perspectives and values? How can we promote the development of doctors who are self-aware and self-critical in their practice of medicine, who can reflect on each of their clinical experiences and modify their approach for subsequent experiences? What was tried? The Professional Competencies Toolkit (PC Toolkit) was developed to give medical students a structure on which to base their clinical experiences. This is a set of 28 flash cards, grouped within six domains of professional competency. Each card is a tool for learning; it defines a topic in a catchy title and lays out a specific set of skills to be acquired within the clinical setting. Students are given ‘tasks’ and ‘tips’ to guide them through the process of clinical observation, self-observation and interaction with patients and colleagues. For example, one of the cards, titled ‘The Invisible Patient’, is designed to show students how patients can be rendered invisible in the clinical setting and to teach students how to combat this common problem. Each student is asked to take a card into his or her next set of clinical experiences and to focus on the issue on that card with the goal of bringing a story back to the group every 2 weeks for further discussion. What lessons were learned? The narratives that have emerged through the use of the PC Toolkit cards have been truly rich and rewarding, and have provided excellent opportunities for in-depth discussions of a broad array of issues related to professionalism and what it means to be a ‘good doctor’. The exchange of narratives in the small-group setting has led to group problem solving, the validation of students’ experiences and values, discussion of the origin and nature of negative behaviours and attitudes that can become normalised in patient care, and the assertion of the primacy of patient-centred care and patient autonomy. Our experience of using the PC Toolkit has so far led us to conclude that the teaching of reflective practice can be successfully integrated into students’ clinical experiences. The PC Toolkit enables the teaching of broad, complex, often abstract concepts that define professionalism, in an applied, skillsbased way, and in small, manageable learning ‘bites’. Guiding students to focus on one or two basic elements within their clinical encounters allows them to develop a deep understanding of the importance and complexity of the broader issue in a way that links the issue to human faces: the faces of their patients. When students are given a tool that enables them to reflect on the encounter as it unfolds, the clinical learning experience becomes rich and serves as the basis of very memorable narratives that can be used for discussion. These reflective skills can be practised and reiterated in subsequent clinical experiences such that the skills become habitual and integral to the student’s professional identity.
