Crack-mediated Release of Encapsulated Bacteria-based Agent Makes Concrete Self-healing
In 2006 a research program was launched at Delft University of Technology aiming for the development of a new class of materials, i.e. materials with an inbuilt healing mechanism. The idea is that these novel materials can selfrepair damage resulting in substantially decreased maintenance and repair costs and increased service life. Several research projects focus on cement-based materials such as concrete and asphalt. One project that will be discussed here concerns bacterial-based concrete. The bacteria, which are added to the concrete mixture in form of encapsulated particles, are able to produce calcium carbonatebased minerals, a process that results in sealing and water tightening of cracks. These and more examples show that novel materials and constructions which are designed to control damage rather than prevent damage by featuring an inbuilt healing mechanism could be more economical than traditional ones. INTRODUCTION Micro crack formation in concrete results in increased permeability of the material matrix. This process eventually results in durability problems as the service life of constructions may be reduced due to premature reinforcement corrosion and leakage problems. Manual repair of damaged concrete is the traditional solution to restore strength loss and water tightness. A novel alternative strategy is implementation of a self-healing system into concrete constructions [1-2]. One specific type of self-healing concrete currently under development at the Delft University concerns an inbuilt encapsulated system of limestone-producing bacteria. Objective of this project is to develop an autonomous crack-healing concrete which features increased durability and prolonged service life compared to traditional concretes. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS The principle mechanism of bacterial crack healing is that the bacteria themselves act as a catalyst transforming an organic precursor compound into a mineral, limestone-based, crack-filling material. Both components, i.e. bacterial spores and organic nutrients, are encapsulated in porous expanded clay particles prior to addition to the concrete mixture. Concrete test specimens were prepared in which part of the aggregate material, i.e. the 1-4 mm size class, was replaced by similarly sized loaded expanded clay particles (bacterial spores 1.7x10 g-1 expanded clay particles, corresponding to 5x10 spores dm concrete, plus 15% w/w fraction calcium lactate, corresponding to 15g dm concrete). Control specimens had a similar aggregate composition but here expanded clay particles were not loaded with the bio-chemical agent. For testing of permeability improvement due to selfhealing, in both controland healing agent specimens a 0.15 mm wide crack was induced using a computer controlled Instron tensioncompression machine. Six replicates of each series were subsequently immersed for 4 weeks in tap water at room temperature to allow limestone formation to occur. Crack-healing of specimens was subsequently quantified by water permeability testing. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION The applied self-healing agent consists of two components: one is the bacteria, which mainly act as catalyst in a biochemical reaction, and the second is the mineral precursor component, in this case calcium lactate, which is converted by the bacteria to calcium carbonate based minerals: Ca(C3H5O2)2 + 7O2 → CaCO3 + 5CO2 + 5H2O The metabolically produced carbon dioxide can furthermore subsequently react with portlandite minerals (Ca(OH)2) present in the concrete matrix to produce more limestone: 5CO2 + 5Ca(OH)2 → 5CaCO3 + 5H2O The metabolic conversion of 1 molecule of calcium lactate thus yields 6 molecules of calcium carbonate.
