Diabetes: Bypassing the side effects of thiazolidinediones?
10.1038/nrd2363 Thiazolidinediones (TZDs) are the major drug class used to improve insulin sensitivity in the treatment of type 2 diabetes. However, TZDs have several adverse effects, including weight gain and renal and liver toxicity. Now, a recent study in Cell Metabolism describes the application of a novel phenotypic screening approach that identified the small molecule harmine, which exhibits beneficial effects similar to that of TZDs in animal models, but without the associated toxicity. The precise mechanism of action of TZDs is still under investigation. However, much of their antidiabetic action is thought to be mediated by its binding to the key adipogenic transcription factor, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ) in white adipose tissue (WAT). This results in the induction of genes involved in lipid and glucose metabolism. Tontonoz and colleagues developed a high-throughput screen based on pre-adipocytes, which were engineered with a reporter system activated by PPARγ, to search for small molecules that promote adipogenesis. Through this, they identified the β-carboline alkaloid, harmine. Cell-based studies designed to elucidate the mechanism of action of harmine demonstrated that, unlike TZDs, harmine is not a ligand for PPARγ but instead increases its expression with consequent elevated expression of PPARγ-target genes. This effect is mediated through the suppression of Wnt signalling, a key pathway known to inhibit adipocyte differentiation. These effects of harmine were also observed in mice — specifically in WAT. Next, the authors investigated whether harmine exhibited antidiabetic activity. In obese (db/db) mice, harmine reduced blood glucose, free fatty-acid and triglyceride levels, and delayed the development of hyperglycaemia, as well as improving glucose tolerance and response to insulin. However, despite the in vitro pro-adipogenic effect of harmine, no differences in body mass or adipose mass were observed, due to the modest activation of the PPARγ pathway, promotion of fatty-acid oxidation in WAT and increased energy expenditure. The beneficial effects of harmine on metabolic parameters correlated with the beneficial effects on both metabolic and inflammatory gene expression in WAT. Importantly, the ability of harmine to selectively increase PPARγ in WAT eliminated many of the toxic effects observed with TZDs, such as fluid retention and hepatic lipid accumulation as a result of PPARγ activation in the kidney and liver. When harmine was administered together with the TZD rosiglitazone, there was an additive effect on fasting blood glucose levels. This study illustrates the novel idea that a small-molecule regulator of PPARγ expression and inhibitor of Wnt signalling in adipose tissue may be used to treat diabetes. However, although toxic effects typically observed with TZDs were absent, harmine did evoke minor CNS side effects in mice and is known to be hallucinogenic in humans. Such CNS effects will need to be eliminated if this compound is to be considered as a potential antidiabetic drug.
