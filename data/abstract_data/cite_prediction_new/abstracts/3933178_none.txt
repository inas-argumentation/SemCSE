Theodor Storm's "The Rainmaiden": A Creative Process
In 1863-64, the German writer Theodor Storm (1817-88) wrote three fairy tales in succession: "Die Regentrude" (The Rainmaiden), "Bulemanns Haus" (Bulemann's House), and "Der Spiegel des Cyprianus" (The Mirror of Cyprianus). The conditions under which Storm composed these tales bears directly on the following interpretation of the "The Rainmaiden." Storm composed "The Rainmaiden" at Christmastime in 1863 while in political exile in Heiligenstadt and while he lay in bed with the measles. In a darkened room, disregarding his doctor's orders, he wrote the forty-nine pages of his story without interruption and completed them in final form within twelve days. Very pleased with his creation, Storm then immediately began work on "Bulemann's House" the idea for which had already been churning in his head during the composition of "The Rainmaiden" and finished the second story to his satisfaction by 17 January After its completion, he turned to "The Mirror of Cyprianus," which produced greater problems of composition than the two earlier tales and so did not appear in completed form until the end of 1864. In letters discussing the three stories, Storm emphasizes the psychological urgency that produced them and the fact that for twenty years he had longed for just such an ability to create fairy tales. He mentions as well that all three tales occurred to him almost simultaneously, that all were the pure products of his own imagination, and that "the almost demonic urge to write fairy tales" came over him despite, or perhaps because of, his concern over the disquieting political conditions in his native SchleswigHolstein.1
