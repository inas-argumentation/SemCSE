Incomplete POEMS syndrome presenting for years solely with scleredema and hyperpigmentation
Dear Editor, POEMS syndrome is a rare paraneoplastic syndrome, characterized by polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal gammopathy, and skin changes, including hyperpigmentation and skin thickening. Herein, we report a case of slowly progressing incomplete POEMS syndrome, which was initially considered as type 2 scleredema with diffuse hyperpigmentation due to monoclonal gammopathy with undetermined significance (MGUS) as reported by Minato et al. A 46-year-old woman presented with gradually progressing skin induration on her face, neck, shoulders and trunk, which started at the age of 43 years (Fig. 1). Diffuse hyperpigmentation of the skin was also seen over her whole body. The patient had no other skin lesions such as hemangioma, hypertrichosis, acrocyanosis or clubbed finger. The histology of her left forearm revealed the moderate increase in the melanin granules in the basal layer of the epidermis, thickened dermis, and the replacement of subcutaneous fat by pale and coarse collagen fibers. There were clefts between collagen fibers, however, Alcian blue staining at pH 2.5 failed to detect glycosaminoglycan deposition in the space (Fig. 2). Close investigation revealed increased serum immunoglobulin (Ig)A level to 683 mg/dL (normal, 93–426 mg/dL) with a slight increase in IgA lambda plasma cells in bone marrow (BM) (6%; normal, 0.2–1.7%). The patient had no history of exposure to drugs or past history of cosmetic operations. She had no metabolic or endocrine disorders including diabetes mellitus. Type 2 scleredema and diffuse hyperpigmentation with MGUS was diagnosed at that time, which was previously reported by Minato et al. Scleromyxedema was a differential diagnosis, but it was ruled out because the patient’s skin surface was quite smooth without any papulosis or erythema that is a characteristic of scleromyxedema. For the next 3 years, both skin induration and hyperpigmentation worsened and the patient started to experience difficulty in raising her arms and opening her mouth widely. In addition, numbness of the extremities developed gradually and a nerve conduction-screening test confirmed severe sensorimotor polyneuropathy at the age of 50 years. The serum IgA level was increased to 1584 mg/dL. In BM, IgA lambda plasma cells increased to 6.9%, with 2.1% of whole cells indicating DNA aneuploidy. The plasma vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) level at that time was 36 pg/mL (cut-off, 200 pg/mL). No lymphadenopathy or sclerotic bone lesions were observed. Together with severely progressing skin manifestations, polyneuropathy, IgA paraproteinemia and plasma cell dyscrasia in BM, it was difficult to attribute all the symptoms to MGUS. POEMS syndrome has many aspects in common with MGUS, however, it is separated from by the presence of at least one minor criterion of POEMS syndrome, such as skin changes. Hyperpigmentation is a common manifestation seen in POEMS syndrome but not in MGUS or type 2 scleredema. In POEMS syndrome, hyperpigmentation is present in more than half of patients. Although the precise pathomechanism has not been investigated yet, monoclonal gammopathy itself could cause hyperpigmentation.
