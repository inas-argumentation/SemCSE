Lack of clarity in reports of adverse events: Is there any harm?
In this issue of Pain, Cornelius et al. raise important concerns about the lack of clarity and consistency in the reporting of adverse events within clinical trials of interventions for neuropathic pain [1]. While withdrawals and serious adverse events are reasonably well reported, readers are often left in the dark as to the severity of most adverse events. Information on methods of ascertainment and frequency of monitoring for adverse events are not consistently available. Interpretation of adverse events data is made more opaque by the difficulty in distinguishing between total number of adverse events and total number of participants with events, bearing in mind the possibility that a single individual participant may have suffered several events while no one else did. This, of course is not the first time that poor reporting of adverse events has been highlighted [2], and nor will it be the last time. Hence, it is worth exploring the causes as well as the implications of inconsistent reporting. The principal difficulty stems from the fact that there is a diverse range of adverse events that can be encountered in a trial, as opposed to a single pre-specified beneficial outcome. Some of the adverse events may be pharmacologically predictable (and thus easily monitored and reported), but serious rare events can occur completely out of the blue. This creates problems for authors, both in reporting the methods as well as presenting the quantitative data, which could involve a multitude of events that may require several pages to fully report. Fitting the diversity of events into a single framework for authors poses some challenges. Fortunately, the increasing availability of online electronic supplements and web repositories means that a structured comprehensive model may eventually be developed and accepted. In order to progress, however, the framework proposed by Cornelius et al. should serve as a handy first step towards a laudable goal, but will need extensive discussion and consensus amongst all stakeholders. It is also the case that assessment and thorough reporting of adverse events is seldom considered to be a primary objective of the investigators. Most trials are designed with the main aim of assessing efficacy, while adverse effects are relegated to secondary or tertiary status (and hence reported in a less detailed fashion). There are, of course, some instances where assessment of harm is a key consideration, and in these cases, the adverse events are likely to have been pre-defined, carefully monitored, and fully reported. Nevertheless, most authors of trial reports are likely to first and foremost concentrate on writing the methods and results for the primary efficacy endpoint. Equally, peer reviewers and journal editors may have focused on sections of the manuscript that deal with benefit, and paid less attention to ensuring clear and transparent reporting of potential harms.
