Taiwan's 1998 Elections: Implications for Democratic Consolidation
Since the mid-1980s, four democracies have emerged (or been restored) in East Asia-those in the Philippines, Korea, Thailand, and Taiwan. Of these four, Taiwan's democratic development has been in many respects the most distinctive. Its regime transition was the most gradual, beginning with the lifting of martial law in 1987 and ending with the country's first direct presidential election in March 1996. Its democratization involved the least visible amount of rupture with the old authoritarian order. In fact, among some 60 democracies that have emerged since the third wave of democratization began in 1974, only in Taiwan has the former authoritarian ruling party held on to power (while maintaining its name and organization) through repeated competitive, open, and fair elections. Although Taiwan is now indisputably a democracy, and in many respects an impressively pluralistic, open, and spirited one, it is not yet a consolidated one. For some observers, this is precisely because the willingness of the ruling party to accept defeat and hand over power has not yet been tested. For scholars and analysts who view democracy primarily in electoral terms, the essence of consolidation is institutionalization of the electoral process-the acceptance of free and fair elections as the only means for obtaining or retaining power.
