Why the karen do not grow opium: competition and contardiction in the highlands of north thailand
This paper attempts to explain why the Karen do not cultivate opium poppy in a region where it offers the highest cash returns for any crop. Its initial claim is that their small nuclear family production units simply lack the manpower for the intensive cultivation which the poppy requires. But this argument raises further questions. Is the Karen social system so inflexible that its members are unable to bend the rules to suit their circumstances? Are there any aspects of their system which allows them scope to maneuver? If so, to what extent are these utilized? Or, if their system serves them so poorly, why does it persist? These questions lead to the highly controversial issues which cultural ecologists and evolutionists have debated among themselves and with their critics about the nature of human adaptiveness, the character of social and ecological systems, the rationality of social systems, and the forces which promote social change. It is obviously beyond the scope of a short paper to tackle these questions head on: nevertheless, I can illustrate how insights can be gleaned by focusing on what Vayda (1967:10) has called "the areas of disorder"?the parts of social systems which the outsider perceives to be out of tune with the socioecological structure as a whole?rather than on the parts which appear to be in harmony with one another. By so doing one might better isolate the points of stress in a system?the points at which fission of one sort or another is most likely to occur?and thus improve understanding of dynamic processes. The economic advantages of poppy cultivation are considerable, despite a widespread impression to the contrary. Opium poppy is only one of many crops grown in the large hill area extending over the provinces of northern Thailand. Rice, grown in swiddens or irrigated terraces, supports most of the inhabitants at a subsistence level. Cash crops other than opium include sesame, tea, and vegetables but are of little importance. Lowland based commercial enterprises include tin mining and forestry but are not highly developed. Those who cultivate poppy are by far the most prosperous farmers in the hills. No doubt poppy cultivators are poor by Western standards, and their returns per unit weight are miniscule compared with those who traffic in heroin on the streets of New York or Sydney, but by Thai standards they are rich.
