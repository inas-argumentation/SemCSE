Progressive muscular atrophy and other lower motor neuron syndromes of adults
Muscle Nerve 41: 161–165, 2010 Controversies about progressive muscular atrophy (PMA) start with the name. It was once called ‘‘progressive spinal muscular atrophy’’ and some authorities still use that term. Some other authorities decided that the word ‘‘spinal’’ would confuse it with the childhood and hereditary disease called ‘‘spinal muscular atrophy (SMA).’’ In contrast, PMA is a disease of adult onset and is almost always sporadic. More important controversies focus on the very concept of the condition. In theory, PMA is a disease of the lower motor neurons (LMNs) as diagnosed clinically and proven at postmortem examination. In this regard, the disease would differ from amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) (in which both upper and lower motor neurons are involved clinically and at autopsy) and also from primary lateral sclerosis (PLS), in which only upper motor neuron abnormalities are evident. It is estimated that PMA and PLS each account for about 5% of all cases of adult-onset motor neuron diseases. Those requirements for the diagnosis of PMA have been difficult to meet for several reasons: 1. The most important uncertainty arises from the evolution of symptoms and signs. Sometimes within months after symptom onset, the clinical evidence of LMN disease is complicated by the later appearance of upper motor neuron (UMN) signs. That is, the diagnosis becomes ALS. 2. Even when no upper motor neuron signs are seen in life, autopsy shows involvement of the corticospinal tract in 50% to 66% of patients with the clinical diagnosis of PMA. Again, the actual diagnosis is therefore ALS. 3. Also, in life, what may seem to be PMA introduces a differential diagnosis with other conditions (Table 1), which are discussed below (see Differential Diagnosis of PMA). 4. Among cases of purely or predominantly LMN disease, clinical manifestations include some with SOD1 mutations. These mutations are overwhelmingly associated with syndromes that compromise clinical signs of both UMN and LMN disease (meeting formal criteria for the diagnosis of ALS) but are nevertheless sometimes seen with the clinical picture of PMA. 5. SOD1 mutations are not the only cause of familial motor neuron syndromes. Dynactin mutations cause an autosomal-dominant syndrome of LMN signs accompanied by the unique manifestation of laryngeal paralysis. 6. Determining which patients with PMA actually have UMN dysfunction has therefore become the subject of a flourishing industry of brain imaging and electrodiagnosis, including diffusion tensor imaging, diffusion weighted imaging, positron emission tomography (PET) scanning, magnetic resonance spectroscopy, measurement of cortical thickness, and transcranial magnetic stimulation to measure conduction velocity in the corticospinal tracts. The profusion of these different methods suggests that none is yet sufficiently sensitive or specific to be reliably diagnostic. Clinical Manifestations of PMA. In an autopsy series of patients with motor neuron diseases, Ince et al. found 63 patients with ALS; 10 of them had PMA from symptom-onset to death. Six started with symptoms of PMA, but UMN signs appeared before death. Age at onset was similar in all 3 groups (ALS; PMA until death; and PMA converting to ALS). The male to female ratio was close to 50:50 in all three groups. The duration of symptoms, however, varied from about 16 months in PMA, to 25.7 in those converting to ALS, and 22.5 for those with ALS. de Carvalho et al. studied 17 patients with LMN signs; 10 did not develop UMN signs in the 1 year of follow-up, but the other 7 did change to ALS within 1 year. They also described patients with either rapid or slow progression.
