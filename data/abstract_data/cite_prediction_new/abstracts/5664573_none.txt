MIS: the urge to control and the control of illusions – towards a dialectic
H elga Drummond has written a thought provoking and well argued essay warning against the dangers of onesidedly using risk registers and related practices in containing management risk. In fact, her article organizes and integrates a bulk of critical commentaries published over the last 30 years about the deficiencies of the model of ‘homo economicus’ and associated models of information seeking. She argues eloquently against such views in the context of risk registers. But her argument has broader applicability as she could have as well picked up as the critical topic process management and ERP systems, financial control systems, or CRM practices. In all those practices the world outside the manager is (in theory) reduced to a disaggregate set of cause-effect relationships where the role of information system (IS) is seen either to provide information about the state of causes, state of the effects, or to offer better ways to model relationships between the causes and effects. Overall, the world of control shrinks to a closed and well defined set of mappings between causes and effects. When the information of these mappings as conveyed by the IS is true and accurate managers can effectively predict the states of the world and influence states of the world through managerial control. The value of Helga Drummond’s analysis is that contrary to widely held conjectures and assumptions of how control is exercised in the world – as reflected in the risk register model that constructs the external environment, the IS, and the manager as a complete and effective control system – the world does not in most cases work like that. The information about the causes, effects and their relationships is subject to political and social manipulation, the information and probabilities often shift like quick sand, and many times the states and their probabilities are unknowable and should be best treated as good guesses. The managers are subject to confirmation biases, escalating commitments, over-confidence and to evaluating differently downside and upside risks. Finally, putting aside the problem of disaggregation and negation of emergence, the whole model of cause-effects relationships can be invalid, poorly constructed and never validated. In fact, other plausible alternatives could do the job better. The problem here is this: these alternatives are never debated, discussed and critically evaluated as part of the control system design. She scrutinizes all these problems with substantial analysis and wit by showing that in the end the IS enters into this analysis as the complete model of the world and is thereby treated as metonymy – a part that is substituted for the whole. Hereby, the managers create the illusion of control while the IS becomes the central element in the social construction of a reified model of the control and sense of control. I agree with most of this and have often raised similar concerns of the dangers of using IS as simple means of organizational control. And, these issues are not necessarily new as they have been raised early on by Ackoff (1967), Hedberg and Jönsson (1978), and Wildavsky (1983). An interesting issue for me is: (1) why this view prevails – what is the urge to control? And: (2) what we can offer as an alternative – that is what types of models are needed to augment and complement this model of control? I will discuss both of them shortly just to set up some research directions for future.
