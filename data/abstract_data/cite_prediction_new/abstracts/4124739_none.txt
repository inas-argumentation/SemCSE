External ventricular drain-related ventriculitis prevention: standard preventive measures still work.
To the Editor: In a recent issue of Critical Care Medicine, we read with interest the article published by Chatzi et al (1) but some aspects called our attention. First, Chatzi et al (1) describe an unusually high prevalence of external ventricular drainage (EVD)related ventriculitis (28%), whereas an extensive review showed a prevalence around 10% (2). This high prevalence could be related to the applied diagnostic criteria. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention accept the presence of neurologic symptoms plus an abnormal biochemical cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis or the isolation of a microorganism in CSF as two possible ways to define EVD-related ventriculitis. However, neurologic impairment could be difficult to be detected in critically ill patients, and biochemical variables could be disturbed by recent intraventricular bleeding. More importantly, microbiological cultures by themselves are unable to discriminate between contamination, EVD-colonization, or EVD-related infections. Due to the above, some authors recommend a combination of microbiological plus clinical or biochemical variables to improve EVD-related ventriculitis diagnosis (2). Chatzi et al (1)in their study do not allow for any contamination or colonization case and all patients received systemic antibiotic treatment (and even intrathecal treatment in 76% of the cases). Furthermore, in 27% of the cases, microbiological CSF cultures were negative. Apart from questions about the clinical significance of the cases reported by the authors, the high prevalence detected may reflect the absence of a specific protocol for the control of nosocomial infections. Soberly, the authors propose different actions that are mainly focused on EVD catheter sterile handling and universal infection control policy (1). Bundles based on these well-established recommendations have previously shown clear benefits decreasing the rate of other nosocomial infections, such as catheter-related bloodstream infections (3). Nonetheless, other proposals seem to be more controversial. Reducing CSF sampling could hinder or delay EVD-related ventriculitis diagnosis causing severe clinical consequences; otherwise, a sterile EVD management should avoid any risk for bacterial inoculation during sampling. Finally, an invasive procedure as EVD routine replacement has even failed to be effective in clinical trials (4) and is not universally recommended. We found a higher frequency of biofilm formation on EVDs placed for more than 7 days, but this is a common feature in any invasive device and neither supports routine EVD exchange (5). As a matter of reaffirmation, a randomized trial with periodic and frequent CSF sampling and without routine EVD replacement showed a lower rate of EVD-related ventriculitis (3.8%) than the prevalence presented by Chatzi et al (1) during the intervention period (10.5%) (4). In conclusion, the high frequency of ventriculitis in this study (even taking into account the chosen diagnostic criteria) could hamper the extrapolation of the results to other settings. Standard interventions for EVD catheter sterile handling and for universal infection control are certainly recommended and surely useful to prevent EVD-associated ventriculitis. Routine EVD catheter replacement or CSF sampling restraint would require a positive two-step clinical trial (standard preventive intervention followed by standard interventions plus periodic EVD exchange and CSF sampling restraint) to achieve definitive recognition as a useful preventive measure for EVD-related ventriculitis. The authors have disclosed that they do not have any potential conflicts of interest.
