Comments to the paper by González-Yebra et al. (2008) “Exposure to organic solvents and cytogenetic damage in exfoliated cells of the buccal mucosa from shoe workers”
Dear Sir, I read the article by González-Yebra et al. (2008) “Exposure to organic solvents and cytogenetic damage in exfoliated cells of the buccal mucosa from shoe workers” with interest. They conWrmed the Wndings of Turkish and Brazilian investigators who reported about increased levels of micronuclei (MN) in exfoliated oral cells of subjects exposed to various chemicals used in shoe production. Unfortunately, I found some serious mistakes which will confuse the readers of International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health. First of all, the authors use for statistics medians (but not means) which are rarely used in MN assay in exfoliated cells. This circumstance makes the comparisons of the data obtained by González-Yebra et al. with the results of other studies extremely diYcult. The authors wrote: “The frequency of micronuclei found in our study (range 0–0.7 with a median of 0) is inferior to the frequency found in other studies.” (Discussion, right column, second paragraph). But in Table 3 as median is indicated 0.1 with the same range (0–0.7). So, it is not clear if median is equal to 0 or 0.1 In the same section (Discussion, but next page, right column) the authors wrote: “In agreement with other reports (Pinto et al. 2000; Martino-Roth et al. 2002; Domínguez et al. 2005). In our study, the ingestion of ethanol was not related to the MN frequency. ...Worth mentioning is that the MN frequency in the alcohol consuming workers was found to be higher when compared to all other subjects.” This is absolutely not clear from this statement if the consumption of alcohol is related to MN formation or not, because there is the contradiction between mentioned two sentences (by the way, the sentences are not grammatically correct!). Moreover, it is clearly indicated in Table 5 that MN frequencies are signiWcantly higher in exposed workers consuming alcohol compared with non-consumer workers, and, hence, “...the ingestion of ethanol is related to the MN frequency...”! Then, the authors wrote (Discussion, the same page, left column, third paragraph) that “However, the reports are controversial, some studies have found an association between age and MN frequency (Pinto et al. 2000) while some other studies concluded that age has no eVect on the MN frequency increment (Fenech and Morley 1985; Konopacka 2003; Domínguez et al. 2005; Benites et al. 2006).” Reference (Fenech and Morley 1985) is irrelevant because, Wrstly, the paper concerns MN level in lymphocytes, and, secondly, there is written the following: “At both 72 and 96 h there was a positive correlation of micronucleus expression with increasing age (P less than 0.001), with an approximately fourfold increase in MN in cultures from 80year-old donors when compared to cultures from newborn donors.” This statement, of course, is in contradiction with the statement of the authors. Serious shortcoming of the paper is the evaluation of only 1,000 exfoliated cells. To obtain reliable results in this assay it is better to evaluate at least 2,000 cells. Tolbert et al. (1991) recommended the scoring of at least 1,000 cells, with an increase to 2,000–3,000 if fewer than 5 micronucleated cells were observed after counting 1,000 cells, although it has been suggested that 10,000 cells may be needed to observe a statistically signiWcant, 50% increase, in MN frequency (Belien et al. 1995).
