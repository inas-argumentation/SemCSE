Hypertonic or isotonic saline for allergic rhinitis in children
Editor, We read with great interest the comment from Degirmencioglu et al. regarding our article on the use of hypertonic saline in the prevention of seasonal allergic rhinitis symptoms in children (1, 2). Specifically, in our study, 10 children were randomized to receive a three-times daily regimen of nasal irrigation with a 3.0% hypertonic saline whereas 10 children receiving no local therapy were used as controls (1).Degirmencioglu et al. do not share our decision to employ hypertonic solutions and hypothesize that normal saline would have been more appropriate. In particular, the authors believe that, based on current available literature on this topic, hypertonic solutions may be detrimental for patients with allergic rhinitis (3–5). We do not share this fear. Indeed, harmful effects have been univocally reported for concentrations above 5.0%, thus higher than the one used in our study (3.0%). More specifically, Degirmencioglu et al. noted that hypertonic saline has been proposed as a provocation test to diagnose allergic rhinitis. However, Krayenbuhl et al. documented that concentrations as high as 5.4%have no effect on nasal reactivity and suggest that solutions to use as provocative should be at least above this cut-off (3). Similarly, Baraniuk et al. brilliantly documented that hypertonic saline may induce sensations of pain, blockage and rhinorrhea in a dose-dependent manner. These adverse effects become evident when concentration as high as 5.4% was used (4). In our study, patientswere asked to report adverse effects including pain. None of our patients reported sensations of pain. Furthermore, adherence to our protocol was complete, thus indirectly supporting that this therapy is not associated with relevant discomfort (1). Moreover, Degirmencioglu et al. suggest that hypertonic saline decreases ciliary movement in human nasal epithelium. This effect has indeed been demonstrated in vitro for concentration of 3.0%, the one used in our study (5). Conversely, Homer et al. failed to observe this effect in vivo at this concentration. Specifically, mucociliary clearance resulted similar when using isotonic and 3.0% hypertonic solutions. A decrease of this clearance was observed with solutions of 5% tonicity (6). Finally, it has to be noted that the decision to employ hypertonic rather than isotonic solution in our trial was based on recent evidence suggesting that an increased therapeutical efficacy could be affected using nasal irrigation with hypertonic saline instead of normal saline in many sinonasal diseases (7–10). In summary, we do not believe that it would have been more appropriate to use an isotonic rather than an hypertonic solution in our study protocol. On the other hand, we are aware that our study did not demonstrate that hypertonic is more suitable than isotonic saline as the two arms of our randomized study were hypertonic douching and no local therapy. Thus, we agree with Degirmencioglu et al. who suggest that this issue should be addressed in a specific randomised clinical trial.
