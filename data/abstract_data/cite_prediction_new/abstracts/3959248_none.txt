After the confirmation of correct placement, the tube was fixed by left side. Anesthesia was maintained by spontaneous ventilation using sevoflurane 1.0 MAC and ketamine if required. The operation lasted 30 min, and blood loss was minimal. The trachea was extubated, while the baby was fully awake. There were no perioperative problems. The histological diagnosis was consistent with congenital teratoma. Cleft palate repair is planned at 1 year of age. The patient was born by normal delivery with uneventful perinatal course for the protruding mass with no functional obstruction. In a situation which is not an emergency, careful preoperative examination of the infant airway and sufficient preparations can improve anesthetic safety in this infant. It has been reported establishment of the airway achieved by fiberscope guided intubation (1) or tracheotomy (2) in the children with mass almost filled oral cavity. In our case, the mass was pedunculated and relatively mobile. As our experience, it can be pulled aside to facilitate intubation by direct laryngoscopy which had been previously used in a child with similar challenge (3). In addition, we considered direct laryngoscopy was a more skillful method for securing the pediatric airway than fiberscope. However, the alternative plans were also considered preoperatively. Prior to embarking on induction of anesthesia, an appropriate-sized facemask solved the problem of offering mask oxygen inhalation. The protruding mass would be enclosed within the facemask, which would make inhalational induction and subsequent mask ventilation possible. This is very important for prevention of ‘cannot intubatecannot ventilate’ situation. In the presence of a potentially obstructed airway, sevoflurane inhalation for induction combined with topical anesthesia of airway was widely used to provide adequate depth of anesthesia while preserving spontaneous ventilation. The mass not only affected the mid-line placement of a laryngoscope but also interfered with the line of sight of intubation. The left-sided paraglossal approach was an unconventional technique in which the blade is inserted from the left corner of the mouth. It was proposed as a viable option to improve the intubating conditions in children with orofacial cleft abnormalities (4). In this case, we preferred it to leave enough room for visualization of the glottis and for insertion of the endotracheal tube. We appreciated this approach for another reason, that was, to avoid trauma to the mass or vulnerable teeth (5) with the laryngoscope blade. Although the vocal cord was visualized through a narrowed and deviated tunnel, it did not cause much trouble to pass the endotracheal tube through the glottis. To our knowledge, ours is probably the first case intubated using the left-sided paraglossal approach for a pedunculated mass protruding from the oral cavity. Similar approach was also successful in the children who undergo division of midline or right tongue flap (6).We would like to recommend the use of left paraglossal laryngoscopy as a technique for intubation in infants with oral congenital teratoma. However, the experienced anesthetist was important to increase the efficacy of the left paraglossal approach in difficult situations.
