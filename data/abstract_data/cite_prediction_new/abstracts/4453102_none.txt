In it, he defends his gastritis theory and excludes the hydrochloric acid theory as the cause of peptic ulcer formation. He was vehemently opposed to the opinion of scientists of his time. He was especially hostile-minded against his counterpart colleague from the Department of Internal Medicine in Hamburg, who vehemently defended Schwarz’s theory of “No acid, no ulcer” (5). His second book, titled Gastric Cancer and published in 1938 (6), comprehensively details the knowledge of that time regarding gastric cancer. The book includes chapters on the symptomatology and diagnosis of early and advanced gastric cancer, signs of malignant gastric ulcer, surgery of gastric cancer, prognostic signs, survival rates after resection, and prophylaxis of gastric cancer. He concluded the chapter on prophylaxis thus: “Ulcer and gastric cancer will be developed through silent inflammation of gastric mucosa. We are unable to distinguish between gastritis causing benign ulcer and that causing gastric cancer. When we were able to prevent gastritis or treat it, we would be able to prevent ulcer and gastric cancer: Prophylaxis of gastritis means prophylaxis against ulcer and gastric cancer” (p. 262). It is remarkable that ulcer researchers before Warren and Marshall did not pay attention to the gastritis theory of peptic ulcer. In the entire 1144 pages of the 1950 Englishlanguage book, Peptic Ulcer, there is not one phrase regarding gastritis as a probable cause of this disease and no mention of Konjetzny’s German article (7). In his revolutionary letter to editor in The Lancet in 1983 (8), Barry Marshall claimed: “The pathogenesis of these bacteria remains unproven but their association with polymorphonuclear infiltration in the human antrum is highly suspicious. If these bacteria are truly associated with antral gastritis, as described by Warren, they have a part to play in other poorly understood, gastritis-associated diseases, i.e., peptic ulcer and gastric cancer.” The contents of this short communication, with the observations of the pathologist J. Robin Warren (9), correspond exactly to the conclusions of the surgeon and pathologist Georg Ernst Konjetzny. Because Konjetzny lacked a bacteriologist at his side and effective drugs, such as bismuth derivative and antibiotics; because his observations were not given credibility by his colleagues; and most probably, because of a lack of communication between Germanand English-speaking scientists during the Second World War, the discovery of Helicobacter pylori and the rescue of millions of people suffering from peptic ulcer disease was postponed.
