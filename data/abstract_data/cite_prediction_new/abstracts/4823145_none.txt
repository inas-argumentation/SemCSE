Multicystic dysplastic kidney: diagnosis and evolution
Sirs, We read with great interest the paper “Natural history of multicystic kidney conservatively managed: a prospective study” by Eli A.S. Rabelo et al. [1]. Five years ago, we performed a retrospective review of the clinical reports of 69 children with multicystic dysplastic kidneys (MCDK) assisted by the Department of Pediatric Nephrology, Hospital de Ni os Superiora Sor Mar a Ludovica (La Plata, Argentina) and Hospital Penna (Bah a Blanca, Argentina) for 18 years, and our data were similar to those reported by Rabelo et al. [1]. It was published in a local magazine in Spanish [2], and we wish to communicate it to our English-speaking colleagues. Their mean age at diagnosis was 2.46 months (r: 0–67 m), and the mean follow-up was 24.3 months (r: 3–128 m). Thirty-nine were boys. All patients had undergone renal ultrasonography (US) and voiding cystourethrography (VCU); renal scintigraphy (Tc DMSA) was performed in 62 and excretory urogram (EU) in the other 7. The diagnosis was based on characteristic imaging findings (non-communicating renal cysts without identifiable parenchyma at US, with no function on scintigraphy or EU). Ninety-five percent were detected before 12 months of age, 66.66% were detected antenatally, 28.98% during the 1st year of life and 4.34% after 12 months of age. Diagnosis was done in an antenatal US control in 66.66%, through the diagnosis of a flank mass in 11.60%, through an US finding in 11.60%, through identification during a urinary tract infection image study in 7.24%, during the evaluation of children with chronic renal failure in 1.45% and while abdominal surgery was being performed in 1.45%. In 39 (56.52%) children, MCDK affected the left kidney. Twenty-four MCDK (34.78%) had one or more contralateral associated abnormalities of the urinary tract or the kidney such as vesico-ureteral reflux (VUR) in 11 (15.94%), hydronephrosis in 5 (7.24%), ureteropelvic junction obstruction in 5 (7.24%), hydronephrosis and ureteral dilation in 2 (2.89%) and ectopic kidney in 1 (1.44%). A nephrectomy was performed in 8 (11.59%) patients, and 31 were followed with US imaging (X: 24.2 months, r: 5–84 m). US revealed no enlargement in MCDK size in any patient. In 19/31 (61.29%), there was a reduction in the size of the MCDK, in 6/31 (19.35%) renal US image was undetectable and in the other 6/31 (19.35%), there was no change at all in MCDK size. The maximal longitudinal length of the contralateral kidney was above the 50th percentile for age in the 31 cases at the end of the US control follow-up. During the follow-up only two patients developed hypertension. Nephrectomy was performed in both of them, but hypertension persists in one of them. There were no instances of malignancy or other related complications. In summary, we observed MCDK size involution or atrophy in 80.64% of our 31 patients not nephrectomized under US scan control. No complications, other than hypertension, were detected during the follow-up. We agree with Rabelo et al. [1] that “conservative management appears to be a safe option” and although “the natural history of MCDK is usually benign, patients must have long-term follow-up with US scans and blood pressure measurements” because of the malignant potential of MCDK and the low incidence of hypertension in these patients. We also concluded, based on our experience, that most of our MCDK patients were diagnosed through prenatal US; approximately 1/3 of the children had contralateral urinary tract abnormalities. The finding of only 16% of patients with VUR without urinary tract infection in most of the cases does not justify performing VCU routinely in MCDK patients.
