AGE AND ENVIRONMENT AS FACTORS IN THE NATURE AND FREQUENCY OF CARDIOVASCULAR LESIONS IN MAMMALS AND BIRDS IN THE PHILADELPHIA ZOOLOGICAL GARDEN *
The nature and frequency of cardiovascular lesions of mammals and birds in the Philadelphia Zoological Garden have undergone a t least three changes within the past 30 years. First, frequency of aortic atheromata increased from less than four to over 20 per cent between 1936 and 1955. This was associated with increased longevity which followed a change in diets during 1935.' Improved diets were also followed by apparent regression of relatively massive atheromata of brachiocephalic arteries and aortic arch of birds, especially the larger psittacines. A third change has appeared more recently. This is arteriosclerosis of the cardiac coronary arteries combined with fibrosis and infarction of the myocardium, which has accompanied efforts to assemble and maintain breeding stocks of many species of mammals and That is, an increased frequency of coronary arterial disease and sequelae have been associated with a changing social en~i ronment .~ The apparent relationship of age and environmental factors to cardiovascular lesions in this animal collection may be illustrated by a review of records for the 20-year period January 1, 1944 to January 1, 1964. This period is especially interesting because of the increasing frequency of myocardial infarction during its second decade.
