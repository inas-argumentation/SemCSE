Antidotes for a Social Malaise: Alienation, Efficacy, and Participation in Tunisia
Studies of modernization and development abound, but most of them treat modernization as a dependent variable. Whether reference is made to men or to their societies, the crucial question has been "How do we modernize?"1 The consequences of modernization, on the other hand, have received relatively little study. Most of the work that has been done in this area has focused on problems of adjusting to Western culture,2 while little attention has been paid the broader question, "What happens to individuals caught in the throes of intense social change?" The importance of this question, however, should be evident. Turnbull paints vivid and poignant pictures of individuals displaced, rendered "marginal" by modernization.3 His 1972 study of the Ik in Uganda portrays in horrifying detail a disintegrating society unable to contend with natural and imposed change.4 Less directly, the problem surfaces in studies of the political instability that has characterized many Third World countries. Political violence has been linked to social evolution producing changes in aspirations or to a gap between aspirations and the means of satisfying them.5 The pervasiveness of these problems warrants the attention of scholars.
