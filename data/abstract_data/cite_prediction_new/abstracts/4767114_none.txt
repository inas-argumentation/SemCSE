How breakthroughs in translational research have impacted treatment strategies for melanoma
Malignant melanoma is the most aggressive form of skin cancer, and although patients have an excellent prognosis if diagnosed early, the prognosis for late-stage metastatic disease is very poor due to the lack of effective treatments. Therefore, improved strategies are urgently required to recognize highrisk tumours that are likely to metastasize, and to identify more effective targeted therapies and immunotherapies to treat metastatic disease and overcome drug resistance. In an era of personalized medicine and increasing demand for specialized dermatology services, there is an ever-increasing need for screening of high-risk individuals both prior to and following the identification of a cutaneous melanoma. A recent systematic review by Watts et al. summarized clinical recommendations for the identification of high-risk clinical characteristics, as well as guidelines for follow-up after diagnosis of melanoma. High-risk characteristics such as many melanocytic naevi, dysplastic naevi, family history of malignant melanoma, large congenital naevi and Fitzpatrick type I and II skin were consistently reported across the guidelines. However, there was no consensus for how patients with increased risk of future melanoma should be screened and followed up, highlighting the variability in clinical practice across different geographical locations and health systems. Although phenotypic characteristics are a strong predictor of high-risk patients, it is well established that genetic predisposition plays a major role in melanomagenesis. An Austrian study by M€ uller et al. of 1668 patients correlated the phenotypic characteristics of high-risk individuals (childhood freckling, positive family history and blond/red hair colour) with driver mutations, such as CDKN2A, CDK4, MITF and MC1R, known to be responsible for melanoma development. The results highlighted an association between CDKN2A mutations and a high risk of developing multiple melanomas, as well as identifying the presence of deleterious mutations in the MC1R, CDK4 and MITF genes of high-risk individuals, revealing the complex interplay between genetic, phenotypic and environmental factors in melanoma development. The interplay between genetic and phenotypic characteristics of patients at high risk of developing melanoma and the importance of genetic testing was also described by Mangas et al. This study focused on a high-risk population from South Switzerland (57 patients, criteria for inclusion: two family members affected with melanoma or pancreatic cancer, or one patient with multiple cutaneous melanomas). In this study the incidence of CDKN2A mutations was as high as 10%; 7% of patients had MITF mutations, and a high number of MC1R variants were identified, highlighting the need for genetic testing in high-risk individuals. Although genetic drivers play a role in both sporadic and familial melanoma, other factors important for maintaining genomic stability are also implicated in melanoma development and progression. Telomere length, which refers to the number of nucleotide repeats located at the end of chromosomes that shorten with each cell division, has recently been associated with increased risk of malignant melanoma. However, a study by Menin et al. demonstrated that while the presence of shorter telomeres trebled the risk of sporadic melanoma, it decreased the risk of familial melanoma, further emphasizing the complex role of telomere length as a melanoma risk factor that can have opposing effects according to the presence or absence of familial predisposition. In addition to the effect of genetic predisposition, acquired genetic mutations, such as those that activate the BRAF protein kinase, also determine the risk of melanoma development and the rate of disease progression. A recent paper by Mar et al. revealed a clear association between the presence of an activating BRAF mutation and reduced survival in a retrospective cohort of 196 melanomas. However, this study also showed that tumours with a BRAF V600E mutation grew more slowly than tumours with a BRAF V600K or NRAS mutation, or those that were BRAF/NRAS wild-type, indicating that the specific mutational status of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway influences disease severity. Although the role of BRAF mutation as a driver of melanoma development and progression is well established, the role of such mutations in benign naevi and the trigger factors that allow establishment of a melanoma originating from a benign naevus are still unclear. A study by Shitara et al. demonstrated a high concordance between the mutational profiles of 61 melanomas and their adjacent benign naevi, implicating a clonal relationship between naevi and naevi-associated melanomas that indicates the melanoma was likely derived from the naevus. In addition, this study also showed that BRAF mutations occur in benign naevi, and alone are not sufficient for melanomagenesis. Interestingly, another study looking at the effect of BRAF mutation on melanoma proliferation confirmed the finding that BRAF mutation is associated with increased proliferation of tumour cells in vivo, which was further enhanced by loss of the senescence marker p16, but increased proliferation was not observed in BRAF mutant/p16 null naevi.
