Application of life table method to naturalistic designs--a comparison of efficacy of tricyclic antidepressants and benzodiazepines in ambulatory depressives.
Anyclinician who follows patients under naturalistic clinic conditions accumulates a wealth of observations about their responses to various treatments. Without random assignment to treatment or other features of the controlled experimental design, information on treatment efficacy obtained from these naturally occurring situations is limited. However, through the use of appropriate statistical techniques such as life table method, considerably more information may be extracted. Although there is no substitute for experimental control, the exploitation of naturalistic data can be of value. Information on large samples collected at a relatively low cost can be utilized, and hypotheses based on ongoing clinical practice may be generated and subsequently tested in controlled randomized trials. The life table method, also called the actuarial method, is uniquely suited to the analysis of longitudinal data and, when applied appropriately, is a sophisticated way of using naturalistically collected data to their maximal extent. 1 This article will discuss the utility of life table method for analysis of longitudinal data collected under naturalistic designs and will illustrate its usefulness in approximating quasiexperimental conditions. Its application will be illustrated in an analysis of relapse rates and of recovery rates in depressed outpatients who are receiving either tricyclic antidepressants or minor tranquilizers.
