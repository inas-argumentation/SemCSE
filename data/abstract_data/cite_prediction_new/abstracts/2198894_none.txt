In an editorial in the Journal of Medical Ethics, Raanan Gillon states that ' ... much contemporary bioethical reasoning ... argues that for any given patient there is no intrinsic difference between withholding and withdrawing life-prolonging treatment.' 1 In the same volume, however, Sulmasy and Sugarman2 argue that there may be significant ethical differences between the two. This they seek to do by the application of a version of Nozick' s Principle of Original Acquisition of Holdings, which would broadly imply that once someone has justly been allocated something, its removal is unethical even where someone else has an equivalent claim. Thus, for Sulmasy and Sugarman, withdrawing treatment in this case would be ethically distinguishable from a decision never to start it in the first place. Issues concerning resource allocation are, of course, not new, but they are of increasing importance. The clinician who makes a decision to invest in life-sustaining treatment will be acutely aware of all of the consequences of this decision, not simply those which relate to the immediate patient. For example, it might be postulated that resources, or their absence, might play a part in such decisions. This is not to say that resources will necessarily play a direct part in the decision whether or not to offer treatment, and certainly it would be difficult to reconcile the conscious use of resource based arguments with the principles of medical ethics.
