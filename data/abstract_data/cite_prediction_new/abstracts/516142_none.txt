The initial morphologic lesion in chronic hepatitis, important or unimportant?
The importance of the original histologic lesion in predicting the eventual course of chronic active hepatitis (CAH) is an often debated problem, primarily because of the lack of adequate control data. Factors felt to exert a significant effect on the course of the liver disease such as corticosteroid therapy, hepatitis B status both as a cause of progression of the disease and as a determinant of the manner in which hepatitis B-related disease responds to corticosteroid therapy, activity of the hepatitic process, exclusion of drug-related hepatotoxicity, sex, age, genetic make-up of the population, etc., are rarely stratified for in a prospective manner. As a result, retrospective analyses based on heterogeneous data provide much of the information on which we base major therapeutic decisions. This, in turn, frequently leads to the use or withholding of treatment in a biased, even if wellintentioned, manner such that determination of the natural history of a disease becomes severely limited. In this setting, the effects of treatment may become impossible to assess. In this issue of Hepatology, Cooksley and associates (1) provide evidence that CAH without bridging necrosis (i.e., piecemeal necrosis alone) is a relatively benign lesion, but the additional finding of bridging necrosis makes the lesion much more serious [CAH with bridging necrosis is often referred to as subacute hepatitis with bridging necrosis (SHB) in the literature]. Two major complications used by Cooksley et al. as well as others as markers of progression of chronic hepatitis are cirrhosis and death. Of the two, death is an easier endpoint to document. Its relevance to chronic hepatitis resides, however, in whether death is a consequence of the liver disease. Assessment of the presence of cirrhosis presents a more difficult task. Physical and biochemical findings may suggest the presence of cirrhosis, particularly when many of these are abnormal simultaneously; but even then, these abnormalities may occur in the absence of cirrhosis, or be absent in patients with documented cirrhosis (2). Reliance is placed, therefore, on examination of liver tissue. Sampling variability significantly limits the ability of the usually performed percutaneous needle biopsy to detect cirrhosis (3), apparently to such an extent that the Mayo Clinic group requires that three successive liver biopsies, performed at different times during the course of the disease, not show cirrhosis before the diagnosis is discounted (2).
