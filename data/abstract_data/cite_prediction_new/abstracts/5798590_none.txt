Cardiovascular risk factors: can long-term alcohol withdrawal benefit heavy drinkers?
In recent decades, the association between alcohol intake and cardiovascular disease has received considerable attention. Most of the prospective epidemiological studies has consistently shown that light-to-moderate alcohol consumption (6–48 g/day) is associated with a lower risk of coronary artery diseases, stroke, myocardial infarction and hypertension compared to non-drinkers [1,2]. Similarly, moderate levels of alcohol consumption have been inversely correlated with total mortality [3,4]. Conversely, there is abundant clinical, experimental and epidemiological evidence that heavy alcohol intake ( 48 g/day) causes increased mortality, and can lead to cardiovascular complications, such as dilated cardiomyopathy, haemorrhagic and ischaemic strokes, systemic hypertension, and dysrhythmias [5,6]. Other noncardiovascular adverse effects include several types of cancer (oropharynx, oesophagus, liver and breast cancer), other diseases of the respiratory and digestive tracts, addiction-related mental disorders, and accidents and injuries [7]. The combination of the beneficial and adverse effects of alcohol consumption has been consistently shown in most studies to result in Jor U-shaped curves that express the relationship between mortality or cardiovascular events in humans and the consumption of alcoholic beverages [8,9]. However, the exact shape of the all-cause mortality curve in a given region or country depends on the proportion of people consuming alcoholic beverages at different levels, especially heavy drinking, and on the prevalence of the diseases referred above.
