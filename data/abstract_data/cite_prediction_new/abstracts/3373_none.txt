Response to "Nutrition Deficiencies in Mini Gastric Bypass Patients: Where Do We Stand Today?"
We thank Dr Athanasiou et al for their interest in our article “Need for Intensive Nutrition Care After Bariatric Surgery: Is Mini Gastric Bypass at Fault?” We carefully read their pertinent comments and analysis. We found it important to answer, since some of the raised points require clarification. Finally, we do not think that our conclusion is in disagreement with the analysis of Dr Athanasiou et al. First, our university institution is a tertiary care center that has a public mission of managing bariatric surgical complications of the region. Even if it is not possible to know the exact ratio of complications due to one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), it is of note that sleeve gastrectomy represents the majority of bariatric procedures in France (60%). Gastric bypass represents only 30% of the procedures, given that Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) is still the technique of reference. What is striking and what leads us to report our experience is the severity of nutrition complications observed after OAGB as compared with other techniques. The length of the biliopancreatic limb is indeed probably the cause, since it plays an important role for weight loss. To our knowledge, it is often inaccurately reported. In our case series study, 4 of 7 OAGBs were revised in RYGB. Since then, we revised 6 other OAGBs in a total of 10 patients mainly because of reflux (n = 7) and malnutrition (n = 3). What we often found is that the biliopancreatic limb is often too short in the case of reflux whereas >250 cm in the case of malnutrition (unpublished data). We definitely agree with the role of major surgical complications as an important factor of nutrition risk, and our purpose was precisely to warn about the possible nutrition consequences. We clearly emphasized that the risk of anastomotic fistula in OAGB is biliary peritonitis, which could have contributed to the severity of nutrition complications observed. We also recently published our experience of OAGB and reported a higher rate of anastomotic ulcer. We are mindful that the bariatric surgical community now has >35 years of experience with RYGB, which is still the standard, whereas OAGB is much more recent. We have to look at long-term data, which are not so numerous in the literature and have a maximum follow-up of 7 years. Finally, we not only agree with the better weight loss results observed with OAGB but also published them. Nevertheless, the weight loss could be related to a greater fat malabsorption. We are currently performing a national multicentric randomized trial comparing 128 patients with RYGB with 128 patients with OAGB (NCT02139813). The inclusions are now completed, and the preliminary results at 1 year seem to confirm better weight loss, better resolution of comorbidities, lower dumping syndrome, but more diarrhea and steatorrhea in the OAGB group (data will be published after 2 years of follow-up). The exact mechanism of important steatorrhea in OAGB has yet to be determined. In conclusion, we agree with the easier feasibility of OAGB and its powerful effect on weight loss and resolution of comorbidities. Such effect may be associated with a greater risk of nutrition complications, requiring a close follow-up with every bariatric procedure. Given the still relevant controversy, there is a need for more scientific evidence. We are expecting that our ongoing study could help to draw more definitive conclusions.
