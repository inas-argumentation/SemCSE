Our report is unique in that both patients were treated with topical application of noncompounded, commercially available oral rapamycin solution (1 mg mL), as opposed to variations of compounded rapamycin described previously. Aside from the fact that compounding pharmacies are not always readily accessible, compounding crushed rapamycin tablets into 30 mL of a 1% ointment, as described by Haemel et al., was estimated to cost $3000 by the Barnes-Jewish Hospital pharmacy. A similar volume of the 0Æ1% oral solution is available for $330. As both of our patients showed considerable improvement, this price differential is significant. While higher concentrations of topical rapamycin may induce a more robust response, concentration and cost are proportional. The higher concentration product may not always be necessary to achieve satisfactory cosmesis. Our report is also noteworthy in that it is the first to note that topical rapamycin, of this particular formulation, did not affect one patient’s connective tissue naevus. Serum rapamycin levels were undetectable throughout treatment, perhaps mitigating concern for systemic side-effects with limited topical application of low concentrations of rapamycin. The only limitation our patients encountered in using the drug was skin irritation. Of note, the compounding process described by Kaufman McNamara et al. involved adding emollient to oral rapamycin solution, presumably enhancing tolerability. Besides decreasing the frequency of application from twice to once daily, both of our patients were advised to use emollients liberally. One patient benefited from intermittent application of 1% hydrocortisone cream when irritation was severe. While these cases provide further evidence that topical rapamycin may be a safe and effective treatment for TS-related facial angiofibromas, this evidence remains anecdotal. A randomized controlled trial (NCT01031901) is under way, more definitively to investigate topical rapamycin for this indication. In the meantime, practitioners should be aware that noncompounded, commercially available oral rapamycin solution (1 mg mL) could be a simple, safe and efficacious treatment option.
