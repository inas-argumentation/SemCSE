Follow-up of infants who had been in neonatal intensive-eareunits in Auckland and in Christchurch confirms that their contribution to the mortality rate of sudden infant death syndrome is relatively small. 14,15 Epidemiological studies have identified a number of other factors which are associated, to a greater or lesser extent, with an increased risk of sudden infant death syndrome. One of the most consistent of these has been maternal tobacco smoking. 16 New Zealand Census returns show that, in 1981, 36010 of NZ women who were aged 15 to 44 years, with live-born children, were smokers. The proportion of Maori women was 63% and for non-Maori women, 31%. Furthermore, in both racial groupings, heavy smoking was more likely among women who had children ..Chetwynd has found that 45.4% of women of less than 26 years of age in Christchurch were smokers. 17 Is the high rate of sudden infant death syndrome in New Zealand the result of a combination of known risk factors that is occurring at a greater rate than elsewhere or are there factors that are specific to New Zealand? The geographical differences which extend to Australia and the racial differences, are striking and present strong leads. Other associations of sudden infant death syndrome in New Zealand appear to conform to those of other countries with some suspected additional factors. 4 . 18 . • Their confirmation awaits the completion of a controlled study. Such associations fall into three broad categories. First, there are aspects of infant care, such as feeding, clothing, positioning and the scheduling of sleep, which are in turn influenced by a multitude of factors including race, climate, parental age, education, social class, state of mind, social support, and so on. Each of these factors may operate directly or indirectly and may act through predisposing the infant to sudden infant death syndrome during gestation or postnatally, or by acting more immediately before death. Secondly, there are infant factors, such as a possible genetic contribution, low birth weight, acute infection and other illnesses, failure to thrive, inborn errors of metabolism, and disorders of the regulation of temperature, respiration, and heart action. Thirdly, environmental factors, such as tobacco smoke and other airborne substances, and climate seem to play a part in mortality of sudden infant death syndrome.
