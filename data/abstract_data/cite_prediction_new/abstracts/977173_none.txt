The influence of the Baltic popular movements on the process of Soviet disintegration
HISTORY ABOUNDS WITH EXAMPLES of central governments affecting politics in outlying regions and of large states influencing the domestic affairs of smaller neighbours. This article investigates one of the rare instances in which one segment of a state's periphery had a decisive impact on political developments in the 'metropole' and in other parts of the periphery. In the final years of the Soviet experiment, nationalist movements in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania not only destroyed the structures of Soviet power in their own republics, but 'exported their revolutions' to other areas of the Union as well. From their inception in 1988, the Popular Fronts of Estonia and Latvia and Sajudis in Lithuania sought allies in other republics. Where these allies did not exist, the Fronts and Sajudis tried to create them through agitation. Where allies were in a weak position, the Fronts and Sajudis shored them up through practical assistance. The fruits of direct Baltic 'fraternal assistance' were reinforced by the indirect repercussions of Baltic politics elsewhere in the Union. Other movements adopted Baltic goals and methods on their own initiative. At the same time, the Baltic challenge catalysed deep divisions in Russian politics-divisions most visibly manifested in the struggle between El'tsin and Gorbachev over the fate of the Union. Both before and well into the Gorbachev era, most Sovietologists remained fixated on politics in Moscow or in Russia, while 'nationality specialists' generally investigated developments in one republic or region without reference to all-Union processes.' With the exception of two pieces written well before the August 1991 coup, the existing literature largely ignores the important role of the Baltic popular movements in the politics of Soviet disintegration.2
