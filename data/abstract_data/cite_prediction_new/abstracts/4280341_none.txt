Commentary: Do we need to understand how the brain works to teach well?
Biochemical Education, the predecessor to BAMBED, ran a set of articles [1–3] responding to the question, “How much biochemistry should a good doctor know?” That provocative question has followed me ever since in my teaching of undergraduates who aspire to become physicians, in discussions with other biochemistry teachers, and as a conversation topic with physicians. The answer from most primary care physicians is: “Not much.” Their main concern is identification. They seek evidence in the form of symptoms, signs, and tests to correctly diagnose and treat patients’ problems. Certainly a lot of basic biochemistry underlies this process, but in most situations, a sophisticated computer program could work as well, or better. Where physicians can make a difference is in how they interact with patients to understand them as individuals and empathize with the uniqueness of each person’s situation. They do this by asking sincere open-ended questions such as, “How are you?” that can prompt a variety of informative responses. Nevertheless, teachers, including myself, agonize over the superficial, confused, and incomplete understanding of biochemistry that we perceive many future physicians have when they leave our courses. Our “patients” are not responding well to our treatment. We chastise physicians for not knowing enough biochemistry to do their best job, yet by analogy, most of us are teaching without ever having formal training in education. Research on how people learn shows that pedagogies of engagement such as problem-based learning (PBL) are more effective [4, 5] than traditional lectures, yet those data seem to be ignored by a majority of science faculty [6]. What does it take to change the way we teach to improve student learning? The reason I adopted a PBL approach in my teaching [7] was affective rather than based on data. There is a strong emotional component to learning [8]. My brain just said, “This makes sense.” My rational brain still ponders the fact that we know a whole lot more about biochemistry and medicine than we know about learning and how the brain works. I wonder, if we knew more about the brain, how might it change teaching to improve learning, and would teachers embrace different methods as a result? The brain is fascinating. Single genes can alter language learning [9] or affect numeracy [10]. Strokes can excise specific functions and leave others intact. Brain competencies display a precise developmental progression [11]. One can imagine a modular brain in which a slightly altered coding or functional unit has global consequences. Our personal experiences reveal the delicate boundary between hallucinations and objective reality, the dramatic changes in mood in ourselves or others that can occur without apparent external changes, and the difficulty of changing minds based on logic and evidence. Clearly, we have a lot to learn about how the brain works to optimize learning. Several centuries ago, James Lind discovered that feeding citrus fruit to scurvy sailors cured their disease. The treatment worked. He did not need to know about ascorbic acid or hydroxylated prolines in collagen. It took generations before Lind’s discovery translated into practice [12]. I imagine that our understanding of learning is comparable with Lind’s understanding of scurvy. We have an idea of what works but have little understanding of why it works or whether there might be better approaches. It seems prudent, however, to adopt those teaching strategies that work even if we do not know why they work. These reflections emerged upon reading Awakenings [13] by Oliver Sacks, an M.D. whose knowledge of chemistry and basic science is impressive [14]. Through 20 medical cases, he describes the effect of 3,4-dihydroxyL-phenylalanine (L-DOPA) on post-encephalitic parkinsonism patients. Some of these patients had survived practically immobile and speechless for decades, yet virtually every one of them transformed into responsive ambulatory people soon after administration of 3,4-dihydroxy-L-phenylalanine. However, the changes toward normalcy were unpredictable, rarely controlled for long, and very dependent on the individual patient. That a single compound related to a neurotransmitter could have such profound effects makes one realize how important brain homeostasis is for those of us who function in various states of approximate normalcy. A slight imbalance can have profound consequences. Certainly, these issues apply to learning. Biochemically, what constitutes learning? How does it happen? Is it the same in different people? How is “thinking” different from memorization?
