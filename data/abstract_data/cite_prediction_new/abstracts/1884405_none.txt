Why aren't We More Ahead? The Risk of Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob Disease from Eating Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy-Infected Foods: Still Undetermined
Eight intense years have elapsed since the British government first officially suggested that there could be a causal link between a new variant of Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) and the Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) [1–4]. Statements from both government officials and scientists had a profound psychological impact on European populations and policies: it was widely perceived that vCJD could be caused by eating BSE-infected beef meat and other prion-contaminated foods. Was the public well informed about the possible links between vCJD and BSE-infected foods? Flahault et al. show in this issue of the European Journal of Epidemiology [5] that the answer is probably: no. Nor did we do well enough in monitoring attitudes and perceptions [5]. While many of the current ways to inform the public are often ethical, scientifically valid, and effective, messages should be more understandable, as well as more acceptable psychologically, culturally and economically. The communication flow should be bi-directional, enabling wide sectors of citizens to express their perception and to assimilate scientific messages. In short, several waves of BSE crises indicate that to develop a scientific pedagogy that is culturally sustainable remains a main challenge. But then, what is the message? What exactly do we know about the BSE/vCJD link? What are the consequences for humans of the epizootic of BSE which occurred in cows, mostly in the United Kingdom (UK) [6, 7]?
