Pathogenesis of chest pain in patients with cardiomyopathies and normal coronary arteries.
Chest pain is a frequent symptom in patients with cardiomyopathy; 40 to 70% of patients with idiopathic hypertrophic cardiomyopathy experience angina1 pain [l] and approximately 50% of patients with congestive cardiomyopathy complain of chest discomfort [2] despite the presence of normal or even dilated coronary arteries. An ECG pattern of myocardial infarction is not an uncommon finding in these patients [1,2]. Areas of interstitial fibrosis indistinguishable from those of previous myocardial infarction are frequently present in the left ventricular wall, particularly the subendocardial region and the papillary muscles, of patients with cardiomyopathy undergoing postmortem examination [3,4]. Transmural myocardial infarction in the absence of significant coronary atherosclerosis is observed in approximately 15% of patients who die from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [4]. It is generally believed that chest pain and myocardial necrosis occur in these patients as a result of an imbalance between coronary blood flow per unit mass and myocardial oxygen demand in these hearts whose muscle mass is significantly increased. Until recently, however, the pathogenesis of the chest pain and myocardial necrosis has remained unclear in these patients.
