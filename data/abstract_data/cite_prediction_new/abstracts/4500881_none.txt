Many computer programs have been produced over the years to generate contour maps. While undoubtedly a great deal of duplication has occurred, it is nevertheless true that different types of data and requirements need different solutions in order to generate satisfactory maps. A good example is the recent emergence of digital terrain modeling systems, where it IS assumed that data may be acquired from air photographs to any desired resolution by the use of automatic stereo-plotters. In marked contrast to this IS the geological situation where data are acquired at much cost from outcrops or drill holes. Here the mapping problem IS the extraction of as much infor-matron as possible from the available data, no matter how inaccurate or poorly distributed. A problem arose in the processing of oil sands drill-hole data for Syncrude Canada. Computer-drawn contour maps of sub-surface geological contacts did not bear a sufficiently close resemblance to those hand-drawn by geologists. One cause was Incorrect data handling by the mapping program where a contact was absent in a drill-hole. Another was that the con-touring system estimated and mapped the top contact of a geological unit even where that unit was absent. In addition, coding errors in the data file were not easy to observe from a computer listing alone, and, finally, hand contouring had permitted a certain amount of data smoothing where the geologist had considered it desirable. Usually in contouring irregularly distributed data of present or previous topography it is desirable to have the modeled surface pass precisely through each data point. Most techniques , being based on artificial grids generated over the map area, are unable to achieve this. While this is satisfactory for very well sampled smooth surfaces, or for very noisy data such as geochemical soil analyses, topographic data often have both high frequency features and low data point error. An alternative method-subdividing the map area into triangles with a data point at each vertex, and interpolating smoothly within and between the resulting triangles (Gold et al, 1977) constrained the surface to pass through each data point. Both of these approaches were considered somewhat extreme by themselves It was considered desirable to be able to specify Intermediate levels of smoothtng, starting with the surface modeling system based on the triangular element data structure of Gold (1977).
