Commentary: towards an effective and safe treatment of small intestine bacterial overgrowth
SIRS, Small intestine bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), a heterogeneous syndrome characterised by an increased number and/or abnormal type of bacteria (i.e. oropharyngeal or colonic type bacteria) in the small bowel, is becoming a common finding in clinical practice. Due to difficulties in its definition and detection, the true prevalence of SIBO is largely unknown and the syndrome is often misdiagnosed and generally underdiagnosed. SIBO develops when the normal homeostatic mechanisms that control enteric bacterial populations are disrupted. The two processes that most commonly predispose to bacterial overgrowth are decreased gastric acid secretion and small intestine dysmotility. Disturbances in gut immune function and anatomical abnormalities of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract also increase the likelihood of SIBO. Once present, bacterial overgrowth may induce an inflammatory response in the intestinal mucosa, further exacerbating the typical symptoms associated with SIBO. Although not universally seen, overgrowth of small bowel intestinal flora may result in microscopic mucosal inflammation. Clinical symptoms may be subtle and nonspecific, which makes diagnosis difficult without objective testing. Nevertheless, SIBO can cause severe malabsorption, serious malnutrition and deficiency syndromes. Small intestine bacterial overgrowth is a challenging and difficult-to-manage clinical condition. The primary aim of the management of SIBO should be the treatment or correction of any underlying disease or defect, whenever possible. Unfortunately, several of the clinical conditions associated with SIBO, such as post-surgical GI abnormalities or neuropathies/myopathies, are irreversible. Drug-induced SIBO could be ameliorated via the elimination of the given medication (e.g. proton pump inhibitors) or substitution with a less harmful one. Bacterial overgrowth must be eliminated and any associated nutritional deficiency duly corrected. Eradication of SIBO is based on antibiotic therapy. Its objective should not be to eradicate the entire bacterial flora, but rather to modify the intestinal microecology to get symptom relief. Antibiotic treatment remains, however, largely empiric. Effective antibiotic therapy must cover both aerobic and anaerobic enteric bacteria. Different drugs (and different dosages) with a success rate ranging from 30% to 100% have been employed. The systematic review and meta-analysis of Shah et al. represent a welcome and timely effort to provide clinicians with an evidence-based treatment of SIBO. The authors found that antibiotics are more effective than placebo (OR: 2.55; 95% CI: 1.29–5.04) in patients with symptoms attributable to SIBO. However, as evidenced by the Forest plot they presented, in their effort to perform a meta-analysis (for which at least 3 RCTs are needed), they included two studies 7 that were unable to highlight any significant effect of antibiotic therapy. The first trial was carried out in coeliac disease and had some methodological drawbacks, whereas the second one was performed in children, who might be colonised with bacteria of different antibiotic sensitivity as compared with those of adults, and whose eradication might need a proportionally higher antibiotic dose or longer treatment duration.
