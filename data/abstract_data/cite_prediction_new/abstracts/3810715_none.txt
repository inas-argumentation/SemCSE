Endovascular Grafting for Aortic Aneurysms: The Learning Curve and Long-Term Outcome
The clinical evaluation of endovascular grafting for the treatment of aortic aneurysms is progressing worldwide, with clinical investigations of many commercial products. Even in the regulated climate of the United States, several trials are under way to determine the safety and efficacy of this new technology for the treatment of aortic aneurysm. Currently, enthusiasm for minimally invasive technology is high following the spectacular successes with the Palmaz stent and laparoscopic cholecystectomy. AS with all new technology, there is a learning Curve, and, perhaps more important, the long-term outcome of the endovascular graft is unknown. New technology brings new problems and new lessons. The major bend in the learning curve so far is endoleak, a term unfamiliar in operative repair of aortic aneurysms but an important problem associated with endovascular grafting. Fortunately, there is free and rapid exchange of information among invesfkators and industry leading to improved patient care. Examples of the lessons learned in device design include the widespread convergence toward use of rigid support for bifurcated graft limbs, the use of stiff guidewires during insertion, and the reengineering of attachment systems prone to metal fatigue. It has been learned from the advice of Juan Parodi, MD, that not all patients are candidates for endovascular grafting (for example, patients who have thrombus within the anticipated attachment system deployment site). Other lessons of implantation technique include Proper positioning of the image intensifier during Proximal deployment, the use of intravascular ultrasound for measurement of aortic morphology, and the pull-down maneuver for dealing with tortuous iliac arteries [I]. In regard to follow-up, it has also been learned that exclusion of the aneurysm is essential for aneurysm sac regression to occur and that thin-cut computed tomography with delayed images and careful size surveillance are central, for failure of an aneurysm to shrink calls for a detailed search for endoleaks [2]. Catheter embolization of endoleaks is an intriguing technique and possibly an option for definitive treatment. These are only a few examples of the many contributions of investigators around the world. With careful attention to these factors, clinical results for such patients will improve. It seems that complications of atheroembolization, renal artery occlusion, iliac artery injury, limb ischemia, and even the nefarious endoleak will be overcome or reduced to such low frequency as to be considered acceptable. The long-term fate of the endovascular graft is a different matter. One area of emerging concern is dilatation of the aortic region where attachment systems are implanted.
