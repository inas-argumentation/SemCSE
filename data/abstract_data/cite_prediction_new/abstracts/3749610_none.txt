Collaborative mental health services using multiple technologies: the new way to practice and a new standard of practice?
he major results of the Myers et al. Children’s ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) Telemental T Health Treatment Study (CATTS) are reported in this issue of the Journal. This study has very significant implications for the practice of psychiatry in general, child psychiatry, rural and primary mental health service delivery, and all of us in this era of increasingly integrated care. The findings could lead to better dissemination of existing evidence-based treatments and new modalities of treatment for some psychiatric disorders delivered at a distance, even if the patient is not particularly geographically isolated. Indeed, service delivery in person and by technology to the same and/or different patient(s)—what has been called hybrid care—is an example of a new way to practice and a way that many psychiatrists and other physicians are increasingly using as multiple new technologies permeate our personal and professional lives. This hybrid approach to care is one of many variations on the theme of providing patient-centered care that is affordable, timely, and easy to access. In addition, the CATTS could lead us to change the way we conceptualize practice, and the methods used in the study could become the future standard of practice for the delivery of not only ADHD treatments but also some other treatments in the mental health arena. CATTS is the first randomized study that demonstrates the clinical effectiveness of a truly hybrid approach to mental health service provision in which different synchronous and asynchronous telehealth approaches to care were combined with traditional in-person modalities. Some previous studies have suggested that this hybrid approach holds a great deal of promise, with some investigators postulating that telepsychiatry consultations might be the new standard of practice in certain clinical situations. Several smaller examples of hybrid care have been published. A small, nonrandomized study of 118 primary care patients compared in-person psychiatric care with telepsychiatric care provided by the same psychiatrist, which resulted in hybrid care for most. Patients’ experience of the psychiatrist and satisfaction were equal for either way the care was delivered. A traditional trial of videoconferencing treatment efficacy turned into an example of the use of multiple technologies (videoconferencing, phone, and e-mail) to deliver care in what was effectively a hybrid model. Original collaborative care studies have involved significant patient education, case management, psychiatric consultation in person, patients with depression and anxiety, and co-management of medications by primary care and psychiatric physicians.
