Papua New Guinea. During 10 years practice of internal medicine at the University Teaching Hospital in Port Moresby, one of the authors has observed only one case of biopsy-proven ulcerative colitis, a few suspected cases of ankylosing spondylitis and no case of Crohn's disease. Furthermore, histopathological records in the past 15 years do not show any cases of ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease with the exception of the single aforementioned case. Although a low rate of autopsy, a high rate of ileocaecal tuberculosis, several common infective causes of bloody diarrhoea, and suboptimal laboratory facilities may mask the diagnosis of these conditions, we strongly believe that these conditions rarely occur in Papua New Guinea. It is possible that the prevalence of these conditions will increase in the future in a similar fashion to metabolic and degenerative disorders, such as diabetes and coronary heart disease, which are on the rise. In our study the patient in whom a high antistreptolysin titre was found could not be regarded as having active rheumatic fever. As stated in our paper the elevation of antistreptolysin titre is not sufficient to make the diagnosis of rheumatic fever in a patient with arthritis. No other major Jones' criteria were present. On the other hand, streptococcal infection is quite common in Papua New Guinea and the most likely explanation in this patient appeared to be a recent coincidental streptococcal infection of the skin. The findings of our study support the concept that "tropical arthritis" is a misnomer and that most cases are in fact reactive arthritis. Further investigation is needed to determine the aetiology of this condition in Papua New Guinea. Andrew Lloyd, FRACP Clinical Superintendent. The Prince ot Wales Hospital High Street. Randwick.
