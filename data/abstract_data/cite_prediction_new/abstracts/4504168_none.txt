Minimally invasive radical cystectomy for bladder cancer?
We read with interest the letter by Guru and colleagues reporting on some of the technical steps of robotic-assisted laparoscopic radical cystectomy (RALC). The standard of care for patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer is open radical cystectomy with thorough bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy and urinary diversion. Additionally, an increasing evidence base lends supports to more extensive lymphadenectomy as a way of improving both staging and patient survival, although the exact extent of the surgical procedure is still being debated. Due to the morbidity related to the standard open surgical approach, several urological surgeons are now assessing the use of laparoscopy for radical cystectomy. Furthermore, the popularity of robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery for radical prostatectomy (RALP) has led several groups to assess its use for radical cystectomy. Since 2003, several abstracts, case reports, case series (six peer-reviewed manuscripts with more than ten patients in each article), and mostly review articles, have been published, describing the technical steps of the RALC procedure, and early postoperative outcomes. What is evident from these reports is the small number of patients studied (up to 37 patients in the largest study that reported outcome), the absence of consistency in the reporting of patient characteristics, transfusion of blood products, pelvic lymphadenectomy templates, number of lymph nodes retrieved, pathological staging, surgical margins, medical and surgical complications, use of adjuvant or neoadjuvant treatments, and functional and oncological outcomes. Eff orts to decrease patient morbidity and improve surgical outcomes through technological advances are welcome and should be encouraged. However, the medical community needs to have a comprehensive and transparent look at RALC before its widespread adoption into patient care. If a prospective randomised trial comparing open and robotic-assisted laparoscopic radical cystectomy will not be done, we propose the use of a standardised reporting system, similar to those used by medical oncologists in clinical drug trials, such as the Common Toxicity Criteria (version 2.0) or the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 3.0). Items that should be collected in a prospective Institutional Review Board-approved database include: detailed preoperative data (eg, demographics, clinical stage, inclusion and exclusion criteria, medical comorbidities); intraoperative data (eg, operative time, blood loss, tumour spillage, technical complications); and postoperative data (eg, length of hospital stay, quality of life measures, medical and surgical complications, pathological staging, positive margins, adjuvant treatment, recurrence, survival). Although being debated, urologists who are experienced with open radical prostatectomy have reported a learning curve of at least 150 procedures for RALP. RALC—including pelvic lymphadenectomy and urinary diversion—is technically more demanding than RALP.Furthermore, urologists who do four or more radical cystectomies every year are considered to be in the 90th percentile of the annual volume for this surgery. For these reasons, we believe that accumulation of suffi cient numbers of patients undergoing RALC will be slower than was the experience with RALP. The number of urologists experienced in the use of RALC is still small, and the learning curve has yet to be established. Therefore, RALC should be done in centres with surgeons experienced in the treatment of patients with cancer of the prostate and bladder, including open, laparoscopic, and robotic-assisted laparoscopic approaches.
