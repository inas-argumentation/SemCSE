Downbeat nystagmus and lower motor neuron disease: 14 years follow-up
Downbeat nystagmus is the prototype of the archicerebellar syndrome resulting from a disinhibition of central anterior semicircular canal pathways [1], an imbalance of vertical smooth pursuit mechanisms [2] or a failure of the neural velocity-to-position integrator [3]. Until recently, it has never been reported in the setting of lower motor neuron (LMN) disorders. Delva and colleagues presented a series of six patients exhibiting both downbeat nystagmus and a LMN syndrome with a benign clinical course [4]. We report on a new case with electrophysiologically documented downbeat nystagmus and postural fore-aft sway with a stable clinical course over many years. In 2003, a then 26-year-old woman complained of oscillopsia and gait unsteadiness. The initial brain MRI was unremarkable and the patient was subsequently considered to have a possible sporadic cerebellar degeneration disorder. In 2007, she began to notice weakness when climbing stairs as well as when manipulating objects with her hands. In 2011, the patient came for the first time to our hospital. She had weakness in upper and lower limbs, more pronounced (MRC 3/5) in wrist and finger extensors and in hip flexors. Sensory function was preserved and tendon reflexes were absent in upper and lower limbs. She displayed a complete downbeat syndrome with oscillopsia, static and gait unsteadiness and spontaneous downbeating nystagmus, more pronounced on left and right gaze. Needle EMG revealed denervation signs in all tested proximal and peripheral limb muscles, more pronounced in wrist extensors and quadriceps and less pronounced in foot extensors. Diagnostic work up, including CSF examination, hexosaminidase-A, anti-GAD antibodies, West-Nile and HTLV serology was negative. A muscle biopsy showed chronic muscle denervation with grouped atrophy and nuclear clumps (Fig. 1, left), suggestive of SMA (spinal muscular atrophy). Molecular testing for SMA was, however, negative. A new brain MRI scan was again normal. Search for a paraneoplastic syndrome was also negative. On reexamination 2 years later her limb weakness had remained largely unchanged. She complained of disequilibrium and reported several falls in the past year. Thereupon, 4-aminopyridine (10 g bid) was tried for symptomatic treatment of downbeat syndrome and the response was monitored by means of oculography (Fig. 1, middle) and posturography (Fig. 1, right). Despite a slight reduction of nystagmus slowphase velocity on quantitative eye movement recordings, the patient denied any clinical benefit on her oscillopsia or her equilibrium, so we discontinued the drug after 4 months. At subsequent half-yearly re-examinations until the end of 2017, the patient remained largely stable with regard to her muscle weakness, maintaining the pattern of more severe pelvic and wrist/finger affection. Neck flexors and extensors as well as cephalic–bulbar muscles were preserved. Downbeat nystagmus also persisted without changes. Our case shares many core features with the series of Delva et al. [4]: co-existence of LMN disease and downbeat nystagmus, benign course over many years and sparing of bulbar and neck muscles. Muscle biopsy was reminiscent of SMA but molecular testing was negative. The phenotype of SMA, although variable [6], does not include symptoms of ocular motor impairment or nystagmus. The same holds for other more rare LMN syndromes such as the O’Sullivan-McLeod syndrome, Kennedy disease, Hirayama disease and progressive muscle atrophy [7]. Interestingly, some degree of cerebellar degeneration is increasingly recognized in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [8, 9]. Due to the slow and rather mild course of weakness in both our case and the Delva et al.
