C O M M EN TA R Y D octors and practice nurses do not have high expectations for positive results when they refer patients for dietary counselling. Thus, it is likely that most clinicians will consider their pessimism confirmed by the main conclusions of the high-quality meta-analysis by Dansinger et al. However, it is disputable whether this glass should be regarded as half empty or half full. An optimist would first recognise that active treatment and maintenance are essentially different aspects of body weight management. Active treatment (dietary counselling) compared with usual care results in a decrease of approximately 2 BMI units over 1 year, a level of weight loss that is clinically relevant. Maintenance of reduced body weight seems to be more difficult to achieve. It should be noted that these outcomes result from comparing dietary counselling with usual care. Apparently, the difference between dietary counselling and usual care diminishes over time. Does this observation imply failure of dietary counselling or success of usual care? Furthermore, weight loss is not the only goal contributing to the value of dietary counselling. Unfortunately, this meta-analysis did not provide data on other targets, such as improving the quality of dietary composition (eg, better fats), that might be more easily achievable even under usual care. Dansinger et al concluded that it is important to identify factors that result in more effective weight loss. However, it is even more important to identify factors for the maintenance of reduced weight. Floris A van de Laar, MD Radboud University Nijmegen Medical Centre Nijmegen, The Netherlands 1.
