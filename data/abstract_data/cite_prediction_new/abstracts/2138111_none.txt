Treatment of multifocal motor neuropathy with immunoglobulin: does route of administration matter?
Multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN) is an acquired, immune mediated, chronic motor neuropathy. It causes slowly progressive, asymmetric, predominantly distal, upper extremity more than lower extremity weakness. On electrodiagnostic studies conduction block is found outside of common sites of entrapment in motor but not sensory nerves or on motor but not sensory studies of mixed nerves. Some patients have high levels of circulating serum antibodies to the ganglioside GM1, although its role in the pathogenesis of the disease is unclear [1,2]. Corticosteroids and plasma exchange, commonly used in other autoimmune neuromuscular diseases, are of little value in treating MMN and may lead to clinical worsening [2–4]. Cyclophosphamide was recognized early as effective in its treatment, but the side effect profile makes it undesirable as first-line therapy [2]. Several uncontrolled studies and a number of randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in the treatment of MMN [1,3,5]. In 2006, the European Federation of Neurological Societies in conjunction with the Peripheral Nerve Society recommended IVIG as first-line therapy for MMN [5]. Although, optimal dosing regimens for IVIG in MMN have not been established, recommendations for initial dosing are for 2 g/kg over 2–5 days. In immunoglobulin responders, recommendations for maintenance therapy were 1–2 g/ kg every 2–8 weeks. Treatment is then tailored to the specific patient response [5]. Whilst effective at least in the short term, IVIG carries with it drawbacks. It is very costly, requires either inconvenient hospitalization or outpatient infusion services or even employment of home infusion professionals for administration and infusion titration, necessitates intravenous access, and is associated with infusion and access related adverse events. Subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIG) has been safely and effectively used in patients with primary immune deficiency [6], but at small doses. Rare patients with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy and MMN have also been treated with SCIG [7], but also at relatively small doses for short periods of time. In the current issue of the European Journal of Neurology Harbo et al. [8] report their findings in a randomized, single-blind cross-over trial of SCIG versus IVIG in nine patients with MMN already demonstrated to be responsive to immunoglobulin therapy with IVIG, and whom were already being treated with maintenance IVIG therapy. SCIG was self-administered two to three times per week after a brief inpatient admission for training, whilst the more customary IVIG was administered during hospital admission. The authors demonstrated that strength gained during treatment with SCIG and IVIG was equivalent (combined dynamometric strength increase of 3.6% during SCIG versus 4.3% during IVIG, P = 0.86). There were no significant differences in electrodiagnostic parameters between the two treatment groups. They concluded that this study demonstrated short-term effectiveness of SCIG in the treatment of MMN, and equivalence of effectiveness of SCIG with IVIG. Six patients reported adverse events related to SCIG infusion during the study which were limited to local injection site reactions, and none discontinued therapy as a result. However, the authors hypothesis that self-administered SCIG would lead to a better quality of life as measured by the Danish version of the SF-36 questionnaire was not supported; there were no significant differences between the two groups in either the physical or mental component summary. Despite this four patients preferred SCIG, as opposed to two who preferred IVIG; three patients voiced no preference. At the end of the study, five of the nine participating patients opted to continue SCIG therapy. This report by Harbo et al. [8] provides proof of concept that higher doses of SCIG than those given for immune deficiency and than have been given previously for immune mediated neuromuscular conditions, and for longer duration than has been reported in those conditions, is safe when self-administered. They have also shown that it is at least as effective in the relative short-term than the current first-line therapy of IVIG. Whilst this is not an entirely new weapon in the arsenal available against MMN, it is an addition that at least some patients may prefer. However, the added convenience may be less meaningful when patients can receive treatment at infusion centers, particularly during the maintenance phase when dose and treatment frequency are reduced. Comparison between SCIG and IVIG infusion in infusion centers would have been an informative one in this respect, but was not performed in this study. One common difficulty in treating patients with MMN is end of dose worsening of weakness. This was reported to be less evident during SCIG treatment, and this was cited as a reason for preference of the SC delivery route.
