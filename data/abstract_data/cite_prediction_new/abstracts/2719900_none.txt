Comment on European Federation of Neurological Societies/Peripheral Nerve Society Guideline on management of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy
Dear Editor, The guideline on ‘‘management of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP),’’ jointly issued by the European Federation of Neurological Societies (EFNS) and Peripheral Nerve Society (PNS), is an important document which is likely to influence medical practice and reimbursement policies (Joint Task Force of the EFNS and the PNS, 2005). It is a commendable effort but has several shortcomings that need to be considered before it is implemented. One glaring limitation is that it disallows making a definitive diagnosis of CIDP based on nerve biopsy or electrodiagnostic evidence of one definitive demyelinating lesion, even in patients with a compatible presentation and no other cause for neuropathy. This is likely to affect a significant number of patients (Vallat et al., 2003; Rajabally et al., 2005). The reason for overlooking the existing literature on the subject is unclear, but it may be due to methodological shortcomings. The manuscript states that ‘‘the personal databases of Task Force members were more useful’’ than the review of the literature, but no information is provided regarding the databases, the patients who were included, or the methods of data collection and analysis. Most importantly, no measures of sensitivity were provided. This stands in contrast to the detailed article on the subject by Rajabally et al. in the same issue of JPNS. The guideline is also problematic in that the diagnostic criteria appear to be designed more for research than clinical practice. At the bedside, a physician decides management based on the most likely diagnosis, given the best available evidence and other possible options. Classifying a patient as having a probable or possible disease may be useful for clinical trials but is not particularly meaningful in practice or to third-party payers. Practice guidelines need to formulate questions in a way that is meaningful for patient care, instead of just being a diluted version of those used for research (Kahn et al., 1997). Equating the efficacy and safety of IVIg and corticosteroids on the basis of a 6-week trial, for a disease that may last a lifetime, also needs to be questioned. There is a considerable literature on the ravages of long-term corticosteroid therapy, which according to one outcome study include hypertension (15%), diabetes (10%), fractures (2% per year), avascular necrosis of the hip (8%), and cataracts (22%), among others (Veenstra et al., 1999). This literature does not appear to have been considered in formulating the current guideline. Finally, the statement that ‘‘This guideline is not intended to have implications regarding reimbursement’’ requires explanation. Health care agencies and insurance companies look to professional organizations such as the EFNS and PNS for guidance. If the guideline is fair and reasonable and meant for practice rather than research, its adoption should be encouraged, and there is no reason why it should not be used to decide reimbursement. It would be irresponsible to issue guidelines that do otherwise.
