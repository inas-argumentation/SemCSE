When the number of squamous cell carcinomas expected in the absence of screening was used as the denominator in our study no significant difference in the proportion of cases accounted for by interval cancer was found between the two age groups (6 8% v 4 8%, p>0. 10). This problem would be overcome if the number of interval cancers was related to the number of negative screening tests issued for each age group. Under these circumstances if the test had equal sensitivity in all age groups the rate of diagnosis of interval cancer per 100000 negative tests should be directly proportional to the underlying risk of cervical cancer for each age group. If the risk of cervical cancer does increase with age the rate of diagnosis of interval cancer per 100 000 negative tests should be higher in older women than in younger women. Our study confirmed this.
