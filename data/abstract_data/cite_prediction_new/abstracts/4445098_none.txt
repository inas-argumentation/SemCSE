Comment on "a comparative study of alternative bone-conduction calibration methods".
Recently, Ear and Hearing published an article by Lankford and Evans’ concerning alternative methods of bone-conduction calibration. Briefly, Lankford and Evans compared three alternative methods of bone-conduction calibration (input voltage; real-ear threshold for normal-hearing listeners; real-ear threshold for sensorineural listeners) in reference to artificial mastoid calibration values. Their findings did not indicate that one of the alternate methods was significantly more accurate than either of the other two. Furthermore, they reported that each alternate method was in direct agreement ( 1 5 dB) with reference artificial mastoid values when averaged across test frequency 75 to 85% of the time, dependent on the comparison alternate method. We have two primary concerns with this study. One concern is with the reference artificial mastoid values and the other deals with the rounding-off of output and threshold levels. Perhaps, if these experimental variables had been better controlled, different results would have been found. Lankford and Evans used HAIC norms as their reference values. The HAIC norms were referenced to a Beltone 5A artificial mastoid for a Radioear B-70-A or a Sonotone B-9. However, the authors used a Beltone 5B artificial mastoid and a Radioear B-70-AA vibrator in their study. The impedance characteristics of a Beltone 5B are very different from a Beltone 5A artificial mastoid. We’ demonstrated that the output of our 5B was 6 to 7 dB less than the output of a 5A at 2000, 3000, and 4000 Hz for the same input voltage to a B-70-A vibrator. If our 5B is like the 5B used by Lankford and Evans, then this amount of error would have been introduced into their results when they converted observed voltages to equivalent decibel HL values by extrapolation of data supplied by the Radioear Corporation. This is especially true because the 5A used in our research’ was the Radioear Corporation Beltone 5A artificial mastoid. Although not specifically mentioned in our research,’ the B-70-AA is different from a B-70-A vibrator. The output of a 5B artificial mastoid is about 3 to 4 dB greater at 250 and 500 Hz and 3 to 4 dB less at 750 and lo00 Hz for a B-70-AA than for a B-70-A. These differences are not as pronounced on a more highly damped artificial mastoid system like the Beltone 5A or the revised Bruel & Kjaer 4930, but are very evident on our Beltone 5B. Using a Beltone 5B and a Radioear B-70-AA to determine reference values might be analogous to using any 6-cc coupler with any microphone and any earphone, and relating reference values to standard reference threshold pressures for a TDH-39 on a NBS 9A coupler. Lankford and Evans initially adjusted the bone-conduction output levels to be within 0.25 dB of HAIC norms; however, the output levels were then rounded to the nearest 5 dB with attenuators set to +3, -4, and -8 dB to get an experimental correction of +5, -5, and -10 dB, respectively. It would seem to us that the precision afforded by their initial adjustment would be negated by the attenuator approximations. Furthermore, subjective thresholds were also rounded to the nearest 5 dB. Thus, it appears that the possibility of error would be increased by rounding off both the output levels and the subjective threshold values. Using these rounding tolerances and correction factors required for a B-70-AA/5B system compared with a B-70-A/5A system, it would seem that 100% agreement among the alternate calibration methods to the reference artificial mastoid values could easily be achieved. Hopefully, future studies dealing with bone-conduction calibration will recognize critical variables so that the standardization of bone-conduction calibration will be advanced to the level of air-conduction calibration.
