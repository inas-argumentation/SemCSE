Models and mechanisms in very low density lipoprotein metabolism
There are at least two good reasons for studying very low density lipoprotein (VLDL) metabolism. First, it will undoubtedly help elucidate the etiology of the hyperlipoproteinaemias; and secondly, information may be generated which is relevant to an understanding of atherogenesis. However, the inherent complexities of the lipoprotein system confounds interpretation of the results. The problem is that many of the procedures which are currently employed to analyse kinetic data were originally applied to simple well characterized systems in which the tracer had the defined structure of a drug, or hormone or a purified plasma protein. Here, clearance and synthetic rates could be determined with some confidence. Lipoproteins are another matter. They comprise a variety of lipids and a group of apoproteins, each with its own peculiar metabolic properties. They exchange freely with other lipoprotein particles and are subject to enzymatic action. In such a complex system the behaviour of the tracer itself may be profoundly influenced by other components of the lipoprotein particle, making simplistic interpretation of the observed data open to error. This problem was dealt with in a 1980 editorial in which Carlson [l] sounded a note of caution with regard to the interpretation of VLDL triglyceride kinetics. His caveats apply equally well to many of today’s studies including the investigation of apolipoprotein B metabolism reported in this issue by Turner et al. [2]. Apolipoprotein B (apo B) is integral to both VLDL and low density lipoprotein (LDL). Of all lipoprotein components, it alone remains with the particle during its lifetime in the plasma. It is not apparently subject to the exchange and enzymatic phenomena mentioned above and is a useful marker of the lipoprotein particle’s metabolic fate. A variety of approaches have been used to analyse its turnover, each expressing its own view of the physiology of the lipoprotein. The simplest restricts the particle to the vascular compartment from which it is cleared by a first order process [3, 41. An extension of this model was devised to account for the bi-exponential appearance of the VLDL apo B decay curve in hypertriglyceridaemic subjects. Here, the lipoprotein is permitted to shuttle between intraand extra-vascular pools [5 ] . An alternative explanation for the bi-exponential decay phenomenon is that VLDL comprises an heterogeneous collection of particles, each with its own metabolic characteristics. The decay curve here is viewed as a composite in which catabolism is occurring simultaneously from two or more pools, each decaying at its own characteristic first order rate [4, 61. The most comprehensive and inevitably complicated approach to date attempts to present a unifying model designed to apply to both normal and hyperlipidaemic individuals in any situation [6, 71. This is conceptually pleasing but is time consuming and requires specialized computer expertise. In many instances, kinetic parameters can be derived from a decay curve using a simple model whose inherent assumptions are sufficient for the purposes of the investigation. The report of Turner el al. [2] describes the use of such a procedure. They employed the curve peeling approach of Matthews [8] to generate a weighted average value for the fractional catabolic rate of apo B from the VLDL density interval. The results they obtained showed that there was a clear difference in this parameter between normal and Type I11 hyperlipoproteinaemic subjects. However, the constraints which their model placed on the data limited their ability to speculate on the underlying mechanisms responsible for the differences. Other workers who employed more sophisticated computer based models [6 ,1 have concluded that VLDL in Type 111 hyperlipoproteinaemic subjects is composed of a mixture of compositionally normal particles which undergo lipolysis at a normal rate and ‘remnant’ lipoproteins which are cleared only slowly from the d<1.006 kg 1-’ density interval. Although such an approach is not always necessary or even desirable, it becomes mandatory when information is available on both triglyceride and apo B kinetics since the decay characteristics of these two components are so different that the process of catabolism in this instance cannot be viewed as a single event which removes particles intact from the VLDL spectrum. The situation will undoubtedly become even more complex when we include the kinetic parameters of VLDL phospholipid and cholesterol in the overall scheme. So far, our attention has been directed towards the difficulties inherent in the development of model systems appropriate to the analysis of lipoprotein kinetics. Of equal importance is the biological status or structural integrity of the tracer itself. Most studies start with the isolation and trace labelling of a fraction which is taken to be representative of the native lipoprotein population in the circulation.
