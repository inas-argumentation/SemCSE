Crooks, Commuters and Chiefs: Home and Belonging in a Border Zone in Pafuri, Gaza Province, Mozambique
This paper is concerned with the socio-economic and historical patterns of place and identity in the Pafuri triangle (or Crookes Corner) in Mozambique. Pafuri is a border zone in Gaza province, situated close to the Kruger National Park (KNP) in the Limpopo (formerly Northern) province of South Africa. Many Tsonga-speaking communities in the Limpopo province, and particularly the Maluleke, share clan linkages with kin on the Mozambique side of the border. For the past 150 years, the shared transborder identities and histories of communities such as the Maluleke have been the subject of numerous historical interpretations. These perceptions range from the opinions of various displaced communities in South Africa to those of frontiersmen, hunters, conservation officials, missionaries, Portuguese colonists and anthropologists. Most of these images are concerned with the definition and history of ethnicity, and Tsonga identity in particular. Debates concerning regional ethnicity (Harries 1987, 1989, 1994) have focused on the ‘imagined’ status of the Tsonga in South Africa and partly in Mozambique, through the processes of exclusion and classification that were so common during the years of apartheid and ‘betterment planning’. Yet little debate has sprung up which moves beyond a description of identity in South Africa alone. This paper will question the limitation of Tsonga identity to South Africa, and is devoted to comparing and understanding those qualities which are shared by many people in Mozambique as well as South Africa.
