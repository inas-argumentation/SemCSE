Acute hepatitis C virus re‐infection in a heterosexual HIV‐positive partner
To the Editor: Outbreaks of acute hepatitis C have been reported in HIV-positive homosexual men. A total of 223 HCV seroconversions were recorded in 5736 patients enrolled in EuroSIDA between 2002 and 2013 (1). Overall 63% of new HCV infections were seen in homosexual men, and the risk increased by 4% annually. The authors concluded that HCV prevention efforts should focus on this population. Herein, we report a case that highlight that HCV prevention efforts in HIV networks should be expanded to heterosexual couples. A 53-year old woman presented with liver enzyme elevations in March 2013. She had used drugs intravenously until 1986, when she was diagnosed with HIV. In 1991 she was found to be reactive for HCV antibodies. Thereafter, she tested positive for viraemia and HCV genotype 3. In 2005, she was treated for 6 months with peginterferon-ribavirin and reached sustained virological response. She had been married with an injection drug user until year 2007 when she divorced. However, they resumed their relationship for 1 month on Christmas 2012. During that period, she admitted several unprotected vaginal intercourses, but denied anal penetrations or sex during menses. Both partners were on stable antiretroviral therapy and had undetectable plasma HIV-RNA and CD4 counts >500 cells/mm. The male partner had 1 230 000 HCV-RNA IU/ml and was infected with HCV genotype 1a. In contrast, she had been negative for HCV-RNA in four tests performed during the last 7 years. The new recognition of viremia and HCV genotype 1a support the diagnosis of HCV re-infection from her sex partner in our patient. HCV transmission through heterosexual contacts among HIV-positive couples has been reported rarely. One study conducted in 234 female partners of 231 multitransfused haemophiliacs concluded that HIV was a cofactor for heterosexual HCV transmission, although HIV was much more transmissible than HCV (2). In another study of 152 women of HIVinfected males with HCV antibodies, none became infected with HCV during 3 years, despite 59% of them had vaginal or anal risk exposure because of lack of condom use and/or its slipping or breaking (3). Although HCV-RNA has been detected in the semen and this fluid can be infectious (4), HCV-RNA levels do not seem to be greater in the seminal fluid of HIV-coinfected compared with HCV-monoinfected males (5). Thus, impaired immunity in HIV-positive persons, even on antiretroviral therapy, could ultimately explain their enhanced infectivity and susceptibility to HCV infection.
