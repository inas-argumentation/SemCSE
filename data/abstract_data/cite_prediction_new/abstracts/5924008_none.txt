That was the war that was: International law, pre‐emption and the invasion of IraqWar in Iraq
Michael McGinty is Editor of RUSI World Defence Systems. He was a regular commentator on the Iraq War for the national and international media British Foreign Secretary Jack Straw noted recently that the war against Iraq was 'self-evidently justified'. Logically, Straw implied that the war was justified simply by virtue of being the war that it was. While doubtless anathema to ethicists and lawyers, Straw's remark is not entirely misplaced. State behaviour in the international system in 2003, as in the international system at any other time, is governed by a set of established norms, which in this context means the loose collection of institutions, regimes, treaties, charters, established practices, ethical frameworks, precedents and so forth, around which the expectations of actors in international society converge, not least with respect to the recourse to war, whose instrumental character can uphold the international system or whose elemental nature can threaten its destruction. Those norms can, and do, develop, incrementally and by negotiation at times but more often than not in response to events. Perhaps Jack Straw merely sought to imply that the war against Iraq, by being 'the war that was', sets a precedent for state behaviour in the post-9/11 security environment, a precedent that has altered the norms that govern international behaviour; or perhaps his remarks were merely the absurd last gasp of one who has lost all the arguments. Whichever is the case, the question remains: will the war herald a shift in international society's expectations governing the recourse to war in the post-9/11 new security environment? Or was it nothing more than an illegal act of aggression against a sovereign state? State sovereignty is a cornerstone of the contemporary international system. Iraq was a sovereign state. The extraordinary lengths to which British and American statesmen were willing to go in terms of establishing Iraqi 'guilt' to lend legitimacy to the invasion, suggest that they did indeed recognize that they were posing a challenge to contemporary established norms of state behaviour. In the light of this, it is unsurprising that an attempt was made to build an evidence-based case against Iraq, of which Colin Powell's presentation to the UN Security Council and the British Government's 'dossiers' formed part. The rationale for invading Iraq may be discerned with modest exertion in The National Security Strategy of the United States of America of September 2002, in which pre-emption forms a dominant theme. The purpose of this paper is to inquire into the attack on Iraq in the context of established norms. As this is a potentially vast field of inquiry, the following discussion is confined to the specific question of the legality of the pre-emptive use of force and with it the nature of the case against Iraq.
