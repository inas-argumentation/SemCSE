EXCITED STATE PROPERTIES OF VISUAL PIGMENTS AND BACTERIORHODOPSIN
The problem of accounting for the absorption maxima of visual pigments has been a subject of great interest for many years. As shown by Wald and coworkers,' visual pigments consist of a chromophore, 11-cis retinal [FIGURE 11, covalently bound to an apoprotein, opsin (See Reference 2 for a review of the properties of visual pigments and photoreceptor cells). The pigments have absorption maxima ranging from 430 to 580 nm, a variation that must be understood in terms of the opsin, since the chromophore is always 11-cis retinal. Retinal itself absorbs light in the ultraviolet (-380 nm), so the various opsins must be capable of both shifting their absorption to the visible and regulating absorption within the visible region of the spectrum. However, it does not explain why the absorption maxima of most rod pigments are near 500 nm or why maxima range out to 580 nm for the reddest cone pigments. An identical problem arises in trying to account for the absorption maximum of the purple membrane protein of H. halobium, which is frequently referred to as bacteriorhodopsin (for a review of this light-driven proton pump see Reference 4). It seems likely that similar mechanisms for shifting absorption maxima are operating in both systems.
