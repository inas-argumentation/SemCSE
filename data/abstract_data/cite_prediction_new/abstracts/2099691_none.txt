DISEASES OF GOATS CAUSED BY MYCOPLASMA: A REVIEW OF THE SUBJECT WITH SOME NEW FINDINGS
Contagious agalactia of goats and sheep was described 150 years ago by Mextaxa in Italy (Zavagli, 195 1 ). The disease occurs in many European countries, in North Africa and in Israel. A clinically similar condition has been observed in India (Bawa, 1944; Dhanda et al., 1959). Very early in the study of this disease, Bridre and Donatien ( 1923) cultivated the etiological agent Mycoplasma agalactiae, in a serum-enriched broth. Two years later they were able to distinguish M. ugalactiae from M . mycoides by cultural and serological means. Contagious agalactia occurs naturally among sheep and goats, and is spread by direct contact and through infected milk and exudates. The disease may be present in one of three clinical forms; mammary, ocular, or articular. In lactating animals, an acute mastitis develops; but in other animals, particularly young goats, conjunctivitis and arthritis commonly are seen. Although contagious caprine pleuropneumonia (CCPP) has been known since 1873 (Longley, 1951), the nature of the causal organism was not established until the middle of this century. There is no doubt that Kearney (1928) and Kiseleff ( 1935) cultivated an organism in serum-enriched medium, and Longley (1940) saw it in dark-ground preparations of infected pleural exudate; but it was not until the publication of the works of Shirlaw (1949), Loizelier (1950) and Longley ( 195 1 ) that the morphological resemblance between the causal organisms of bovine and caprine pleuropneumonia became generally recognized and accepted. In his 1951 paper, Longley included the results of Klieneberger’s tests made at the Lister Institute in 1941, which indicated that the organism he had named Borrelomyces peripneumoniae Capri in 1940 was not related serologically to M. mycoides or M . agalactiae. CCPP is said to occur in a number of European countries, but is probably more widespread and of greater economic consequence in Turkey, West and East Africa, India and Pakistan. The disease in the field is restricted to goats, and infection apparently occurs through the respiratory tract. CCPP is an acute disease characterized by serofibrinous pleurisy and pneumonia, which develops very rapidly to involve one or more whole lobes. In a high proportion of the natural cases, the lung lesion is unilateral. In addition to these two primary diseases of such great economic consequence over vast areas of the world, there have been described in goats a number of other conditions of more limited distribution. One mycoplasma infection in the latter group which seems to be fairly common, at least in Greece, is the so-called “Sparta disease” described by Debonera (1937). This infection is characterized by painful, subcutaneous, oedematous infiltrations affecting the head region, frequently the lower jaw, the lumbar region, the forearm, thigh and extending sometimes to the area around the teats. At necropsy, the lymphnodes draining the oedematous areas are swollen and reddened; ecchymoses are found on the epicardium with excess fluid in the pericardial sac. There is an acute nephritis.
