The Political Implications of Economic Stratification in the Black Community
HERE IS a tendency to see the American black community as something of a political monolith. Two decades after the passage of major civil rights legislation, black candidates for office are still primarily seen as "black" candidates and blacks are usually associated with issues of race and civil rights. These generalizations neglect significant class divisions within the American black community that have been detailed in numerous studies (see Dollard 1957; Frazier 1957; Gershman 1980; Marable 1980; Wilson 1980; among others). Not only is there evidence of the existence of a black middle class, but that middle class has grown substantially since the mid-1960s. Class stratification within the black community raises the potential for political divisions among blacks along the lines of class, resulting from differing life experiences. The potential consequences of class cleavage for political issues and for political leadership in the black community have not gone undiscussed in the past. It has been argued that the black middle class would orient itself toward "status" goals over "welfare" goals (Banfield and Wilson 1966: 298). It has also been argued that the black middle class would become "co-opted by the system" and "more benevolent" of the established order (Bachrach and Baratz 1970: 101-2), thereby influencing "the politics of millions of less affluent, less educated Blacks . . . against their best, longrun interests" (Marable 1978: 373; also see Stone 1978). Along these lines, some have even seen the rhetoric of racial politics as a cover for policies aimed primarily at the interests of the black middle class (Gershman 1980: 98-99), providing entrance into the system for those blacks with the backgrounds, the requisite skills or the educational credentials to take advantage of the opening of the system (Brown and Erie 1981: 319). William Julius Wilson, in his controversial book, The Declining Significance of Race, argues that class is gradually replacing race in influencing the "life chances of individual blacks" (Wilson 1980: 1). Relying on Wilson's premise that race is decreasingly significant, it might be expected that the attitudes and political behavior of middle class blacks would have significantly diverged from lower class blacks since the middle 1960s.
