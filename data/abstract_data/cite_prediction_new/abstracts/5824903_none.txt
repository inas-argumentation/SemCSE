The Indian genetic landscape and disease-related genes
A recent publication by The Indian Genome Variation Consortium (IGVC) provides the most thorough investigation to date of the genetic diversity of the Indian people (IGVC; Journal of Genetics, April 2008). The authors’ broad conclusion is the genetic diversity of India correlates mainly with ethnicity and language. The conclusion is followed by a discussion of what the fi ndings might imply for the world-wide quest to uncover links between genetics and epidemiology. Genetic diversity is an important factor for association studies that aim to discover genetic factors implicated in human health and disease. In any population being studied, if there is an underlying genetic structure that is ignored (because its existence is unknown), spurious associations can result. Therefore a clear understanding of the genetic structure of human populations is of fundamental interest to medical science. Thanks partly to the high-throughput genotyping technology that is now available, several publications have appeared dealing with this area of concern (Jakobsson et al 2008; Li et al 2008). They confi rm classical results concerning human genetic diversity: (i) There is a low level of differentiation among populations, and most of the genetic diversity is within-populations rather than between (from 85 to 95%). To put it another way, the average genetic difference between individuals from different populations only slightly exceeds the difference between unrelated individuals from a single population. (ii) Genetic diversity is highest in Africa and decreases with geographic distance from Africa. (iii) Genetic distance between populations correlates with geographic distance. Indeed we are a recent species that originated in Africa. Populations outside Africa derived from the exodus of a small number of Africans around 50000–60000 years ago. Further colonization of the planet followed from a series of ‘founder effects’: population expansions occurred via successive migrations of a small fraction of individuals from the previous location. These studies also showed that human genetic diversity clusters into fi ve population groups corresponding approximately to the fi ve continents. The fi rst clustering approach was developed by Rosenberg et al (2002) using approximately 400 DNA microsatellite sequences. These clusters arise from genuine features of the underlying pattern of human genetic variation. It is in the accumulation of small but concordant differences in allele frequency across many loci that population structure is identifi ed. In many populations, individuals predominantly cluster in only one group whereas in other populations, there are clearly multiple clusters. This mixed ancestry is diffi cult to interpret; it can arise from recent admixture among many founder populations or it can be due to shared ancestry before the divergence of sub-populations. There is a global continuity in genetic diversity that follows a geographical pattern, but some places on earth acted as barriers, limiting exchanges between populations on either side of the geographic barrier, the best-documented being the Sahara desert. Within this global framework, the report by IGVC fi lls an important gap. The global panels that have been used in Li et al (2008) and in Jakobsson et al (2008) do not include any population from India despite the fact that Indians comprise about one-sixth of the world population. Further, India was on one of the fi rst routes out of Africa (Quintana-Murci et al 1999). And more interestingly, India contains a rich diversity in terms of language, religion, and ethnicity. Several recent studies have highlighted the importance of such social factors in shaping genetic diversity (Chaix et al 2007; Oota et al 2001; Segurel et al 2008).
