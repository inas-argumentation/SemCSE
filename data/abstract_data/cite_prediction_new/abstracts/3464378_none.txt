Comments on “Corrected western Atlantic sea-level curve for the last 11,000 years based on calibrated 14C dates from Acropora palmata framework and intertidal mangrove peat” by Toscano and Macintyre [Coral Reefs (2003) 22:257–270]
It is widely accepted that the common reef-crest coral Acropora palmata (Lamarck) is one of the best candidates for reconstructing Holocene sea-level because it only develops monospecific thickets in waters less than 5 m deep (Goreau 1959; Adey and Burke 1976; Lighty et al. 1982). The first reconstruction of Holocene sea level using this coral-framework approach was made by Lighty et al. (1982) who examined cores and underwater exposures through ‘‘well-documented reef facies to insure that all samples were from an A. palmata framework and not from storm-ridge deposits’’ (Lighty et al. 1982, p. 126). They defined ‘‘well-documented reef facies’’ as those with in-place colonies of A. palmata, and identified them using the asymmetric pattern of skeletal growth bands common to this species (Lighty et al. 1982). Obviously, they recognised that the main source of error for sea-level reconstruction was the exclusion of samples from storm ridges, where large A. palmata clasts can be deposited up to 5 m above msl (e.g. Rigby and Roberts 1976; Blanchon and Perry 2004). Despite this careful work, subsequent use of A. palmata to identify sea-level history has deteriorated into an ‘indicator species’ approach where, instead of concentrating on identifying in-place framework, any core sequence containing this coral in abundance is assumed to be from a reef-crest environment developed within 5 m of sea level. This has led to the simplistic notion that age/elevation data from any A. palmata sample can be used in a ‘join-the-dot’ approach to interpret sea level through time (Fairbanks 1989; Bard et al. 1990; Rubenstone et al. 1995). The error associated with storm deposits has therefore been subsequently ignored, and the precision of these curves is therefore significantly reduced to a ± 5 m error (the +5 m error represents the habitat range of the monospecific A. palmata community, whereas the 5 m error represents the maximum elevation of storm-ridge deposits). In other words, sea-level curves drawn through undifferentiated A. palmata deposits have a maximum error of 10 m—not 5 m, as previously claimed (Fairbanks 1989; Bard et al. 1990; Blanchon and Shaw 1995; Rubenstone et al. 1995).
