Carcinoma of unknown primary origin: on the cusp of a new era.
The conduct of clinical trials in patient with carcinoma of unknown primary (CUP) is fraught with difficulty. The presentations of this entity are varied, reflecting the varied primary sources and the unusual natural history of these tumors. In part for this reason, referral patterns may not be indicative of the breadth of presentations, and selection bias in trials is a significant problem. Subsets of patients with better (or favorable) outcomes based on clinical presentation have been identified, but there is no universal agreement, and their inclusion may alter the outcome of a given trial. Patient accrual to these studies is difficult because the number of patients with true CUP seems to be decreasing as imaging and pathologic evaluation have improved, and even at large centers, the number of patients seen is modest. In that context, the study presented is quite remarkable in that it represents a randomized trial of almost 200 patients. Surely, this is the largest trial of CUP patients ever reported. The authors are to be congratulated for having successfully conducted such a trial. The authors should also be congratulated because most of our current regimens for CUP have come from their group, and in many ways, they have defined the therapies active in the treatment of CUP. In that sense, the results of this study comparing paclitaxel/carboplatinum/etoposide with gemcitabine/irinotecan are not surprising. Both regimens were equally effective, although the response rate was less than expected, and the gemcitabine/irinotecan arm was less toxic. Maintenance gefitnib seems to have had no significant effect on disease progression or overall study outcome. As treating physicians, we are left with the conclusion that many regimens have modest activity in patients with CUP, especially when those in favorable subsets have been excluded. This result, however, does not really describe the significant upheaval going on in the CUP world. In that sense, this is probably one of the last empiric trials that will be reported lumping together patients without attention to histology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and the emerging molecular studies available to assign a putative primary source in CUP patients. In a sense, the management of CUP patients has always been about identifying subsets where we understood the best treatment based on the most likely primary source. The commonly reported “favorable subsets” are all favorable because the presentation is so suggestive of a putative primary tumor with a specific treatment (eg, women with axillary adenopathy respond well to breast regimens, patients with squamous tumors in the neck respond well to the treatments used for squamous head and neck cancer). In the modern era, we have new ways of identifying subsets of patients with a “most likely” source. Thus, CUP patients whose tumors are thyroid transcription factor-1 positive form a group who should be treated as if they have lung cancer regardless of the site of presentation. Patients whose tumors have breast markers (GCFDP-15, mammaglobin, etc) should be treated as breast cancer. Patients whose tumors are estrogen receptor/progesterone receptor positive represent a small group of possible primary tumors including breast, ovarian, uterine, and eccrine tumors. Because robust IHC has become available in the community, new groups of CUP patients with predictable outcomes have emerged. As an example, the authors cite recent work, suggesting that patients with tumors who are CK7 , CK20 , and CDX2 represent a subset of patients whose tumors often respond to colon cancer regimens.1 Currently, these additional subsets are based on IHC criteria. As additional subsets are described using more sophisticated IHC or molecular profiling, fewer patients will be treated with the broad empirical regimens that have dominated the literature to date. Because active new drugs have been approved for specific tumors, the concept of empiric regimens that comprise the active agents of the most common malignancies has lost its rationale. A more reasonable strategy has emerged, which tries to assign the most likely primary source based on the clinical presentation and imaging, pathology and robust IHC, and more recently, molecular tests to assign a primary source. Recent publications have demonstrated that these tests based on different technologies (real-time polymerase chain reaction microarrays and microRNA)2,3,4 can reproducibly correctly determine the primary source in approximately 90% of cases when metastases are studied from patients with known primaries and in more than 80% of patients with CUP whose tumors can be evaluated. Overall, a putative primary designation can be assigned in 60% to 70% of cases. Although technical problems in sample collection and preparation exist, the next series of clinical studies in CUP patients will hopefully focus on subsets of patients who have been grouped using these more modern techniques. Although the value of these molecular tests in assigning therapy is as yet unproven, early analyses suggest that this information may be helpful in choosing a treatment regimen. For those of us who specialize in the treatment of CUP patients, appropriate IHC and molecular analyses have become important factors in decision making. What then should we make of this clinical trial? In fact, although the results are somewhat disappointing, the information provided is useful. As the authors point out, many regimens have been shown to have value in CUP patients. Nevertheless, paclitaxel/ carboplatinum/etoposide has remained a commonly used regimen in the community setting. As shown in this study, it does not work better and has more toxicity than other commonly used regimens. Hopefully, we will see it used less in this setting. Paclitaxel/carboplatinum alone may also be a reasonable regimen in the right setting. Finally, in their discussion, the authors allude to another change on the horizon for CUP patients. Because new targeted agents have shown benefit in patients with specific known primary tumors, these agents have potential use in CUP patients as well.
