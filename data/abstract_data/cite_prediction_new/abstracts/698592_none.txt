Ticlopidine-induced cholestatic hepatitis successfully treated with corticosteroids
TO THE EDITOR: Ticlopidine hydrochloride (Ticlid, Hoffmann-La Roche, Nutley, NJ) is an antiplatelet medication given to prevent thrombosis in patients with coronary artery or cerebrovascular disease. Even though ticlopidine has been associated with diverse adverse effects, only 1% or less of the patients treated develop cholestatic hepatitis. A 68-yr-old female was admitted to the hospital with a 2-month history of weakness, anorexia, pruritus, and abnormal liver function tests. Pertinent physical examination revealed the patient to be jaundiced. Her medical history was unremarkable, except for treatment with ticlopidine (250 mg daily) until 2 wk before hospitalization. Laboratory tests revealed an Hb of 11.8 g; total bilirubin, 16.5 mg/dl (direct, 11.1 mg/dl); ALP, 209 U/L; AST, 768 U/L; and ALT, 575 U/L. Antinuclear antibodies and hepatitis A, B, and C serologies were all negative. Ultrasound of the gallbladder and ERCP were normal. The patient declined a liver biopsy. Because of the temporal relationship between drug administration and cholestasis, a diagnosis of ticlopidine-induced hepatitis was considered. Four days after admission, treatment with methylprednisolone (40 mg i.v. every 8 h) was started, and 48 h later she was discharged receiving prednisone (40 mg p.o. daily), which was slowly tapered and, 4 wk later, discontinued. As an outpatient, she clinically improved to the point where she became asymptomatic and her liver function tests were almost normal, revealing an ALP of 117 U/L; AST, 33 U/L; ALT, 56 U/L; and total bilirubin, 1.9 mg/dl. Why ticlopidine produces hepatic damage is not known; however, it is possible that one of the potential mechanisms of liver injury is due to a hypersensitivity or “allergic type” of reaction. A review of the literature (1–5) failed to find a case of ticlopidine-induced cholestatic hepatitis that has been successfully treated with corticosteroids. The approach to this problem is usually observation, and unfortunately, even though the mortality rate is low, a great majority of cases described had prolonged periods of remission (3). The case herein described demonstrates that corticosteroid therapy may prove useful in treating patients suffering from ticlopidine-induced cholestatic hepatitis because it may accelerate the recovery period, therefore improving symptoms and normalizing liver function tests faster.
