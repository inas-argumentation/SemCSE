Risk stratification for congenital diaphragmatic hernia—Is it all oxygenation but not ventilation?
Dear Editor, Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH) remains one of the most challenging conditions to manage in the field of neonatology. A risk stratification strategy proposed by Terui et al.[1] in a recent issue of the Journal with oxygenation index (OI) and Apgar score is a simple and efficient way to assess prognosis in infants with isolated CDH. The authors classified patients into risk categories that provided a reasonably accurate prediction of survival at 90 days. However, we are concerned that their model did not include PaCO2 to assess ventilation. The two primary determinants of outcome in CDH are lung hypoplasia and persistent pulmonary hypertension (PPHN) [2, 3]. Arterial CO2 (PaCO2) is a significant predictor of outcome in infants with pulmonary hypoplasia due to CDH [4–6]. We propose, an index that includes both oxygenation and ventilation parameters, such as ((PaO2/ FiO2)−PaCO2) also known as oxygenation ventilation index (OV) and may be a better indicator of the severity of PPHN and lung hypoplasia. When infants with CDH are delivered in centers without a level IV NICU, a decision to transfer a neonate with CDH to a center with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) facility needs to be made in the first few hours after birth. In which case, a simple but reliable risk stratification strategy that includes both oxygenation and ventilation may be optimal compared to oxygenation alone. We evaluated OV index on an arterial blood gas in the first 6 h of admission in 216 infants with CDH from 3 centers (University at Buffalo, Medical College of Wisconsin, and University of Iowa). A cutoff value of zero for the OV index distributes patients into the low and high risk of ECMO/death (AUC-0.714 (0.639–0.789, p< 0.0001). Furthermore, the OV index of ≤0 was associated with higher mortality (OR–6.2 (95% CI: 3.4–11.7)). The OV index of zero was used to stratify CDH infants (Table 1). ECMO rate was higher with high risk (42 vs. 10%, p< 0.001). Mortality rate was also higher with high risk (58% vs. 13%, p< 0.001).
