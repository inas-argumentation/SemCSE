Confronting Discrimination and the Integrative Curriculum
The passage of the Americans With Disabilities Act of 1990 will change the way educators create mass communication learning environments. Prior research provides evidence that educators and professionals have been notably silent in weaving ableism‘ issues into the fabric of multicultural curricula (Edwards, 1992; Cohen, Lombard & Pierson, 1992). Two Journalism Educator articles attempted to explain this silence. Edwards (1992) notes that both journalism educators and practicing professionals stood at the cutting-edge of integration in the racial civil rights movement. Both groups actively and publicly participate in the battle over gender and equity. Both, however, seem reluctant and silent concerning the disability movement. Cohen, Lombard & Pierson (1992) developed a model which formally modifies the mass communication and society course curriculum to reflect scholarly approaches to multicultural issues. Ableism falls within the realm of this approach since the authors defined the term multicultural to “include people of color, minorities, gender distinction, religious beliefs, and other attributes that distinguish one identifiable set of people from another” (Cohen et al. 1992, p. 7). But this emerging multicultural approach to curriculum development typically continues to narrow the focus to issues of gender, race and ethnicity in mass communication and journalism (Cohen et al., 1992; Glasser, 1992). Many of the culturally-bound precepts related to ableism do not receive the attention they merit. Cohen et al. (1992) suggest that the hesitancies to add culturally-diverse perspectives to the core courses in mass communication may be founded in ideology. Critics blame champions of minority interests for what they call “overtly ideological scholarship” (D’Souza, 1991, p. 52). As related to ableism, this contention is not necessarily the case.
