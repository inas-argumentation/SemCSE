Limitations of the SEER database for demonstrating causal relationships between treatments and outcomes in pediatric intestinal tumors.
Thirty-six years ago, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) created the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program. The goal of SEER is to capture the nation’s cancer incidence and outcomes by aggregating data from multiple cancer-based population registries. At conception, SEER accumulated data from only a handful of regions in the United States and was criticized for not accurately representing minorities (African Americans, Hispanics, and Native Americans). Today, SEER has grown to encompass 17 geographic areas within the United States and accurately reflects the US population with relation to race, ethnic, socioeconomic, and geographical distribution [1]. The SEER database has many advantages for population-based clinical research. SEER contains data on patient demographics (ethnicity, race, age, etc.), tumor characteristics (location, histology, grade, stage), use of surgery or radiation therapy, and patient survival [2]. The vast numbers of cancers contained within SEER combined with long-term survival data make it an ideal tool for studying rare childhood cancers. Not only is SEER representative of the US population, it also has high data quality and is frequently audited for accuracy. Over the past 20 years, there has been an exponential increase in the number of publications generated by analyzing the SEER database [3]. However, the database is limited by exclusion of several clinically relevant data elements. Most notably, it does not include pathologic resection margins, surgical margins, details of surgical technique, significant patient comorbidities, or dosage and duration of chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Thus, the SEER database is a valuable tool to identify demographic, epidemiologic, and survival information for rare malignancies, but analysis of the data to draw conclusions about adjuvant therapy must be viewed with caution. In an article recently published in Journal of Surgical Research, Zhuge et al. analyze data from the SEER database to report on the demographic, epidemiologic, and survival outcomes of pediatric intestinal foregut and small bowel solid tumors [4]. In aggregate, pediatric forgut and small bowel malignancies are exceedingly rare as evidenced by the 105 patients identified in the SEER database spanning 32 years. Most tumors in this study were poorly differentiated and presented with advanced disease. Sarcomas and carcinomas were the most common tumors, with similar incidence.
