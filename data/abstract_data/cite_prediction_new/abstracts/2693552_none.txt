5.1 Biology: Elementary Education: Assessing Hypothesis Testing Skills
Secondary Education Pre-service elementary teachers interested in learning and teaching biology generally take the introductory course BIO 100: The Living World. ACEPT has reformed the course to now use inquiry and hands-on learning to teach biological concepts and develop students' reasoning skills. Improving students' reasoning skills better prepares Elementary Education students to teach science. In BIO 100, ACEPT investigators regularly pursue research questions that contribute to our understanding of how students learn. The study reported here is an example of this ongoing inquiry. Introduction Research has indicated that reasoning patterns develop across adolescence, at least in some students, and play an important role in the ability to do science and construct scientific concepts. Research has also documented that improvements in reasoning as a consequence of instruction are possible and of general use. Consequently, one goal of BIO 100, a course often taken by elementary pre-service teachers, is to encourage students to develop generalizable hypothesis-testing skills during a series of lab and field activities. The course promotes the development of these skills by encouraging "deliberate and mindful abstraction" of question-asking and question-answering skills in a variety of domain-specific contexts. In BIO 100, variation in students' performance on test items involving unobservable theoretical entities led researchers to investigate its cause. It was hypothesized that two general developmentally based levels of hypothesis-testing skills exist. The first hypothesized level presumably involves skills associated with testing hypotheses about observable causal agents; the second presumably involves skills associated with testing hypotheses involving unobservable entities. Method To test the hypothesis, a test was administered at the beginning of the semester to 667 students to assess their ability to test hypotheses involving both observable and non-observable entities. Based on their responses, students were classified in one of four categories: Level 0 = students not able to test hypotheses involving observable causal agents; Low Level 1 = students inconsistently able to test hypotheses involving observable causal agents; High Level 1 = students consistently able to test hypotheses involving observable causal agents; and Level 2 = students able to test hypotheses involving ! 125 Chapter 5 Reformed Courses ! ! ! ! unobservable causal agents.
