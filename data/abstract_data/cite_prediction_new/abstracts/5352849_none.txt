Differences in genetic composition between populations experiencing selective migration
Genetic differences within a system of partially isolated populations may be due to many causes, but the two factors generally suggested to explain major differences are variation in the selective pressures acting within the system and, if the populations are small, random drift. The effect of both these factors have been considered in some detail (see for example, Bodmer & Cavalli-Sforza, 1968; Moran, 1959). Further, it has been shown (Hiorns et at. 1969; Harrison, Hiorns & Kiichemann, 1970) that in the absence of these diversifying factors the unifying effect of migration will rapidly remove any genetic differences which may exist between the populations, even when the migration rates are small. Here it was assumed, in common with most other migration models, that the migrating individuals were random samples of the populations from which they came. Burt (1961) and Cliquet (1968) have, however, shown that a number of characteristics must influence social mobility and mate selection in man, since the more socially mobile individuals within a population are found to be anthropologically different from the less socially mobile, and it is suggested that this selective migration could exaggerate the genetic differences between populations rather than diminish them. The way in which an individual’s mobility is influenced by any character will of course be complex, but as a first step in understanding the effects of selective migration a very simple model is suggested here. Suppose the criterion for migration is based on a single quantitative character. For each class there will exist a group of individuals whose range of phenotypic values for this character are best adapted to their class environment and who will remain resident in their class, whereas individuals with more extreme phenotypic values will migrate. An example of this movement can be considered in the effect of intelligence on migration between the occupational classes. As Burt (1959) states, there has for many generations been a discernible tendency for individuals endowed with high intelligence to rise to a higher class and for those of low intelligence to drop to a lower class, and he concludes that there must now be appreciable differences in the genetic composition of different social classes and that these differences will show themselves most plainly in differences between the mean level of intelligence for each class. In this paper a mathematical analysis is made of the divergence of the gene frequencies between the populations from an initial state of homogeneity until the system reaches the equilibrium state, under the proposed model of selective migration. The approximate analytical results are confirmed by computer simulation.
