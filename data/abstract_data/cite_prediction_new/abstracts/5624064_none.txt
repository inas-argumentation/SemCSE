Treatment opportunities in a maximum security psychiatric hospital: a problem survey.
Staff in maximum security psychiatric hospitals have the unenviable task of providing treatment services for a heterogeneous collection of very difficult patients. Because of legal constraints and the fact that they are maximum security, these institutions seldom have control over their admissions and often have little control over their discharges. When combined with difficulties in evaluating the efficacy of treatment programs, these difficulties become stifling and almost insuperable. The problems of staff recruitment, staff training, and bureaucratic disorganization which hinder the development of treatment programs in such settings are well known (e.g. Quinsey, 1981). Part of the cause as well as the effect of these difficulties is a lack of a coherent and cogent rationale for the development of particular kinds of programs in maximum security settings (Quinsey, 1977), as well as poor decisionmaking strategies (Quinsey & Ambtman, 1978, 1979). The lack of a clear rationale for treatment programming leads directly to serious disagreements among clinical staff as to the efficacy of different treatment strategies, profound pessimism about treatment efficacy in general, and a lack of emphasis in patient assessment on variables that may be related to patient treatability and to the selection of particular treatment programs for particular patients (Quinsey & Maguire, 1984). Last, but not least, the lack of explicit rationales for specific program elements leads to an underutilization of already existing scientific knowledge (Glaser, 1973; Rice & Quinsey, 1986). In the absence of a comprehensive and empirically validated approach to these problems, some rational and incremental strategy of program development is required. Fortunately, there is an approach to rational program development which does not depend upon having the correct answer in advance.
