Therapeutic drug monitoring of mycophenolates: why make simple things complicated?
The debate whether concentration-controlled dosing of Mycophenolates (MPAs) (Cellcept [Mycophenolate Mofetil: MMF] and Myfortic [Enteric-Coated Mycophenolate sodium: EC-MPS]) will improve outcome and/or reduce drugrelated adverse effects in solid organ transplantation (SOT) remains unsolved. Although the convenient clinical use of fixed doses of MMF or EC-MPS has been advocated since their introduction in SOT, pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that measured MPA exposure is widely variable when used in this fashion. Moreover, many studies, especially in renal transplantation, have now shown that MPA exposure early after grafting is related to efficacy, as determined by the incidence of biopsy-proven acute allograft rejection. Particularly, in organ recipients cotreated with cyclosporine A, the use of a fixed dose of MMF or EC-MPS results in early underexposure in more than 50% of patients, at least when target MPA area under the concentration time curve ranges between 30 and 60 mg⁎h/L are adhered to. Although in different types of SOT, this lower MPA area under the concentration time curve threshold of 30 mg⁎h/L seems to apply; the clinical significance of the upper range threshold seems less robust. Few studies have demonstrated a clear increase in specific drug-related adverse events (eg, gastrointestinal disturbances, bone marrow toxicity) with increased MPA exposure. In fact, the latter finding questions the very essence of MPAs as compounds suitable for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). In an attempt to consolidate MPAs as drugs benefiting from TDM, 3 recent randomized clinical studies have been accomplished, albeit without providing a clear cut answer to the question whether TDM of MPAs improves clinical outcome. Although one study showed a clear benefit of TDM, the other 2 studies did not, or only in specific risk groups of patients in post hoc analysis of results. This, together with the lack of sufficient validated data on the relationship between MPA exposure and clinical outcome parameters in nonrenal SOT, has put the transplant clinician involved in daily patient care in a difficult position. Is the extra time, resources, and financial costs of TDM of MPA a worthwhile investment for the transplant patient and the community?
