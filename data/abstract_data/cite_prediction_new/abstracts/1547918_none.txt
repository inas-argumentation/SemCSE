(Mis)trusting authorities: a social theory of currency crises
The scholarly debate over the causes of currency crises has reached an impasse with policy failure and market power approaches engaged in a deadlocked “tug-of-war.” In an effort to break this deadlock, this article builds and applies a social theory of currency crises drawing on work in political theory and the sociology of embedded business networks. It argues that changes in trust relations between state and market actors influence exchange relations by mediating how these actors assess risks. This approach differs from existing accounts in two fundamental ways. First, it takes an international political economy view to what has traditionally been seen as a domestic political economy issue. Second, it argues that currency relations are mediated by socially-constituted relations of trust, relations generally deemed epiphenomenal in mainstream theory. This “authority network” approach is then applied to the interwar gold standard crisis. The case study illustrates how variations in trust relations among networked actors produce distinct logics of economic exchange, influence state and market actor behavior and thereby produce episodes of currency stability, volatility and crisis that rationalist approaches struggle to explain.
