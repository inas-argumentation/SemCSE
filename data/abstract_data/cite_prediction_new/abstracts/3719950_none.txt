The role of the nurse in the management of deliberate self-poisoning in the general hospital.
In recent years there has been a marked increase in the number of cases of deliberate self-poisoning referred to the health services (Bancroft, Skrimshire, Reynolds, Simkin and Smith, 1975; Holding, Buglass, Duffy and Kreitman, 1977; Wexler, Weissman and Kasl, 1978). In the United Kingdom, the official policy in the last decade has been to ensure that these patients are seen by a psychiatrist before they leave hospital (Hill Report, 1968), but the magnitude of the problem and the practical difficulties involved in implementing such a policy have led to the exploration of alternative approaches which have included the use of non-medically qualified professionals such as social workers (Newson-Smith and Hirsch, 1979) and nurses (Catalan, 1979; Catalan, Marsack, Hawton, Whitwell, Fagg and Bancroft, 1980) and also general medical teams (Gardner, Hanka, O’Brien, Page and Rees, 1977) in the assessment of these patients. The involvement of the nurse in the assessment of self-poisoning patients represents a departure from more traditional nursing duties and, therefore, requires close evaluation. In this paper, a clinical service where nurses carry out most of the assessments and the subsequent management of deliberate self-poisoning patients is described, and details are given of the nurses’ work and training. Reference will be made to the results of studies that have evaluated the work of the nurse in the management of self-poisoning patients.
