Letter to the editor: Progressive neurology in a young woman with a known Currarino’s triad
Through this letter we wish to inform your readers on the well-being of our patient we presented in this journal in 2012 [1]. As had been reported, she was admitted to our clinic at age 22 because of an incidentally found anterior presacral mass of unknown origin, suggestive of a meningocele. Together with her medical antecedents this prompted us to diagnose her with Currarino’s triad. Indeed, genetic counselling, not yet available at the time of publication, demonstrated a mutation in the MNX1-gene, also known as HLXB9, in the patient and her father, which confirmed Currarino’s syndrome. At the time, there were no neurological symptoms because of which we pursued a conservative approach, without surgical intervention. Ever since, she had been followed on an outpatient basis. She presented to the neurologist in October 2015, 25-years of age, with progressive sensory complaints. These comprised of progressive hypesthesia of the left arm, primarily confined to the C8-dermatome, but later also involving C5-, C6-and C7dermatomes. There were no other neurological deficits. Magnetic resonance imaging showed an increased diameter of the anterior meningocele and a significant syringomyelia from C1 up to Th7. The medullary conus remained at the L3–4 level and the small syrinx at this level remained unchanged. Because of these findings and the progressive neurology, she was operated upon via a posterior approach. The myelum was untethered and the anterior dura at the level of the meningocele was ligated. Fortuitously, an epidermoid cyst was found and resected. Postoperatively, she initially complained of perineal and lower extremity dysesthesia as well as urinary retention. In the course of 4 months these complaints resolved completely. An MRI at 3 months after surgery showed a significant decrease in the anterior meningocele and the syringomyelia. This follow-up on an earlier case of incidentally recognised Currarino’s syndrome serves to show that progressive neurological symptoms may even appear after adolescence. Therefore, careful follow-up is mandatory. Up to date, there remains uncertainty regarding the neurosurgical treatment in the absence of neurological symptoms [2, 3]. Kole et al. reviewed 193 unique cases in literature of which none included criteria for surgical untethering [3]. Interestingly, it has been suggested that the risk of tethered cord syndrome may be higher amongst patients with a lipoma and lower amongst those with a teratoma or anterior meningocele. We cordially remain with our conclusion that early diagnosis, preferentially at birth or prenatally, is of great importance to be able to monitor these patients closely and prevent them from developing neurological deficits or worsening conditions. Decisions on treatment depend on the clinical presentation of (neurological) symptoms and radiological findings. This is true for all open and occult dysraphisms [4].
