Regulation of B-Z conformational transition and complex formation with a Z-form binding protein by introduction of constraint to double-stranded DNA by using a DNA nanoscaffold.
A known structural variation of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), Z-form DNA, has a stable left-handed helix that is induced from a B-form CG repeat sequence by high concentration of metal cation. The biological role of the lefthanded DNA structure that includes the regulation of gene expression is being gradually revealed. The DNA-binding domain of dsRNA adenosine deaminase (ADAR1), called Za domain, specifically binds to the Z-form of CG repeatcontaining dsDNA in the equilibrium state of the B–Z transition under physiological conditions. Crystal-structure studies revealed that two Za domains form a stable complex with a Z-forming CG repeat sequence. Conversely, artificially constrained dsDNA structures, such as bent, overwound, or constrained structures, are used to control the binding of proteins and subsequent reactions. We developed artificially constrained dsDNAs by placing them in a DNA nanostructure and found that the tension of double helices affected enzymatic reactions, such as DNA methylation and base-excision repair. This method can also be used to visualize the dynamic movement of the DNA strands and conformational changes. Therefore, the constrained dsDNA constructed in the DNA nanostructure can be a useful tool for investigation of the DNA–protein interactions and reactions. We recently visualized the B–Z transition in the equilibrium condition by high-speed atomic force microscopy (AFM) by using a 5-methyl-CG repeat-containing dsDNA placed in the DNA frame structure. This suggests that the rotation of double helices can be regulated and manipulated in the designed DNA nanoscaffold. Herein, we designed structurally constrained dsDNAs, in which the rotation was suppressed by their introduction into a DNA nanostructure. The Za protein should bind preferentially to the rotatable double helix compared with the constrained dsDNA.
