Learning from the experts: drought resistance in desert plants.
The changing climate is expected to bring about higher temperatures and severe drought stresses inmanyplaces across Earth (IPCC, 2014). This prospect has inspired research aimed at developing crop plants with better drought tolerance, in order to maintain yields under challenging environmental conditions. Solutions have been sought via studies of drought-induced gene expression, root architecture, the protective cuticle on plant surfaces and the regulation of stomata, adjustable pores formed by pairs of specialised guard cells on plant leaves that control gas exchange with the environment (Hu&Xiong, 2014). Thus far, most studies have been conducted using model plants, and relatively little attention has been focused on plants which are already adapted for growth in conditions of severe heat and drought, due largely to limited availability of genome sequence data and the difficulties ofworkingwith these slow-growing species. In this issue of New Phytologist, M€ uller et al. (pp. 150–162) present an exciting study of stomatal morphology and regulation in a desertadapted plant, the date palm (Phoenix dactylifera). They describe the specific architecture of the stomata and cuticle and analyse guard cell ion-channel regulation in this droughtand heat-tolerant species. Characterization of the control of water loss by the cuticle and stomata that takes place in desert plants could provide valuable information and tools for improving crop survival and water-use efficiency (WUE) in drought conditions. The cuticle, a layer of cuticular waxes on aboveground plant surfaces, is considered to be themost important protective structure for land plant survival in dry conditions (Xue et al., 2017). The varying composition and architecture of the cuticle in different parts of the plant may convey specific functions. For example, M€ uller et al. show that in the date palm, chimneys formed of cuticular wax surround the stomatal pores, similar to some other monocotyledons (Barthlott et al., 1998), which presumably protects the plant from excessive water loss via increased boundarylayer resistance to stomatal transpiration. Further characterization of the development and synthesis of these structures in desert plants could give valuable insights into the control of plant water loss. Several drought-sensitive mutants with defective cuticle structures have previously been isolated, and a modification of the expression of components involved in the biosynthesis of the cuticle has been successfully applied to increase plant drought tolerance (Xue et al., 2017). Recently, a protein required for the formation of the cuticular ledge was identified in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana (Hunt et al., 2017), whereby the lack of this protein was associated with higher drought tolerance, but it also impaired stomatal function due to coverage of stomata by the cuticular layer. These results suggest that any modification to the biosynthesis of the cuticle with the aim of increasing drought tolerance should also assess the concurrent effects on the stomata. The plant hormone abscisic acid (ABA) regulateswater loss both via the stomata (Cutler et al., 2010) and the cuticle (Cui et al., 2016) and the genes controlling these pathways are highly conserved (Chater et al., 2013; Cui et al., 2016), further highlighting the importance of the synergy of the cuticle and stomata in the control of plant transpiration. In this respect, recent studies which address the structure of the cuticle in a drought-adapted plant species, as done in M€uller et al., and the mechanisms of stomatal outer cuticular ledge formation, as in Hunt et al. (2017), are very timely. Further research in this direction will undoubtedly provide valuable insight into the cooperation of the cuticle and stomata in protecting the plant from excess water loss while maintaining efficient photosynthesis.
