Cautions as Regulators Move to End Exclusive Reliance on Intention to Treat
Intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses of randomized trials, which analyze participants in their assigned groups regardless of actual receipt of the intervention, have been favored because they alleviate concerns about baseline confounding. However, this benefit comes at a price: ITT analyses estimate the effect of being assigned to an intervention rather than that of actually receiving it. The magnitude of the ITT effectin statistical parlance, the causal estimanddepends on the degree to which participants receive assigned interventions, and ITT analyses can miss harms and result in erroneous claims of noninferiority (1, 2). Given these shortcomings, some regulators, pharmaceutical companies, and academics advocate complementing the ITT effect with more clinically relevant estimands. One example is the per protocol effect, the effect under full adherence to the treatment strategies defined in the protocol (3). The validity of non-ITT effect estimates, however, requires strong assumptions (4). For example, in the presence of nonadherence, unbiased estimates of per protocol effect generally require adjustment for determinants of adherence after randomization (5). Recently, the International Council for Harmonisation has been questioning whether the ITT effect should be the only estimand for regulatory decision making and has proposed alternatives in a draft addendum to its guideline on statistical principles for clinical trials (6). Open for comment until 30 April 2018, this E9 Addendum has been accepted by the European Medicines Agency and endorsed by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (7). Although the move away from ITT-only approaches is an advance, the validity and proper interpretation of any non-ITT causal estimand depends on its specifications. Here we offer our view of what these specifications should be when trials are designed, conducted, analyzed, and interpreted. Make the Treatment Strategies Explicit The E9 Addendum defines 4 attributes of a causal estimand: population (for example, diabetic patients aged 50 to 75 years), end point (for example, all-cause mortality), effect measure (for example, risk ratio), and approaches to account for nonadherence and competing risks (5 such approaches are described). This last attribute conflates the scientific question with the methods used to answer it and distracts from what we believe should be the fourth attribute: the treatment strategiesor policies or plansto be compared (for example, do not treat vs. treat until contraindications arise). Fundamentally, most causal estimands cannot be meaningfully defined as contrasts of point-in-time treatments but rather as contrasts of sustained treatment strategies that are implemented over follow-up. The E9 Addendum refers to the ITT effect as the treatment policy effect, which is unfortunate because although the ITT effect is indeed a contrast of treatment policies or strategies (including assign to treatment, then do whatever you want), so are all other estimands. Focus on Clinically Relevant Treatment Strategies in Identifiable Groups of Patients The E9 Addendum should emphasize the importance of basing regulatory decisions on the comparison of treatment strategies that may be realistically implemented in well-defined groups of patients. For example, an estimand that implicitly compares the strategies do not treat versus treat even if contraindications arise should be irrelevant to both regulators and clinicians because the latter treatment strategy is never used in practice. Similarly, an estimand that compares strategies in a subgroup that cannot be clinically identified (for example, the principal stratum of persons who would have adhered regardless of treatment assignment) provides a questionable basis for regulatory or clinical decision making. Demand a Precise Definition of Adherence for All Estimands Not adhering to a treatment plan is different from stopping treatment if stopping treatment is planned. For example, under the strategy receive treatment until contraindications arise, patients with a contraindication who stop treatment are adhering to the strategy. The definition of adherenceand therefore the need for adherence adjustmentthereby depends critically on the definition of the treatment strategy. Although the E9 Addendum makes this point for some estimands, it misses an opportunity for greater clarity by giving center stage to the term intercurrent events, an umbrella word for postrandomization events. Such events include use of rescue medication, use of a medication prohibited by the protocol, discontinuation of treatment, treatment switching, and death. Regulators would be better served if the E9 Addendum recommended that investigators first define the treatment strategies, which would automatically reclassify some so-called intercurrent events as components of the strategy. An example illustrates our recommendations. Consider a cancer trial with a mortality end point in which patients are randomly assigned to first-line therapy A1 or A0. After disease progression, patients will switch to 1 of several second-line therapies B1, B2, B3, and so forth, which are not randomly assigned. The ITT estimand is unsatisfying because it compares treatment strategies assign to A1, then switch to any B after progression versus assign to A0, then switch to any B after progression. Therefore, we would not know whether a difference in overall survival had been caused by the first- or second-line treatment. An alternative estimand that contrasts first-line therapies during the entire follow-up is clinically irrelevant because that never occurs in practice. Further, changing the end point to progression-free survival (thereby making therapy B irrelevant) begs the questions of whether delaying progression without improving survival is clinically meaningful and what treatment should be given after progression. For regulatory decision making, a more relevant estimand for this cancer trial would be defined by a contrast of treatment strategies, such as receive therapy A1 until progression, then receive therapy B3, in a patient population defined by its measured baseline characteristics. Because mortality is the primary end point, informative causal estimands will involve contrasts of treatment strategies that include both first-line and second-line therapies, not just first-line therapies. In summary, the E9 Addendum is a welcome effort to define causal contrasts that go beyond ITT estimands. However, to provide more effective guidance for all types of randomized trials, those designing and interpreting trials should focus on estimands that compare clinically relevant and potentially implementable sustained treatment strategies in groups of patients defined by measurable characteristics. Regardless of the final recommendations of the International Council for Harmonisation, an emphasis on executing trials with high levels of adherence to clinically relevant strategies will reduce the need for estimands other than ITT effect.
