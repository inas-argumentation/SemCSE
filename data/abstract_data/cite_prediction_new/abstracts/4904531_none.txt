Pilomotor seizures: an unusual presentation of limbic encephalitis
A 64-year-old man presented to his general practitioner due to multiple episodes of piloerection accompanied by a ‘‘feeling of anxiety’’, nausea and a sensation of impending loss of consciousness. The episodes had started several months ago, occurred more often in stressful situations and lasted several seconds. These episodes occurred lately on a daily basis. The patient reported no history of head injury, cerebrovascular disease or febrile convulsions, but had a history of heavy smoking, gastro-esophageal reflux disease and healed duodenal ulcer. The patient’s family history was notable mainly for mood disorders. During clinical examination, a 30–40 s episode of bilateral piloerection occurred that was not accompanied by loss of consciousness or paresis in any extremity. On neurologic examination, there were no clinically significant deficits in his cognitive functioning (with the exception of mild difficulties with his short term memory, visuospatial abilities and language), cranial nerves, tendon reflexes and motor and sensory functions. The patient’s mood was mildly depressed, possibly due to social problems. His vital signs were normal and the electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm. Complete blood cell count, coagulation profile, glucose level, C-reactive protein levels, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, cardiac biomarkers, thyroid, renal and liver function tests were normal. Due to the depressed mood, escitalopram was administered, which led to partial amelioration of his depressive symptoms and possibly of the frequency of the piloerection episodes. A brain MRI performed on September 2012 showed enhanced signal intensity in T2 and FLAIR sequences in the interior temporal lobes bilaterally (Fig. 1). Also, serum antibody titers against voltage-gated potassium channels (VGKC) were abnormally elevated (0.193 nM; upper normal limits: 0.084 nM). Oral levetiracetam was initiated, with mild improvement on the frequency of the episodes, and the patient was referred to the Neurology Department, University Hospital of Heraklion, Crete, for further evaluation. The admission electroencephalogram (EEG) did not reveal paroxysmal activity; however, no episode of piloerection occurred during EEG recording. CSF studies showed 17 nucleated cells/lL (97 % lymphocytes), mildly increased protein (54.1 mg/dl), normal glucose (74 mg/dl), normal IgG Index, normal titers of anti-VGKC antibodies and negative PCR testing for HSV and CMV. Serum testing for HCV, HBV, CMV and HIV was negative, as were autoimmune studies (ANA, ANCA, C3, C4, IgA, IgG, IgM). A paraneoplastic or infectious origin of the LE was ruled out through extensive imaging and laboratory workup (chest and abdominal computing tomography, neoplastic serum markers, etc.). The patient refused to undergo endoscopic studies or PET scanning. Following this, corticosteroids were initiated (prednisone 1 mg/Kgr body weight, with gradual tapering) and piloerection episodes improved in frequency and duration.
