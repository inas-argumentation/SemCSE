Corticosteroid‐induced morphological changes in cells of the myeloid lineage
A 60-year-old woman with no known past medical history presented with generalized body swelling. She was found to have nephrotic range proteinuria and an elevated creatinine of 1.9 mg/dL with no known baseline creatinine. White blood cell count was 6,000/mL with an unremarkable manual differential: hemoglobin, 11.5 g/dL and platelet, 249,000/mL. Human immunodeficiency virus was negative, and vitamin B12 and folic acid level were within normal limits. A renal biopsy demonstrated IgA nephropathy with additional features of focal crescent formation. The patient received intravenous pulse corticosteroids, followed by prednisone 60 mg daily. Her symptoms improved, and she was discharged on a monotherapy of prednisone 60 mg daily for a 6 month course. Peripheral blood smears were examined before initiation of steroid therapy, one month after, and after the discontinuation of steroid therapy. The 1-month blood smear showed morphological changes in the nuclei and granules of myeloid cells, which were not present on the initial blood smears and smears after steroid therapy was discontinued. Bone marrow biopsy was not considered due to low suspicion of underlying hematological diseases. In response to chronic steroid exposure, neutrophils displayed a variety of nuclear morphologies with a high degree of heterogeneity (Image 1). We observed ring-shaped nuclei (Image 1A) such as those reported in myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with MDS-related features. Nuclear projections including clubs and hooks (Image 1B,C), previously reported in patients with androgen-secreting tumors [1], were also seen. These clubs and hooks are distinct from “drumstick” projections which represent Barr bodies [1]. We observed hyposegmentation of nuclei (pseudo-Pelger-Hu€et anomaly, Image 1C) [2], which have been documented in MDS, AML with MDS-related features, or severe infections. Hyper-segmented nuclei were also seen (Image 1D), as has been previously reported in vitamin B12 deficiency. We also observed a highly clumped single-lobed nucleus (Image 1E), a form of granulocytic dysplasia seen in MDS or AML with MDS-related features. Multiple nuclear lobes connected with long intersegmental filaments (“Pseudo-myelokathexis”, Image 1F) were seen, as has been shown in the WHIM syndrome (warts, hypogammaglobulinemia, infections, and myelokathexis) [3]. Nuclei of eosinophils also displayed a wide range of morphological changes and heterogeneity in response to steroid exposure (Image 2).
