Quality Assurance, School Self-management and the Contradictions of Control
The often acrimonious debate which educational reform has precipitated is indicative that attempts to effect what constitutes revolutionary change in the education system in Great Britain and Northern Ireland inevitably raise issues about the implications for the balance of power among the various stakeholders in the education system. Although the precise outworking of the 1988 Education Act and the4Education Reform (Northern Ireland) Order 1989 as an adaptation of it is not yet completely clear, there are indications that professional morale in schools is in a sorry state. Finding a coherent effective and workable response to the major changes in ideology and methodology which are precipitated by recent legislation appears to be constituting a serious threat to teacher efficacy and in the long run, therefore, to school effectiveness. The 1988 Education Reform Act has virtually reversed the direction of British educational history in that it has substantially demolished the infrastructure of what was conceptualised as a decentralised educational system. Most notably it abandons the concept of a partnership of central government, local education authorities and teachers held together by consultation, negotiation and by the promise of consensual outcomes. This has been achieved, for example, through the imposition of a 'national' curriculum and its corollary 'national' assessment, and by a process in which the ideological preconceptions of central government have thrust aside the partnership with scarcely disguised contempt. In the latter, professional concerns would appear to have been relegated to the category of vested interest and the defence and protection of inertia. Thereby, the progress towards effective development of the quality of educational provision being achieved through a progressive programme of school-based review launched in the early 1980s has been arrested. Curriculum initiatives in Northern Ireland, such as the Primary Guidelines and the 11-16 Programme, had been perceived and responded to by teachers as offering a real and innovatory opportunity for their commitment and skill development to make an impact and be valued as a significant quid pro quo for the 'grassroots autonomy' which has been a cherished feature of British education.
