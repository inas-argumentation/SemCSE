Response to “a fast and simple method to calculate protonation states in proteins”
“A Fast and Simple Method to Calculate Protonation States in Proteins,” by Mehler and Warshel (MW), is concerned with the importance of electrostatic selfenergies for calculation of protonation states in proteins. This is an important and interesting discussion, which we certainly are willing to enter into with the authors. However, we find the manuscript unnecessarily aggressive. It starts by stating that our article (SE) “embodies some common misconceptions regarding electrostatic effects,” continues by criticizing us for not having cited a number of articles, including four reviews by the authors themselves, and criticizes us for using distance-dependent screening functions suggested by the authors without following their precise prescriptions etc. We agree that they have a legitimate reason to protest our not having cited and discussed their articles. The rest of this criticism is unfair, but we choose not to respond in full detail here. We think it more fruitful and interesting to the readers of this journal to limit our discussion to the scientific issues. Our model is clearly described in SE 1999. It lacks self-energies. This is also clearly stated. Despite this, it describes the large pKa shifts in hen egg-white lysozyme considerably better than Sham et al. and almost as well as Mehler, which will be shown below. Therefore we find the main thesis in MW to be wrong. The main scientific issue is whether it is justified to neglect self-energies compared to interaction energies. The intention of our article was to improve the interaction energy contribution to pKa shifts. We did not include self-energies because we believed them to be relatively small. Further, we did not trust the accuracy of available simple models. Were we right or wrong? The best way to judge this is to compare to available experimental data. MW give data for lysozyme in their comment. However, they focus their comparison on four residues (His15, Tyr20, Glu35, and Tyr53), for which their self-energy gives a contribution that brings the pKa shifts closer to experiment. This is unfair, since there also are residues with large shifts for which the self-energy of MW increases the deviation to experiment. There are large downward experimental shifts for Asp48, 66, and 87 (see Bartik et al.) which are only partially predicted in Table I of MW. We prefer to make a more unbiased comparison by selecting the nine residues with the largest difference between the experimental pKa in the protein and that of the amino acid model compound in water (rmsdL in SE 1999). These shifts are all of the order of 1 or larger. Three of the residues selected by MW are indeed included in this set (Tyr20 is excluded as its shift is not that large). However, the remaining six (Glu7, Asp18, Asp48, Asp66, Asp87, and the C terminus) are not discussed in MW (but are included in their table). We summarize the rmsd for all residues and these nine large shift residues in Table I). Our result without self-energies is almost as good as the MG (Mehler and Guarnieri, 1999) model and much better than the result of Sham et al. (for the six residues of our nine that they have). Thus, our model without selfenergies is about as good as their models with self-energies to predict large shifts in lysozyme. There is, however, a difference between different residues. They have good results for the large upward shift of Glu35 where we clearly fail. On the other hand, they clearly fail to predict the large downward shifts for some of the aspartic acids. However, by combining the self-energies of the MG model with our interaction energies (SE(i)1MW(tr) in Table I), we get a substantial improvement of the agreement with experiment both compared to our model and the MG model.
