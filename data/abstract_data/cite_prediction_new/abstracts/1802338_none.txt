Endolymphatic Optical Coherence Tomography Facilitates Evaluation of the Thoracic Duct Prior to Embolization
To the Editor, The thoracic duct is the most prominent lymphatic conduit in the body and typically enters the thorax at the aortic hiatus, extending cranially to the right of the descending aorta prior to draining into the venous system at the left jugulovenous angle [1, 2]. Though an estimated 40–60% of patients demonstrate this normal course of the thoracic duct, anatomic variability is well documented [1, 2]. This variable course and its proximity to vital intrathoracic structures may predispose patients to thoracic duct disruption [1, 2]. Disruption of the thoracic duct and/ or lymphatic flow disorders may result in chylothoraces, often complicated by respiratory compromise and nutritional depletion [1, 2]. Thoracic duct evaluation has primarily depended on magnetic resonance imaging and pedal or intranodal contrast lymphangiography. More recently, endolymphatic ultrasound has been described as a potential method to evaluate for thoracic duct leak and facilitate thoracic duct embolization [3]. These diagnostic modalities are constrained, however, by anatomic and technical considerations. Optical coherence tomography, with its improved spatial resolution and image contrast, has been described for the visualization of plaques and vascular structures. These characteristics may be advantageous to localize subtle thoracic duct disruptions. This report describes optical coherence tomography for the evaluation of a subtle branch thoracic duct disruption which was not detected on lymphangiography. Institutional review board approval was not required for the preparation of this report. A 35-year-old woman with Gorham–Stout syndrome and periodic chylothorax resulting in recurrent dyspnea refractory to conservative medical management (non-fat diet and octreotide) presented with a moderate left-sided pleural effusion and a pleural fluid triglyceride level of 702 mg/dL. She required half-liter volume thoracenteses on average once a month. Computed tomography imaging demonstrated no chest wall masses contributing to her chylothorax. She was referred for thoracic duct embolization. The patient was placed under general anesthesia, and the left upper extremity, bilateral groins and entire abdomen were prepped. Under ultrasound guidance, 25-gauge spinal needles (Becton Dickinson; Franklin Lakes, New Jersey) were used to cannulate bilateral inguinal lymph nodes. Ethiodized oil (Ethiodol; Guerbet; Villepinte, France) was injected by hand under intermittent fluoroscopy. The contrast-opacified cisterna chyli was punctured under fluoroscopic guidance with a 21-gauge Chiba needle (Cook; Bloomington, Indiana). A 0.018-inch Transend Guidewire (Boston Scientific; Marlborough, Massachusetts) and a 2.4French Progreat microcatheter (Terumo; Tokyo, Japan) were advanced into the thoracic duct with subsequent injection of contrast medium (Isovue 300; Bracco Diagnostics; Monroe Township, NJ), demonstrating a normal appearance of the thoracic duct with no abnormal branching. Multiplanar imaging failed to identify a leak. A decision was made to image the thoracic duct with endolymphatic optical coherence tomography to further evaluate thoracic duct integrity.
