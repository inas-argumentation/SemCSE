Delivering Papillomavirus Vaccines When and Where They’re Most Needed
Technology enabling production of effective vaccines to prevent cervical cancer was developed within 15 years of the proposal by Zur Hausen and colleagues that human papillomaviruses(HPV) might be responsible for anogenital cancer.1 In retrospect, the development phase of the HPV vaccines could be regarded as remarkably short, when compared with development efforts for other less tractable vaccines for viruses such as HCV and HIV. Of course, the immunobiology of HPV infection turned out to be favorable. Antibody directed against conformational determinants on the capsid of the virus was demonstrated host protective for animal papillomavirus infections,2,3 allowing a conventional approach to development of an HPV vaccine based on viral capsids. Indeed, if HPVs could have been grown efficiently in cell culture, the vaccine strategy would have been even simpler than the recombinant approach now adopted. Production of HPV virus like particles(VLPs) in eukaryotic cells using recombinant DNA technology,4-6 and their subsequent purification from cell lysates, has resulted in vaccines shown near 100% effective at preventing infection with the HPV types incorporated in the vaccine,7-9 and cervical pre-cancer associated with those types. The two commercial products, Gardasil®,7 offering protection against HPV types 6 and 11, associated with genital warts, and 16 and 18, associated with genital cancer and Cervarix®,9 offering protection against HPV 16 and 18, are each based on VLP technology. They are the second group of vaccines, after Hepatitis B vaccines, to be marketed based on recombinant proteins, and the first group designed specifically to prevent a cancer. If the aim of a vaccine is cancer prevention, proving vaccine efficacy is not straightforward. Initial clinical studies with the HPV demonstrated that immunisation with a vaccine based on HPV16 VLPs gave near complete protection against detectable HPV16 infection.8 Larger studies, as yet unpublished, have been powered to show efficacy at preventing not only infection but also cervical pre cancer, and the results from these studies (www.fda.gov/ohrms/dockets/ac/06/briefing/2006-4222b-index.htm) have been used to support applications for licencing of Gardasil®. These studies have required a comprehensive understanding of the natural history of HPV infection, which has in large part been determined in response to this need. Surprisingly, the vast majority (>95%) of infections with “high risk” (HR) HPVs regressed spontaneously.10,11 Perhaps less surprising is that regression is dependent on an intact immune system, as demonstrated in patients with HIV.12 The exact component of the adaptive immune system responsible for regression of HR HPV infection remains unclear, with CD4 responses to viral non-structural proteins the most likely candidates.13 Also surprisingly, cervical pre-cancer is a much earlier and commoner complication of acute infection with high risk HPVs in younger women than had been predicted, with a significant incidence as early as two years after primary infection.8 Reconciling these data with a prior linear model of progression of HPV infection to cancer over decades, and with the observed frequency of pre-cancer in older screened populations, has led to significant shifts in the paradigms concerning HPVs role in initiating cervical pre-cancer. It’s now accepted that the molecular and cellular events initiating high grade premalignant change in HPV infected cervical cells can occur very early after infection. Equally clearly, HPV associated high grade cervical precancer lesions, detected through directed biopsy, progress only slowly to become a dominant pathology in the cervix detectable through routine pap smears14,15 and can regress without intervention much more commonly than the figures from observations on regression and progression of advanced Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia in New Zealand would have suggested.16 Fortunately, the revised natural history of HPV infection has allowed studies over periods as short as three years to establish HPV VLP vaccine efficacy against the premalignant precursors of cervical cancer, paving the way for vaccine licensure in the developed world over the last year.
