OPIOID‐INDUCED HYPERALGESIA IN A PATIENT WITH DEMENTIA
To the Editor: Although paradoxical agitation is a wellknown adverse response to benzodiazepines, less well known is the syndrome of opioid-induced hyperalgesia. Presented is what is believed to be the first case of a patient with dementia of the Alzheimer type who developed escalating behavioral disturbances in the context of increasing doses of opioid analgesics. The patient had a 7-year history of progressive dementia complicated over the previous several years by a complex pain syndrome. She experienced a series of pains that initially focused primarily on the abdomen, chest, and shoulder. As time went on, the pain became migratory and severe, leading to numerous consultations and procedures, including on one occasion the extraction of a normal tooth in an effort to relieve persistent facial pain. The patient also had a background of restlessness and anxiety, particularly in the evening, with coexisting sleep disruption. The chronicity of the pain led eventually to the gradually escalating use of scheduled and as-needed use of oxycodone, which was supplemented by OxyContin approximately 16 months before the initial consultation. Increasing pain led to increasing doses of oxycodone and OxyContin in the weeks before that consultation. At the time of consultation, the chief complaint was ‘‘pain all over her body.’’ The pain was so severe that, over the prior 3 weeks, she could not be distracted, and the frequency of pain changed from intermittent to constant. The patient was described as spending most of her day moaning and wincing. She was heard to remark that she ‘‘can’t go on like this,’’ was able to sleep only 2 hours per night, and was nearly constantly agitated and restless. Upon examination, she was a thin, distressed, agitated, anxious woman who repeatedly walked to the sink in the examining room, swished water in her mouth, and spit it out. She also splashed water on her face and rubbed and grabbed at her face and teeth, complaining of pain. She repeatedly pulled down her slacks and splashed water on and grabbed at her perineum. She left the room at least five times to go to the bathroom in a period of half an hour. She also clutched her abdomen complaining of pain and repeatedly tried to remove her clothes while stating ‘‘I’m too cold, I’m too cold.’’ She also removed her shoes and socks because of foot pain. Her facial expression was one of extreme misery, and she appeared tortured by pain. Her husband administered medications, which included at least ten 5-mg tablets of oxycodone as needed throughout the day, along with three (and at times four) 10mg OxyContin tablets. Other relevant medications included memantine 10 mg twice a day, trazodone 50 mg at bedtime, lorazepam 0.5 mg up to four times a day as needed, and acetaminophen 1,000 mg four times a day. Quetiapine 25 mg at bedtime had recently been added. Given the lack of response of her pain despite the escalation of dosage, a syndrome of opioid-induced hyperalgesia was suspected, and it was decided to taper oxycodone. Her medication regimen was concurrently simplified by stopping trazodone and memantine. The dose of quetiapine was gradually increased over a week to 50 mg three times a day to attempt some symptomatic relief. Continued misery of the patient led to inpatient admission to taper all narcotic pain medication. At the conclusion of that admission, the patient had no pain complaints except for some mild, intermittent, long-standing hip pain and was taking no pain medication. She has required no pain medication other than scheduled acetaminophen since that time (8 months later). Opioid-induced hyperalgesia is a distinctive and important emerging syndrome in which a patient becomes more sensitive to pain while receiving opioids for the treatment of pain. The clinical picture was reported more than 100 years ago ‘‘[W]hen dependence on opioids finally becomes an illness of itself, opposite effects like restlessness, sleep disturbance, hyperesthesia, neuralgia and irritability become manifest.’’ The syndrome is seen most often in patients receiving high, escalating doses of opioids. Patients develop new pain complaints that are unrelated to their initial source of pain, and these often become more diffuse and can result in whole-body hyperesthesia and allodynia (pain elicited by normally innocuous stimulation). Accompanying features include agitation, myoclonic jerks, seizures, and delirium. Although the cause is unknown, purported mechanisms include elevation of the morphine metabolite morphine-3-glucoronide, which produces behavioral excitation in rats. Clinicians need to be alert to the possibility that opioids can sensitize pronociceptive pathways, resulting in aggravation of preexisting pain or generating pain sensations that generalize. The key historical finding is an inadequate response to opioids in the presence of escalating unexplained pain, particularly if diffuse allodynia is present or pain is not associated with a site of injury. The key differential is often whether the escalating pain is due to emerging opioid tolerance. Careful history is needed to separate these possibilities, which dictate opposite clinical decisions. Opioidinduced hyperalgesia, although uncommon, is a syndrome that is of great importance particularly in older patients, who are likely to be taking these drugs, and as this case demonstrates, an important potential cause of behavioral disturbance in those with dementia.
