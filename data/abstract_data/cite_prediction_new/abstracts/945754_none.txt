Relation of pulse pressure and blood pressure reduction to the incidence of myocardial infarction.
To the editor I was extremely interested in the article by Madhavan et al "Relation of pulse pressure and blood pressure reduction to the incidence of myocardial infarction" (Hypertension. 1994;23:395401.), and I have personally no doubt as to the relevance of the data. However, I should like to comment on two points: the first one on the J-shaped curve, and the second one on the predictive value of body size on the risk of myocardial infarction. Because of the propagation of a pressure wave along the arterial tree and to the summation between incident and reflected pressure waves, pulse pressure is physiologically higher in peripheral than in central arteries for the same mean arterial pressure. Thus, it is difficult to extrapolate coronary systolic, diastolic, and pulse pressures from brachial pressure measurements. Particularly in younger subjects, systolic blood pressure is expected to be lower and diastolic blood pressure higher at the site of the coronary artery compared with the brachial artery. Subsequently, the finding of a J-shaped curve between brachial diastolic pressure and coronary events should be considered as a simple association and not as a cause-and-effect relation. There is another argument in favor of this interpretation. The so-called J-shaped curve presented in epidemiologic studies represents in fact a steady-state curve. This pattern does not represent at all the primary physiological experiments, which relate acute changes between pressure and flow, thus characterizing coronary pressure-flow curves. As established from studies of pulsatile arterial hemodynamics, pulse pressure is due to the summation between an incident pressure wave and a backward pressure wave. It is the backward pressure wave that may be detrimental to the heart, particularly when it returns during systole, thus causing a mechanical force that may be opposed to ventricular ejection. The backward pressure wave is influenced by several hemodynamic factors, one of the most important being the distance between the heart and reflection sites. When reflection sites are closer to the heart, there is an enhanced backward pressure wave and a higher pulse pressure. This distance is particularly influenced by the length of the vasculature and therefore by body height. This important aspect has been recently recognized in humans, particularly in subjects with end-stage renal disease. It probably explains the incidence of systolic hypertension in older women, whose height is shorter than that of men. Moreover, reduced height is now recognized as an independent factor for cardiovascular risk, particularly for myocardial infarction.1 should be pleased if you could introduce body height in your Cox model in order to evaluate whether this parameter may influence pulse pressure and/or the incidence of myocardial infarction.
