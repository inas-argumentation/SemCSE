Introduction—Modification of plant growth by hormones and other growth regulators
The plant hormones Plants are particularly subject to various environmental stresses which, unlike animals, they are unable to avoid by movement or migration, and hence they have to become adapted to tolerate environmental stresses. This requirement has placed a premium on adaptive plasticity on the plant body, which is partly a result of the indeterminate pattern of growth by continued activity of the growing points of the shoots and roots, but even organs of determinate growth, such as leaves, are capable of great modification by both "internal" and "external" factors. Thus, plants offer exceptional opportunities for modification of their growth and development by man, and such modification is, of course, as old as agriculture itself, since the object of all cultural practices, including tilling, manuring, irrigation and seed selection, is to modify growth in desired directions. Similarly, in horticulture such practices as pruning and disbudding are direct attempts to modify the pattern of growth. However, the introduction of chemical growth regulators adds a new dimension to the possibilities for modifying plant growth. The possibility of using chemical growth regulators first arose as a consequence of the discovery of plant hormones and the availability of highly active synthetic substances in the 1930s. The first group of natural hormones to be discovered were the auxins, now known to be indole derivatives, of which the most widely occurring and active is indol-3yl-acetic acid (IAA). Synthetic IAA became available for horticultural purposes in 1936 and soon after this a considerable number of synthetic compounds was screened by Zimmerman and others, some of which were shown to have high auxin activity, including indolebutyric acid (IBA), a-naphthalene acetic acid (NAA) and other related compounds. The availability of these synthetic auxins soon led to several horticultural uses, the first being for the promotion of rooting in cuttings and soon after came their use for reducing fruit-drop, especially in apples, and for stimulating fruit set and development in various species. The introduction of synthetic auxins, notably 2,4dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) in the United States and 4-chloro-2-methyl phenoxy acetic acid (MCPA) in Britain, as herbicides in the 1940s was another very important landmark in the history of chemical growth regulation. After these exciting developments in the decade 19351945, further advances occurred less rapidly until the recognition of the gihberellins as natural plant hormones in 1955. Since the first determination of the structure of gibberellic acid a large number of related natural gibberellins have been isolated from higher plants and from the fungus, Gibberella jujikuroi, and at the present time over fifty such substances are known. Because of their complex structure it has not yet been possible to synthesize them or to produce synthetic unnatural substances of comparable activity. However, gibberellic acid and several other natural gibberellins of the fungus can be produced commercially by large-scale fermentation.
