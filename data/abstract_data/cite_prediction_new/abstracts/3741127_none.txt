Is scientific excellence a probabilistic consequence of quantity ? On the relationship between productivity , impact , and excellence among early career researchers in mathematics
Bibliometric indicators are increasingly used in science policy to identify and stimulate research excellence by allocating resources on the basis of publication and citation statistics (Abramo, Cicero, & D’angelo 2014). The most commonly used indicators for identifying scientific excellence are citation based indicators of highly cited papers (e.g., top k %; Bornmann 2014; Zitt, Ramanana-Rahary, & Bassecoulard 2005). At the level of individual researchers this would suggest that an excellent researcher is one that produces highly cited articles (Abramo, Cicero, & D’angelo 2014). The use of citation based indicators as decision support tools to concentrate resources at the individual level is based on the assumption that the publication track record of a researcher can be used to predict whether or not that researcher will produce excellent research in the future, i.e., that there is continuity between the researcher’s past and future achievements in science (Danell 2011). However, the information value of citation based measures in the context of selecting for excellence is questioned by the so called constant-probability-of-success model proposed by Simonton (1985; 1997; 2003).
