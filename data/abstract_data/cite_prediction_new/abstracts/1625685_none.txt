Comments on Wassermann et al. (2013): Assessment of ABCG2-mediated transport of xenobiotics across the blood-milk barrier of dairy animals using a new MDCKII in vitro model
In their discussion, these authors claim that “no suitable in vitro model is available to investigate ABCG2 function in the normal blood-milk barrier” and that “we generated for the first time a suitable in vitro cell model for the lactating mammary gland of dairy animals using MDCKII cells expressing bovine or caprine ABCG2”. Finally, in the discussion, Wassermann et al. incorrectly assert that “In this study, we have established for the first time an adequate in vitro model for the normal blood-milk barrier”. In reality, however, a suitable in vitro model for the study of ABCG2-mediated transport across a physiological barrier using MDCKII cells expressing bovine ABCG2 (two variants, wild-type and Y581S) was already established in 2011 by our research group (Real et al. 2011). In this study, we generated new MDCKII cell models stably expressing the wild-type bovine ABCG2 and the Y581S variant as valuable tools for functional analyses of bovine ABCG2, including ABCG2-mediated transport of drugs across membrane barriers. Several fluoroquinolone antibiotics were identified as new bovine ABCG2 substrates in transcellular transport experiments and increased activity of the Y581S bovine variant was reported. In our case (Real et al.), the full-length bovine ABCG2 cDNA was cloned from the bovine liver; in the case of the article by Wassermann et al., it was cloned from the bovine mammary gland. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no report of any difference in the ABCG2 cDNA sequence due to the tissue source used for cloning. Thus, an in vitro model to study ABCG2-mediated transport across a physiologically normal barrier was firstly generated by Real et al. Unfortunately, this reference is omitted in the article by Wassermann et al. Furthermore, MDCKII cells cannot be considered to form a “normal blood-milk barrier” as Wassermann et al. claim, since these cells were originally obtained from canine kidney. They simply form physiological membrane There is considerable concern from public health, food safety, and regulatory perspectives about the presence of drug residues in milk for dairy consumers. The use of antibiotics in adult dairy cows and other food-producing animals can contribute to increase antimicrobial resistance. ABCG2 is the main protein induced in the lactating mammary gland involved in the active secretion of drugs and toxins into milk (Jonker et al. 2005; Merino et al. 2005). As a result, a number of research groups are taking an interest in the emerging field of ABCG2 in dairy animals. Wassermann et al. (2013) recently published the results of a study in which MDCKII cells stably expressing bovine ABCG2 and caprine ABCG2 were used to identify new ABCG2 substrates. This is a very interesting paper which contributes substantially to new knowledge regarding ruminant ABCG2 pharmacology and toxicology. However, Wassermann et al. claim several times throughout the text that they were the first to generate a suitable in vitro cell model to study ABCG2-mediated transport across the physiologically normal blood-milk barrier using MDCKII cells expressing bovine or caprine ABCG2. Several statements with regard to this issue are quoted in the following paragraph. In their abstract, Wassermann et al. stated that “no suitable in vitro model is as yet available to systematically investigate ABCG2-mediated transport of xenobiotics into milk of dairy animals”. In addition, in their introduction, the following sentence appears: “no adequate in vitro model is as yet available to study ABCG2-mediated transport across the physiologically normal blood-milk barrier”.
