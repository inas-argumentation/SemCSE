Outcomes With Cerclage Alone Compared With Cerclage Plus 17α-Hydroxyprogesterone Caproate.
We read with great interest the study by Stetson et al1 on the use of cerclage plus 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate compared with cerclage alone in singleton gestations. There is a paucity of data on this important issue, and their study is a welcome, important addition to the literature. It is particularly important for several reasons. First, it highlights once again the fact that prevention of spontaneous preterm birth often may require more than one therapy. This would make sense, because spontaneous preterm birth is related often to many different causative pathways. It would make sense that cerclage and progesterone can have a cumulative effect, because they have different mechanisms of action—cerclage a more mechanical one and progesterone an anti-inflammatory one. Second, this study is the first, as far as we can tell from reviewing the literature, to include mostly historyindicated cerclage in the assessment of possible cumulative effect between cerclage and progesterone. A few prior studies assessed the possible cumulative effects of progesterone and cerclage. Rebarber et al2 report on singleton gestations with cerclage in place (unclear for which indication), comparing 232 who had 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate and 1,650 who did not. They found no benefit from the addition of 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate to cerclage2 but several possible confounders could not be controlled for. In a secondary analysis of the Vaginal Ultrasound Cerclage Trial,3 Berghella et al4 compared rates of preterm birth in 300 women with singleton pregnancies and a prior spontaneous preterm birth who had transvaginal ultrasound– measured cervical length less than 25 mm before 24 weeks of gestation and received either 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate, ultrasound-indicated cerclage, both, or neither. The incidence of preterm birth at less than 28 weeks of gestation was 25% if women received neither 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate nor cerclage, 17% if they received only cerclage, 15% if they received only 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate, and 9% if they received both.4 Similarly, rates of preterm birth at less than 32 weeks of gestation were 34% if women received neither 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate nor cerclage, 25% if they received cerclage, 21% if they received 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate, and 17% if they received both.4 These data seem to show a clear cumulative effect of 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate and cerclage for women with singleton pregnancies with a prior spontaneous preterm birth and a transvaginal ultrasound– measured cervical length less than 25 mm. Rafael et al5 reported 58 women with singleton gestations with prior spontaneous preterm birth and a transvaginal ultrasound–measured cervical length less than 25 mm who had ultrasound-indicated cerclage. Women who also received 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate had similar rates of recurrent spontaneous preterm birth to those who did not receive 17ahydroxyprogesterone caproate.5 This study had a relatively small sample size and may have lacked statistical power. Szychowski et al,6 in another secondary analysis of the Vaginal Ultrasound Cerclage Trial,3 reported that, in 99 women with singleton gestations, prior spontaneous preterm birth, and a transvaginal ultrasound–measured cervical length less than 25 mm who were receiving 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate, cerclage was associated with nonsignificantly lower rates of preterm birth at less than 37 weeks of gestation (49% compared with 60%, adjusted odds ratio [OR] 0.62, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.27–1.41), less than 35 weeks (30% compared with38%, adjusted OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.27–1.52), and less than 28 weeks (9% compared with15%, adjusted OR 0.46, 95% CI 0.12–1.70), compared with no cerclage. Now Stetson et al1 report that, in 260 singleton gestations with cerclage (about 70% history-indicated, and 26% ultrasound-indicated), the addition of 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate is associated with a 73% reduction in preterm birth at less than 24 weeks of gestation. They do not mention or comment on all prior related studies, and we wonder whether they would comment on them to put their study in the right context with a more complete review of the related literature. Overall, the data from these studies1–6 support a cumulative effect of cerclage and 17a-hydroxyprogesterone caproate in singleton gestations, confirming prior data in particular on those with prior spontaneous preterm birth (for which the progesterone is indicated) and a transvaginal ultrasound–measured cervical length less than 25 mm before 24 weeks of gestation (for which ultrasoundindicated cerclage is recommended). Again, our congratulations to Stetson et al for an important contribution to the treatment of women at risk for preterm birth.
