Accordingly, inhibition of TNF-a activity might theoretically worsen or trigger skin lesions of psoriasis in some patients. In this perspective, it could have been assumed that both patients actually had psoriatic arthritis misdiagnosed as RA and that anti-TNF-a treatment revealed the cutaneous side of the disease. However, the diagnosis of RA was considered to be clinically and radiologically beyond any doubt in both cases. Furthermore, even if the patients did have misdiagnosed psoriatic arthritis, the joint inflammation should also have been worsened by the treatment, provided that skin and joint lesions share similar cytokine profiles in psoriasis. Accordingly, the mechanisms underlying this unexpected cutaneous side-effect remain elusive to date. The fact that both patients had seronegative RA is perhaps coincidental, but further reports of such skin lesions induced by anti-TNF-a drugs will perhaps clarify this point.
