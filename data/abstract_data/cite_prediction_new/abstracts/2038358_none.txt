Ecology Challenged? Who? Why? Where is This Headed?
A primary role for the ecological sciences is to develop an understanding of the environment that is required for managing our natural resource base in a sustainable manner. Regardless of whether we are testing theory, evaluating new techniques, studying life history strategies of species, evaluating management effects, or any of a number of other research endeavors, the ultimate value of such research is to increase our ability to understand the environment. Modern ecological science recognizes that the environment is (a) complex—many interacting factors are involved in ecological processes; (b) dynamic—factors vary over time in complex ways; (c) spatially variable—heterogeneous and exhibits different patterns at different scales; (d) biologically diverse—complex assemblages of thousands of interacting species; and (e) physical-chemicalbiological-social-economically controlled—requiring interdisciplinary efforts to understand ecological patterns and processes. The Ecological Society of America (ESA) is well recognized for its leadership in advancing these aspects of our discipline with publications such as the Sustainable Biosphere Initiative and the ESA Report on Ecosystem Management. This makes it all the more astounding that the 1998 annual ESA meeting provided a remarkable set of keynote talks and symposia that, in effect, questioned the progress of ecology toward these ends.
