Editorial Comment to Impact of insulin resistance, insulin and adiponectin on kidney stones in the Japanese population
Large epidemiological studies have examined the risk of kidney stones associated with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. Metabolic syndrome’s association with kidney stones might reflect multiple overlapping risk factors, including diet (high oxalate, protein and sodium content) and insulin resistance. There are physiological mechanisms to help to explain this association. Obesity and insulin resistance tend to lower the urine pH, possibly as a result of insulin’s effect on P-dependent and P-independent glutaminases. Lower urine pH can precipitate kidney stones by lowering the excretion of citrate, creating a favorable environment for uric acid kidney stones. Although calcium oxalate kidney stones are still more common in obesity, uric acid stones increase more than other stones with increasing body mass index. These data support the association of metabolic syndrome, obesity and insulin resistance with kidney stones. Insulin resistance can be estimated using fasting insulin and glucose, adiponectin, or clinical markers, such as metabolic syndrome. The criteria for the definition of metabolic syndrome can differ; for example, the American Heart Association and National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (AHA/NHLBI) uses a lower fasting blood glucose and a larger waist circumference compared with the International Diabetes Federation (IDF). Variations in the criteria can affect the defined prevalence of metabolic syndrome in a given population. For example, in a Japanese patient, AHA/ NHLBI uses a waist circumference of 102 cm in men; however, IDF criteria is >85 cm. The IDF criteria were used for Ando’s study in this issue of the journal. Ando also used the product of fasting blood glucose and insulin level to estimate insulin resistance, also known as the homeostasis model of assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). In this issue of the journal, Ando et al. examined fasting adiponectin values, insulin, blood sugar and other clinical markers to find an association with a history of self reported kidney stones in 1016 otherwise healthy people. Of these Japanese subjects, 119 had kidney stones and 917 had no history of stones. There was no apparent difference in the adiponectin values; however, fasting insulin correlated with stones formation for women. Age, body mass index, waist circumference, systolic and diastolic blood pressures, HOMA-IR, and insulin were significantly higher in women with kidney stones than without. Obesity and metabolic syndrome had a low prevalence in the study population. In female stone formers, only 5.7% were obese (BMI > 30) and 11.4% had metabolic syndrome. In contrast, only 0.8% of non-stone formers were obese and 4% had metabolic syndrome. These low numbers affected the statistical power of the study. Other studies have shown an association of kidney stones in obesity and metabolic syndrome; however, the association with men tends to be weaker, suggesting that there is a fundamental physiological difference between men and women. Estrogen, which reduces urinary excretion of calcium, is considered a protective factor for kidney stone formation, particularly for surgical menopause. Hormone replacement therapy has not shown promise for a reduction in the prevalence of kidney stone formation. Further studies will need to clarify what the physiological reason is for women with metabolic syndrome having an increased risk for kidney stones. Ando’s study shows an association of insulin resistance and history of kidney stones, that has a predilection for women. Future research should focus on physiological reasons for the sex specific association, and applicable treatments. Further epidemiological studies might be warranted to readdress a potential reduction in kidney stone formation with hormone replacement therapy, or another sex-specific therapy to help reduce the risk of kidney stone formation in women with metabolic syndrome. Current research supports recommending weight loss for obese patients with kidney stones, particularly in women. Although there are no data for a true effect on kidney stone risk reduction, other cardiovascular risk reduction should be a motivating factor for overweight patients. Increased hydration during exercise should be encouraged to avoid having concentrated urine as a result of insensible losses from sweat. Future studies will hopefully show a reduced risk of kidney stones with weight loss, and improved insulin resistance.
