Transfusion‐associated graft‐versus‐host disease in nonimmunocompromised hosts
Transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TAGVHD) resulting from the engraftment of competent lymphocytes contained in blood products has been well described in immunocompromised patients.’.’ These patients have usually had profound cellular immunologic defects from congenital immunodeficiency states, prematurity, a malignancy (usually acute leukemia or lymphoma), or aplasia (from chemotherapy). These patients have been thought to lack sufficient immunocompetence to destroy the mature, viable lymphocytes in the blood product. Because of this risk, some institutions have provided irradiated blood products to all patients with the diseases listed above. However, no national consensus exists for the need to irradiate blood products for all patients with these disorders. With the confusion surrounding TA-GVHD in incompetent hosts, it is not surprising that the first cases of TA-GVHD in immunologically normal hosts were not diagnosed. In Japan, many patients developed an unexplained condition characterized by fever, rash, liver disease, diarrhea, and pancytopenia after surgery. This condition was named postoperative erythroderma (POE). POE was highly fatal and was usually attributed to a severe drug reaction. A subsequent papers from Israel reported two patients, also thought to be immunologically normal, who engrafted related-donor (offspring) blood that was homozygous for one haplotype shared by the patient. These patients are analogous to the experimental model in which the transfusion of lymphocytes from one of the parental strains into F1 hybrid offspring results in lethal GVHD. The offspring in this situation are immunologically normal. The offspring “seesyy nothing foreign because of the common haplotype, whereas the graft c‘sees” the foreign haplotype from the other parent and attacks the recipient. The immunologic competence of the host is irrelevant; the host is incapable of rejecting the transfused lymphocytes. The chance of this happening is obviously greater in patients receiving blood products from first-degree-related donors than from a donor from a large heterogeneous population. However, i f the antigen pool is sufficiently small and homogeneous, a transfusion from an unrelated donor may have the same HLA composition, which would result in TA-GVHD. There is no way to ascertain how many patients die undiagnosed. The classic clinical picture of TA-GVHD is one of overwhelming multiorgan dysfunction: pancytopenia, hepatic failure, diarrhea, gastrointestinal bleeding, and rash. Any combination of these findings may be seen; all need not be present. These findings can be seen in other conditions including sepsis, viremia (particularly with cytomegalovirus), and drug reactions. Since patients receiving blood products are already sick, the symptoms of GVHD may be obscured by other medical problems. Unless TAGVHD is considered, the diagnosis is unlikely to be made. Even if appropriate skin, gut, or liver biopsies are obtained, GVHD is so uncommon a condition that only pathology departments in centers with allogeneic marrow transplant units are likely to feel comfortable wi% the histologic diagnosis. Definitive proof of the engraftment of foreign lymphocytes is often impossible because of the pancytopenia of the patient. One of the normal patients who developed POE did have typing showing engraftment of major histocompatibility complex-mismatched blood.7 This patient engrafted cells that should have been recognized as foreign and rejected. The only possible mechanism for this patient’s TA-GVHD was that the patient was immunologically incapable of rejecting the graft even though there were recognizable histocompatibility differences between the donor and host.
