Correction to “External hydrocephalus in infants: six cases with MR venogram and flow quantification correlation”
Dear Editor, I wish to point out some errors in the paper “External hydrocephalus in infants: six cases with MR venogram and flow quantification correlation” [1], which became apparent after it was published online. The paper utilized an MR phase contrast technique to measure blood flow in the arteries and sagittal sinuses of six infants with external hydrocephalus and compared these with six controls. The net aqueduct flow rate was also measured with this technique. The scanner used was a relatively new 3-T unit. It became apparent during follow-up studies that the default setting for the flow quantification software, as supplied by the vendor, disables the background correction even if a reference region of interest is specified. If the background correction is enabled by selecting it in the software, when the scanner is rebooted each morning, the background correction is again disabled. This means, unbeknownst to the researchers, that the data published were processed without background correction. The effect of this was to add some spurious flow to the measurements due to (1) movement of the brain during the cardiac cycle (probably not significant) and (2) to the phase offset error of the scanner. The phase offset error is due to eddy currents in the gradient coils and is an inherent error in each machine which must be removed from all measurements, by estimating its magnitude in an adjacent stationary region and subtracting it from the region of interest [2]. Phantom experiments show that the phase offset error for this scanner is approximately +5 mm/s for each velocity measurement. With this being the same across all velocity encoding values used. The mean velocity for each data point in the arterial measurements was approximately 20 cm/s giving a systematic error of 2.5%. The mean velocity of the venous measurements was 10 cm/s giving a systematic error of 5%. These errors are within the expected error range of the technique, which is about ±10% [3]. As the controls and the patients were affected equally, no change in the differences noted, or the interpretation of these differences came about. The net aqueduct flow measurements are however, much more likely to be affected by phase offset errors. Although the peak flow in the aqueducts was about 12 cm/s, because the flow is bidirectional, the average velocity is close to zero, so the offset error greatly inflated the net flow. The recalculation of the aqueduct flow from the original raw data, with background correction turned on, showed that the net flow for the controls was 0.21±0.12 ml/s i.e., flow from the subarachnoid space refluxing into the ventricles. The net flow for the external hydrocephalus patients was reversed being −0.30±0.37 ml/min i.e., from the ventricles into the subarachnoid space. The change in flow between control and external hydrocephalus was significant, p=0.01. These findings do not change the conclusions of the original paper however, as the flow in the controls remained into the ventricles which suggests G. A.
