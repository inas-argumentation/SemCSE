Editorial overview: Insect pheromones: making sense of a rapidly diversifying field of study.
Studies of insect pheromones have diversified in both depth and breadth over the decades since the first pheromone was identified and the term coined in 1959 [1,2]. The number of papers dealing with pheromones continues to grow, with over 30 000 publications to date (31 956; Web of Science, September 2017; Figure 1a). Chemistry and behavior when appropriately linked together have led to the identification of thousands of pheromones (http://www.pherobase.com) mediating mating, aggregation, trail following, alarm and many other interactions. The field once dominated by studies of sex and aggregation pheromones (Figure 1b,c; e.g. moths and bark beetles), and trail and reproductive pheromones of a few social insects, now includes increasingly diverse taxa and behavioral interactions. There has been a gradual shift from taxon specific research to studies with potentially broader impact, such as studies of signal production, signal transduction, and their genetic control. The early interest in the potential for pheromones to be a part of pest management has been sustained (Figure 1d), and in many cases this potential has been realized. The growing importance of molecular biology, biochemistry, neurosciences, behavior and evolutionary biology promises to give new insights into pheromone biology. Pheromone studies, indeed, are contributing to our understanding of sensory processing of information, central integration of that information, evolution of communication, and speciation (see [3,4] for broad reviews).
