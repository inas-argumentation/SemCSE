Implications of participant observation in medical studies.
Rosenhan1 has described an investigation in a psychiatric hospital in which the investigators, using the technique of participant observation, assumed the roles of patients to find out whether hospital staff would be able to identify them as healthy individuals rather than mental patients. The startling finding was that 8 of the 10 role-playing "patients" escaped detection for the entire period of the study a finding of much import for the planning of hospital psychiatric services. A similar example in the context of general medicine is now provided by the study of Buckingham and colleagues reported in this issue of the Journal (page 1211). The methodologic innovation of these two studies raises important ethical and practical questions for medical anthropologists and physicians, especially concerning secrecy. An important question concerns the validity of the data collected in this manner. For what types of research problems are such data useful? Can, or should, the observations of a pseudopatient be checked against data collected by more traditional methods? How do you measure "death with dignity"? How do you measure "quality of life"? The health sciences have no uniform answers to these questions, for any answers relate as much to our cultural beliefs and attitudes as they do to any organized body of knowledge, and participant observation may provide the health professions with a new evaluation tool. But what are the possible long-range effects of such research on the medical anthropologist's i'elation with the health professions as a whole? Is participant observation a valid method of exploring consumer reaction to health care? Should limits be imposed, or developed, on the use of this new method of research? These issues must be considered by physicians and anthropologists, especially if this research method is to be used more widely in the health field. Buckingham and colleagues describe the use of role-playing in a setting quite different from that of the psychiatric hospital, but their study suggests some interesting methodologic comparisons with Rosenhan's study. The study described by Buckingham and his associates was part of an evaluation of the palliative care unit of the Royal Victoria Hospital, Montreal. The primary goal of this unit has been to meet the special needs of dying patients within the hospital setting.2 (Other programs, such as that of the Hospice centre in New Haven, Connecticut, concentrate currently on supportive home care, and St. Christopher's Hospice and St. Joseph's Hospice in London, England each have a free-standing, independent facility.) To meet these needs, new rules and operating procedures emphasize the individuality of patients, and the continuous accessibility of the patient to his family and friends has been established. Staff for the Montreal unit underwent special training to sensitize them to dying patients' needs and to ensure their understanding and compliance with the changed procedures. After 1 year of operation the hospital administration and the funding agencies involved were anxious to know whether the goals of the unit were being met, and whether the unit should be continued in its present form, modified, or abandoned entirely in favour of some other approach. A technique of evaluation was therefore sought. Evaluation of the palliative care unit, however, posed special problems. Those who had created the unit and knew it best were also deeply committed to it, and could hardly provide a completely independent, objective assessment of it; and since the focal centre of the unit was the dying patient and his relatives and friends, the question arose as to how their views could be obtained in an unbiased way. Certainly an objective method of evaluating this type of care is greatly needed: no empirical studies incorporating the total concept have emerged from existing hospice programs because a satisfactory technique of measurement is difficult to develop. A further problem was that if the investigator was associated in any way with an identifiable medical role, bias would invalidate much of the information. The services of a medical anthropologist (R.W.B.) with prior experience in palliative care evaluation were therefore sought and the method of participant observation was adopted as a means of collecting data. This technique required meticulous planning to make the investigator's patient role plausible, consistent and undetectable by professional medical staff. The plan had to be flexible enough to cover many possible contingencies within the hospital that could not be entirely anticipated, and constructed in such a way as not to endanger the real health of the investigator and still allow him to observe and record data in the hospital. With careful planning and preparation nothing went wrong. An auxiliary role was that of the pseudopatient's "cousin" (W.B. M.), who was required to do little more than accompany the pseudopatient through admission and discharge procedures at the hospital and to make a daily visit (lasting from 30 to 60 minutes) for the 9-day duration of the hospitalization. These visits allowed the "cousin" to monitor the pseudopatient's wellbeing, to observe the hospital milieu being studied by the principal investigator and to convey information unobtrusively to and from the pseudopatient, and from this limited experience some minimal contributions were made. The major concern was to ensure the comfort, safety and productivity of the hospital stay, and the attention of the "cousin.. was focused on the pseudopatient at all times.
