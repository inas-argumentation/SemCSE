Despite the signiWcant progress achieved in cancer immunotherapy and in our understanding of the nature of the anti-tumor immune response, it is increasingly evident that cancer vaccination remains less eVective than required for many types of malignancy. Clinical applications of cancer vaccination aimed at enhancing T-cell recognition and elimination of malignant cells appear to have proceeded faster than the studies required to determine interaction mechanisms of the host immune system with the growing tumor. A wide variety of factors may account for the limited success of current immunotherapeutic strategies, e.g., failure of eVector cells to compete with growing tumor burden, production of humoral factors by the tumor that locally block cytotoxicity, antigen/MHC loss, T-cell dysfunction, or production of suppressor T cells and many other factors [1–3]. Considerable attention has recently been paid to immune escape mechanisms that may result from a dysfunction of the host immune system or an immunosuppressive eVect of the tumor microenvironment. The major obstacle to successful immunotherapy is the ability of tumors to evolve into resistant variants under the pressure of immune selection. This may explain the limited response in various ongoing clinical trials. Therefore, it is very important to develop novel strategies to counter tumor resistance mechanisms. In order to optimize immunotherapy strategies, there needs to be simultaneous consideration of the stimulation of T-cell activating signals, suppression of inhibitory factors, and elimination of newly developed resistant tumor phenotypes. The therapy that activates multiple responses will probably be the most eVective approach. Clinical monitoring of the patients’ immune response to treatment is crucial for the timely detection of newly emerging tumor immune escape variants and the appropriate optimization of the therapy. The series of international conferences “progress in vaccination against cancer” (PIVAC) is dedicated to discussing the most recent advances and limitations in cancer immunotherapy in the format of intensive interactions between basic scientists and clinicians in meetings limited in size to 100 participants. The sixth PIVAC meeting was held at the Virgen de las Nieves University Hospital in Granada, Spain, on 28–29 September 2006. This summary provides a general overview of the diVerent aspects of cancer immunotherapy discussed at the conference and describes some particular contributions of the participants.
