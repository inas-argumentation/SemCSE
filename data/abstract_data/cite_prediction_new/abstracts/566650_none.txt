Desertification, Development, and the “Admirals” of Manchar Lake in Sind, Pakistan
In Pakistan's extremely arid to seasonally dry, sub-humid climate, rainfall is generally unreliable. Its failure in single years is common, and droughts lasting several years are the fate of every generation. At the same time, floods and torrential storms are of frequent occurrence, indirectly promoting desertification through erosion or deposition of sterile sediment. Meanwhile, there is a legacy of desertification going back at least 5,000 years. Although some is attributable to climatic change, deterioration is often associated with human mismanagement of precarious rainfall, fragile vegetation communities, and exotic surface waters [22, p. 7; 4; 20; 9; 19; 5; 7]. None of the major types of economy seem immune from blame here: not the nomadic herdsmen nor the peasant agriculturalists; not the cultivators of the irrigated Indus lowlands nor of the terraced mountainsides of the north. Least of all are the various "civilizations" blameless. Ancient Harappa, once a flourishing city of the Second Millennium B.C., is now a waste saline alluvium probably induced by irrigation. Recently, away from the richer and fast developing zones of intensive irrigation agriculture, overgrazing, destruction of vegetation cover, and accelerated erosion have been increasing sharply [6; 15; 21]. The age-old and continuing complexities of social and cultural upheavals in and around the Indus valley are an important factor in the failure to establish land-use systems that have demonstrable long-term stability [11].
