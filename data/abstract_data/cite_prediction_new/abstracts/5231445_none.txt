Stochastic Analysis with Financial Applications, by Arturo Kohatsu-Higa, Nicolas Privault and Shuenn-Jyi Sheu (Eds.)
Stochastic analysis has a long history of challenges and achievements. Highlighting just two of these will help underscore the contribution of this recent book, Stochastic Analysis with Financial Applications (SAwFA). First, Merton (1974) reframed corporate debt as being an option on a firm’s underlying asset value. Nevertheless, while realizing huge practical success, this reliance on Brownian motion doomed it to: a) systematically under-price short-term instruments, and b) fall short of the ‘fatter than Gaussian’ tails typically found in financial data. A separate milestone came in 1997 when Artzner et al. (1999) at once demonstrated the ‘incoherence’ of one of the financial markets most applied risk measures: the Value-at-Risk (VaR), a loss percentile tail statistic. At that time, and to this day, VaR was canonized in both industry practice and international banking capital requirements, e.g. the Basel II Accord (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision 2004). The shortcomings of strictly Brownian/Gaussian diffusion have been recognized since the earliest days when they were first applied to financial data. Mixed Jump/ Diffusion, Cox, and Lévy processes each overcome, by some degree, the twin problems of accommodating nonzero very short-term rates, as well as, yielding fatterthan-Normal tails in distributions of price changes. Seven of this book’s 21 articles apply and explore the properties of Lévy processes. For example, two papers (Ishikawa Ch.8, and, Øksendal and Sulem Ch.12), offer complementary findings for optimal stopping and stochastic control respectively. Stopping arises often enough when dealing with financial problems, for instance, when modeling the expiration date of a call/put option. 2012, Springer Basel
