Fetal lung fibroblasts secrete and respond to insulin-like growth factors.
The somatomedin hypothesis was proposed in 1957 to ex­ plain the failure of growth hormone (GH) to stimulate ana­ bolic response in hypophysectomized rat cartilage in vitro (1). A GH-dependent factor in serum was found to be directly active in vitro. The source of the serum factor was originally unknown, but there is now abundant evidence that it arises mainly from the liver in postnatal life. Progress in this field in the past three decades has greatly expanded our understanding of the significance of the somato­ medins (for a recent review, see [2]). The difficult task of iso­ lation and characterization of the primary structures of two somatomedin peptides, known as insulin-like growth factors (lGF) I and II, was achieved by Rinderknecht and Humbel in 1978 (3, 4), and the subsequent biosynthesis of these pep­ tides by recombinant techniques has made possible a great increase in the number of investigations of the significance of the IGFs in many tissues. We now know that IGFs are syn­ thesized in many tissues. This is particularly true during fetal life, when almost all cell types express the IGF genes, inde­ pendent of GH action, in response to the requirements for the local regulation of mitogenesis and anabolic processes. It appears that autocrine and paracrine modes of action are paramount in fetal life, whereas the endocrine mode of IGF action appears to predominate in postnatal life. In an article by Stiles and associates (5), which appears in this issue, evidence is presented that rat lung fibroblasts contain IGF-I mRNA and release IGF-I peptide and its bind­ ing protein and that endogenous EGF acts at least in part through the IGF-I receptor. This article, therefore, extends earlier work of Atkinson and colleagues (6), who found that human fetal lung fibroblasts release IGF into conditioned medium as detected by an RIA that we now know detects pri­ marily IGF-I. The article of Stiles and associates also illustrates some of the technical problems encountered in somatomedin re­ search. All cells that secrete IGFs also secrete one or both of the two principal IGF-binding proteins which may modify IGF action in either a positive or negative direction.
