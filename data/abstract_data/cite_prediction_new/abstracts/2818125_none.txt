Nativity and the distribution of wealth: Chicago 1870
This study adds to the limited evidence of American wealth in the 19th century by comparing ownership patterns for different nationality groups in the city of Chicago in 1870. The growth of Chicago, which experienced a dramatic population increase in the late 19th century, can be directly attributed to the congregation of diverse ethnic cultures largely from Europe. The Germans were the largest single ethnic group in Chicago throughout the 19th century. The Irish came next in numbers. Fewer in number were the Scandinavians, including Danes, Norwegians, and Swedes. Alongside these lived emigrants from English-speaking countries, including England, Scotland, Wales, and Canada. The empirical evidence of wealth-holding patterns in American history has been largely limited to urban areas. James Henretta (1970) found significant skewness in colonial Boston, while the 19th century studies of Gallman (1969) and Soltow (1975a,b) have found large gini coefficients signifying a disproportionate share of income and/or wealth in the hands of a few families or individuals. Pessen (1971, 1973) found very unequal wealth distribution and very limited economic mobility in four large northeastern cities in the antebellum period. His study of the rich led him to conclude that the egalitarian hypothesis was not sustained. In the continuing debate, Gallman (1978) pointed out that egalitarian qualities could not be inferred merely from the concentration of wealth in the United States. The key issue is “relative” inequality and the proper standard of comparison is the contemporaneous state of affairs in other societies. Pursuing this theme. Atack and Bateman (1981a,b) found wealth in the rural North on the eve of the Civil War to be more evenly distributed than that in the slave South or in urban areas of the time. While systematic evidence is limited on overall distribution patterns, it is even rarer concerning the wealth of separate nationalities or ethnic groupings. Soltow’s work (1971) is one of the few available. He analyzed separate nativity groups’ distributions for the state of Wisconsin in 1860.
