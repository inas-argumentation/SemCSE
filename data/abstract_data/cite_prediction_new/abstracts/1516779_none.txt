Social and Environmental Accounting Research: Homogeneity, Faddishness and Herding at the Expense Social Significance?
The present issue of Social and Environmental Accountability Journal (SEAJ) opens up an important debate about the future of social and environmental accounting research (SEAR). The first paper in the issue, by Correa and Laine offers a bold and thoughtprovoking reflection on the field of SEAR. By drawing attention to what the authors describe as ‘conformity’ within the field, Correa and Laine raise concerns that SEAR is (i) paying less attention to issues that really matter, and (ii) failing to challenge the taken-for-granted assumptions that underpin much of the research in this area. The authors make an impassioned call for SEAR scholars to stop going through the motions, engage in self-reflection and start challenging the ‘orthodoxy of thoughts and beliefs’ in the field. Correa and Laine point to homogeneity, faddishness and herding in SEAR and question whether much research in this area has any social significance. The authors provocatively ask, has ‘careerism . . . overtaken broader social concerns?’ Given the importance of the issues raised by Correa and Laine, and the impassioned way in which these issues are raised, the Editors felt it was fitting to invite two esteemed members of the SEAR community to comment and share their own reflections. In the first commentary, Thomson suggests that Correa and Laine’s version of the SEAR community does not necessarily conform to his own view – nevertheless, he does recognise that aspects of the research community seem ‘uniformed’ and ‘unreflective’. In considering the current state of SEAR, Thomson also points to some structural issues which might prevent impassioned SEAR research from being heard more widely – for example, most mainstream accounting journals still do not publish such research. Nevertheless, as Thomson points out, SEAR was much more marginalised 25 years ago than it is today.
