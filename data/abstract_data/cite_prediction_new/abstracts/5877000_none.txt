A Team Production Model of Corporate Governance Revisited
This is a substantially revised version of the previous version of this article. A post-Enron consensus on corporate governance has emerged among investor groups, government regulators, and private sector market overseers. The corporate board should function principally as an oversight body for shareholders. To ensure the board's independence from management, the board should have a substantial majority of independent, outside directors, who neither have business relationships to the firm nor social relationships with management. Some proposals go further by only allowing one insider on the board, the CEO. But, all the proposals call for the board's audit, compensation and nomination committees to be comprised exclusively of outsiders. In general, we call this the shareholder or investor model of corporate governance. Corporate governance looks quite different if one works from the perspective that the firm functions as a cooperative team to produce new wealth. From this "production" perspective, corporate governance recommendations differ substantially from the recent reform proposals. In general, these recommendations must support the coalition of interests that embody and perpetuate the firm's core capabilities. Rather than be principally a monitoring body, the corporate board should be a deliberative forum. Rather than have directors solely represent shareholder interests, boards should represent those interest groups among the firm's stakeholders that add value and assume unique risk. And rather than keep the number of insiders to one, boards should include a select number of employees (managers and workers) who bring the firm's know-how to the table. However, the audit, compensation and nomination committees should be comprised solely of independent, outside directors. Our production or innovation model for corporate governance has far-ranging implications. The recommendations derived from our model suggest that the latest reform proposals offered by the New York Stock Exchange, the Conference Board, the Business Roundtable, and the Securities and Exchange Commission create a governance board that does not adequately consider the firm as a new wealth-producing association.
