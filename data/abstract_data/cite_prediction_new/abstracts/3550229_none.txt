Playing cowboys and Africans: Hollywood and the cultural politics of African identity
In his introduction to Africans on African-Americans, Yekutiel Gershoni relates a discussion he once had with the African historian L.H. Gann. The most popular films among African miners in Northern Rhodesia’s Copperbelt, Gann informed him, were Wild West films, and their ‘favorite recreation’ was simulating the exploits of American cowboys. Gershoni warns us, however, that ‘this and similar information is not the type of material that is reported in official documents [. . . and] is difficult to obtain. Much of it passes without a trace.’ Focusing primarily on South Africa and Anglophone Africa, this paper attempts to recover some of these arcane traces—to throw light on the cultural interface between the mimetic proclivities of the African underclasses and the flickering images of American cowboys. Post-colonial critics of the importation of Hollywood’s wares into the subcontinent, I contend, often marginalize the experiences of the very populations on whose behalf they profess to speak. In fact, Wild West films, which were watched with so much ardor, offered an organizing theme, whereby migrant laborers and newly urbanized blacks distanced themselves from the dilatory tempo of African rural life, subsumed ethnic differences, resisted the introduction of time-work discipline, and, in the words of Jonathan Haynes, constructed the very type of ‘dialogized, fragmented, and multiple consciousness that goes with urban life.’ Although this sign manipulation and identity transformation by young males constituted, in part, a symbolic dialogue with ruling whites, they more crucially reflect the growing tensions materializing within African communities during a period of rapid industrialization and precipitous social change.
