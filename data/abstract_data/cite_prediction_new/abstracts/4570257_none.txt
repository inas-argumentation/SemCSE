The Striking Superiority of Social Security in the Provision of Wage Insurance
From the original opposition to Social Security in the 1930s, through the controversy over the 2005 privatization proposals of George W. Bush, and now extending to the current Social Security debate and the so-called Bowles-Simpson proposal, the basic function of Social Security has been misrepresented, obscured, and misunderstood. Social Security is not a program of forced savings for life’s expensive contingencies; nor is it a welfare program designed simply to relieve poverty. Rather, Social Security has from its inception been a program of wage insurance. Social Security insures wages against loss due to death, disability, or old age. Universal wage insurance performs an extremely valuable social function. As this Article demonstrates, no private arrangement designed to replace wages in the event of retirement, disability, or death can come even remotely close to Social Security in efficiency of its administration, security of its defined benefits, fairness of its distribution, or reach of its coverage. In light of a looming retirement income crisis, the United States should expand Social Security, while also taking the modest steps necessary to restore the program to long-range actuarial balance.
