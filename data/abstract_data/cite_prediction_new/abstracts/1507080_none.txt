Reply to: Threshold of number of rib fractures in elderly blunt trauma: A simple or complex matter of numbers?
Dear Editors: First, we thank Drs Dikmen, Delis, and Esquinas for their interest in and thorough review of our recent article “Number of Rib Fractures Thresholds Independently Predict Worse Outcomes in Older Patients with Blunt Trauma.” We agree that there are many complex clinical considerations that affect outcomes in the elderly population with thoracic trauma; however, we think accounting for all possible factors in this highly heterogeneous population would be beyond the scope of any single multicenter study. We would like to address the concerns raised by Dikmen et al. While we agree that coexisting injuries can alter a patient’s course and prognosis, we have accounted for this variability in our cohort. We used the Injury Severity Score (ISS) to control for overall trauma burden. The ISS accounts for the 3 highest Abbreviated Injury Scale scores, which includes a head trauma score when appropriate. Admittedly, using solely ISS introduces ambiguity with respect to the location of coexisting injuries; however, when reviewing the current trauma literature, ISS is often used as an accurate surrogate and predictor of a patient’s global trauma burden. In addition to controlling for trauma burden with ISS, we also recognized that medical comorbidities, especially in the elderly trauma population, have a substantial impact on patient outcomes. In fact, frailty has been shown to be highly predictive of outcomes in this elderly trauma population. We thought our novel use of 24 different comorbidities from our database was the best approach to control for the high-prevalence of frailty among this elderly cohort. We understand that ventilation strategies and therapeutic interventions can impact the outcomes of thoracic trauma. Unfortunately, limitations of the database precluded us from completely assessing these nuances in our study. Within the National Trauma Data Bank, there is no way to extract the strategies of pulmonary toilet, such as noninvasive continuous positive airway pressure, bi-level positive airway pressure, and patient compliance with inspiratory spirometry. Details regarding specific ventilator settings are not available data points. In fact, studies have not demonstrated one particular ventilator setting to improve pulmonary outcomes substantially in patients with thoracic trauma. Another important therapeutic intervention shown to improve outcomes in patients with rib fractures is appropriate pain control. Once again, individual doses and durations of pain medications are not available data points. Epidural analgesia was utilized in <2 percent of our population (1,101 of 67,695 patients) but did not significantly decrease mortality in this cohort (P = .08). We agree that a large database analysis such as ours shows an incomplete picture of each individual trauma patient, but we acknowledge this as an inherent issue with using large data sets. Attempting to control for every facet of patient care leads to statistical overfitting. Furthermore, it should be noted that retrospective reviews can only show correlation and not causation between any treatment intervention and outcome measure. Despite their limitations, large databases allow the unique ability to identify persons at greatest risk for worse outcomes who may benefit from closer monitoring and more aggressive care. Additionally, large, prospective, placebo-controlled, randomized controlled trials are costly and especially difficult in the setting of trauma given issues of informed consent in this emergent and critically ill population. As a result, large database studies such as ours can be invaluable in identifying populations that would benefit from a randomized, controlled trials to better understand the impact of specific therapeutic interventions. As such, our work is the first to show the importance of a threshold number of rib fractures among an elderly population and is an excellent stepping stone for further research.
