Ultrasonographic diagnosis of neonatal spigelian hernia
Received: 29 April 2003 Accepted: 7 May 2003 Published online: 13 September 2003 Springer-Verlag 2003 Sir, We read with interest the recent article by Levy et al. on pre-operative sonographic diagnosis of spigelian hernia (SH) [1]. Most adult SHs are small and intraparietal, so imaging studies are usually needed to make the diagnosis [2]; few of the 33 published cases on pediatric SH were diagnosed with US or CT scans [3]. The Levy team used ultrasonography to make an emergency US diagnosis of SH in two neonates in whom the diagnosis was uncertain. The demographic and clinical data provided in the article by Levy et al. are incomplete and not entirely correct in some other respects. A recent review of the English language literature [3] found that 53% of the pediatric SH patients were aged 0–9 months, including 6 newborns; there was a 1.4:1 male-to-female ratio; the hernia was congenital in 24%, with 15% of the SHs bilateral. Both sides were about equally affected (20 left versus 18 right). Twelve percent had coexisting abdominal wall and 6% diaphragmatic hernias, and 21% had nondescended testicles [3]. The accuracy of statistical data is particularly important in diagnosing very young patients, who are unable to provide a history. We would argue with the authors’ contention that the diagnosis of pediatric SH is notoriously difficult and usually made at exploratory laparotomy [1]. SH bulges are uniformly visible in pediatric patients, with a physical diagnosis made in 82% of the cases [3], probably because of the thin abdominal muscles or tissue laxity. A physical examination in adult SH patients is more difficult because few of them bulge [2]. We would further disagree with the conclusion that SHs can be post-traumatic or postoperative [1]. Spigelian hernia is defined as spontaneous ventral hernia [2]. A literature review on handlebar hernia revealed 13 pediatric patients whose hernias were located at or near the junction of the semilunar and semicircular lines, the typical SH site [1]; the designation ‘‘spigelian hernia’’ was used in some reports and ‘‘traumatic hernia’’ in others [3]. The occurrence of a handlebar hernia at the typical SH site follows a random injury and has been similarly described in other regions, including the inguinal and lumbar regions [3]. Handlebar hernias, which are by definition post-traumatic rather than spontaneous, should therefore be excluded from SH statistics, even when they occur at sites often associated with SH. In only two of the published pediatric SH cases did the hernias occur after surgery for other pathology, in both cases with incisions remote from the typical SH sites [3].
