Editorial: What have we learned from studies of migrants?
Migrant studies have utilized the variation in cancer incidence among countries to assess the relative importance of environmental and genetic influences in cancer etiology, and to gain insight into the time relationships among environmental exposures and appearance of cancer. Cancer risks have been compared between populations of similar genetic background but living in different physical, social, and cultural environments, and among populations of different genetic backgrounds living in the same geographic environment. The major contribution of the earliest formal studies, which were of Japanese and Chinese migrants to the United States, was to demonstrate that, for most cancer sites, the risk changed towards that of the host country, over time and from one generation to the next. (For a review of migrant studies and their methodology, see References 1 and 2). Israel, Australia, and Britain, each with large immigrant populations, also have been fruitful sources of investigation. Migrant studies predominantly have used data routinely collected for cancer registration or death certification, together with population data from censuses, to calculate cancer rates in migrants which have been compared with those of the native-born in the host country and, when available, those in the country of origin. Measurement of changes in rates according to age at migration or duration of residence in the host country, possible for migrants to Israel and Australia, has allowed inferences to be drawn about the stage of carcinogenesis at which environmental factors might act. Comparisons between migrants and their offspring in Israel, the US, and Canada have permitted deductions about genetic susceptibility but, at the population level, these cannot be distinguished from persisting cultural influences. Comparisons among countries with two-way migration, case-control studies utilizing personal interviews of migrants, and cohort studies have been uncommon and only rarely has exposure to possible etiologic factors been assessed for the migrant population. In this issue of Cancer Causes and Control, Iscovich and Howe describe the changing pattern of cancer at various sites over time since migration to Israel from the former Soviet Union. Cancers known from other epidemiologic studies generally to be associated with low socioeconomic status (stomach, cervix, mouth and pharynx, larynx) became proportionately less common, while the opposite was true for cancers generally linked with a higher standard of living (breast, colon, kidney). There was no particular trend for the majority of smokingrelated sites. The likelihood of greater accessibility in Israel than in the former Soviet Union to protective influences such as oral contraceptives (ovarian cancer), cervical smears (cervical cancer), and fruit and vegetables (stomach cancer) were suggested as contributing to the trends. Interpretation of migrant studies is not straightforward, as those who emigrate are self-selected and clearly not representative of the population in their country of birth with respect to ethnicity, religion, socioeconomic status, occupation or health – all indicators of cancer risk, and thus potential confounders for which it is rarely possible to adjust. Bias also will be introduced into migrant studies when there is variation in the quality of data from different sources, if the numerator and denominator used for calculating rates are mismatched (infrequent censuses, different definitions of country or region), if migrants use medical services to a lesser (or greater) extent than those born in the host country and, in mortality studies, when migrants return to their country of birth to die. In addition, temporal trends in cancer risk may be different in the migrants and the host country, necessitating adjustment for time period when calculating the effect of duration of stay. Duration of stay has been taken as a surrogate for cultural change usually with no supporting data. Aspects of diet, for example, may alter immediately on migration or may remain little changed well into the second generation.
