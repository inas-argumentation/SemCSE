Treatment of Carotid In-Stent Stenosis: “To Stent or Not to Stent”
‘‘To be or not to be’’ is one of the most wellknown Shakespearean quotations, but its meaning is the subject of disagreement, just as in the treatment of carotid in-stent stenosis (ISS). The questions regarding the management of carotid ISS, the presence of symptoms, and the difficulty of the treatment give rise to two main issues: ‘‘to treat or not to treat’’ and ‘‘to stent or not to stent.’’ Many studies concerning carotid artery stenting (CAS) have shown that, in most cases, carotid ISS is asymptomatic. The treatment of asymptomatic lesions must be conservative, at least at this moment, until the natural history of ISS is well defined, and its long-term evolution is known. If carotid ISS presents with symptoms, then the lesion has to be treated because the symptom mechanism is similar to native vessel stenosis. At least theoretically, the management should be similar as well, including the use of an embolic protection device and stent implantation. However, many studies reported very good results using simple balloon dilation or cutting balloons for carotid ISS. Even if symptoms are not of major concern, the treatment is still controversial as ‘‘to stent or not to stent’’ a potentially embolic lesion. In the study by Reimers et al., 69% of the patients had only balloon angioplasty for carotid ISS, while Younis et al. published a series in which 73% of the patients were stented. In this issue of JEVT, Tekieli et al. demonstrate their choice for a second stent: a balloon-expandable drug-eluting stent (DES). In a recent review, the most commonly reported treatments for carotid ISS are balloon angioplasty or cutting balloon (54%) vs. stenting (31%). Clearly, the optimal management of symptomatic carotid ISS is not well defined, and some guidelines are necessary. We think that several aspects of carotid ISS should be kept in mind when selecting the right method of revascularization. For type I stenosis at the distal segment of the stent, simple balloon angioplasty could be dangerous because of dissection due to secondary balloon slippage. This type of ISS is challenging for stent placement due to mechanical issues. The use of self-expanding stents in combination with balloon-expandable stents has strong biomechanical implications. A thorough biomechanical analysis of this stent combination is beyond the purpose of this commentary, but we will try to give a preliminary interpretation of the way in which the 2 stents interact. When the balloon-expandable stent is implanted fully within the self-expanding stent, the artery together with the plaque, the ISS tissue, and the 2 stents act like a composite material. The biomechanical behaviour of this composite material can be understood by analysing each of its components. The narrow soft layer of ISS tissue turns into a hyperelastic material that ‘‘sticks’’ the 2 stents together, especially after endothelialization of the second stent.
