Cumulatively, these results strongly suggest that jackals were the principal host species of the rabies cycle, and transmitted the infection to the wild dog pack. The supposition is that the alpha male brought the infection into the pack, since he was the first to die. On October 1, immediately after rabies was diagnosed, the surviving seven dogs were immobilised and moved to a 1 hectare holding boma at Madikwe for observation and vaccination. The dogs were given inactivated rabies vaccine (Rabisin) at a dose of 1 ml given intramuscularly in the biceps femoris, during immobilisation with tiletamine and zolazepam (Zoletil, Virbac) and xylazine (Rompum; Bayer). One yearling died during immobilisation and was diagnosed negative for rabies. Despite vaccination three dogs died of rabies 16 to 21 days after they were placed in the boma. Two of the surviV'ing dogs were released from the boma after 10 months. The last surviving animal was successfully integrated into a captive breeding programme at another site. The three survivors were vaccinated at intervals and their serum tested in cell culture for neutralising antibodies (Cliquet and others 1998). Antibody titres were present soon after the primary vaccination but declined despite booster vaccinations (Table 2). The poor response to vaccination is worth further investigation and may indicate why the adult males and other wild dogs (Burrows 1992, Kat and others 1996) were susceptible to rabies despite having been vaccinated. It has been suggested that intervention may increase the susceptibility of wild dogs to rabies (Burrows and others 1994). In this outbreak, however, vaccination probably saved three of the dogs. Table 1 strongly suggests that the dogs that died following vaccination did not shed virus, while those that died without vaccination had rabies antigen in their salivary glands. Therefore, vaccination may have reduced or eliminated virus shedding from infected animals and may have reduced the risk of infection to other members of the pack. It was felt that euthanasia of the affected dogs was important in preventing excessive contact of healthy dogs with infected ones. Another possibility, that of isolating the affected dogs, was rejected on the grounds that it would cause unnecessary stress that could mask clinical signs and interfere with monitoring. Rapid and effective intervention in an infected pack is important in preventing dissem'ination of the disease to all members of the pack.
