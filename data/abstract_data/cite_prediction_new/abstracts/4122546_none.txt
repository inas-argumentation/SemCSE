Dermatology under siege: public education is the way ahead.
In recent years in Australia we have tried to take a population-based approach to helping people with skin disease. It is a public health model that has been well tried and shown to be effective in the phenomenally successful “Slip! Slop! Slap!” and “SunSmart” programs. 1 As a result of these programs, dermatologists in Australia are very clearly identified by the community as the experts in skin cancer. Continuing in the model of the “Slip! Slop! Slap!” programs, our first step was to undertake epidemiologic studies mapping out the frequency of very common skin conditions in the community. These include acne, warts, tinea, atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis, seborrheic keratoses, cherry angiomas, birth marks, napkin dermatitis, and many others. Like the common cold, they cause considerable concern, morbidity, or both in the community, but frequently are underreported. Certainly, their true prevalence is underestimated unless recorded in high-quality studies. An atlas summarizing the results of these studies—showing the frequency and age specificity of many of these conditions from birth to death, where people seek advice, and whether or not the advice they are getting is of any value—was launched after 5 years of basic epidemiologic work 2 (Fig 1, A). There was extraordinary public interest in the data, as these conditions impact every person in the community in one way or another. Interestingly enough, our research showed that fewer than 50% of people with skin disease in the community seek advice from a medical practitioner. 3 Apart from their doctor, the most common source of advice on cutaneous diseases is from the local pharmacist who is able to dispense over-the-counter products.
