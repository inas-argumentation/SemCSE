Heparin‐induced immune thrombocytopenia – a clinical or laboratory diagnosis?
Despite recent advances, heparin-induced immune thrombocytopenia (HIT) continues to pose an important diagnostic challenge because various mechanisms other than drug sensitivity can cause platelet levels to drop in patients treated with unfractionated (UFH) or low-molecular weight (LMWH) heparin. In this issue of the Journal, G.K. Lo and his colleagues in Hamilton, Ontario, and Greifswald, Germany, describe studies to test the value of an algorithm (the four Ts) for the diagnosis of HIT based on (i) the severity; (ii) the timing of thrombocytopenia; (iii) whether the decline in platelets was accompanied by thrombosis; and (iv) whether another likely cause for thrombocytopenia was present. The prospective study involved evaluation of 336 patients suspected of having HIT in two academic centers [1]. A score ranging from 0 (low likelihood of HIT) to 8 (high likelihood) was assigned to each patient on the basis of the selected algorithm. Scores were compared with the results of laboratory testing for HIT antibodies. The Hamilton group considered patients whose serum gave a value of 50% or greater in the serotonin release assay (SRA) and an OD of at least 1.0 in solid phase ELISA using an IgG-specific secondary probe to have a high likelihood of HIT. The Greifswald group used positivity in each of two in-house tests, the heparin-induced platelet activation test (HIPA) and the solid phase ELISA (OD at least 0.7) as their criterion. Subjects who were studied in each center were a comparable mix of postcardiovascular surgery, internal medicine, and intensive care patients. In Hamilton, only one of 64 patients (1.6%) with a low score (0–3) was seropositive in contrast to eight of 28 (28%) with an intermediate score (4–5) and eight of eight (100%) with a high score (6–8). InGreifswald, the comparable figures were none of 55 (0.0%), 11 of 139 (8%), and nine of 42 (21%). Further analysis of patients who had a low score but tested positive in the solid phase assay revealed an explanation other than HIT as the likely cause of thrombocytopenia in nearly all cases. The authors conclude that the negative predictive value of the HIT score was high in both clinical settings. However, a high score was predictive only in Hamilton. Possible explanations for this discrepancy were that the Hamilton patients were evaluated by an individual with extensive experience in HIT and with the scoring system itself whereas many different physicians did the scoring in Greifswald. Greater use of LMWH, known to cause HIT less often than UFH, in Germany could also have contributed by accounting for a lower frequency of HIT among the German patients. The authors suggest their scoring system could be helpful in the management of HIT in several ways. On the one hand, overdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment of HIT could be reduced by limiting serologic investigation to patients with high or intermediate scores. On the other hand, patients with a high likelihood of HIT could be quickly identified and started on an alternative anticoagulant while serologic studies are pending. Almost everyone agrees that HIT is not purely a laboratory diagnosis, but a clinical one made with the help of laboratory tests. Accordingly, the appropriateness of using laboratory results considered to be diagnostic of HIT to validate a clinical scoring system can be questioned. However, this may have been unavoidable, given the nature of this study. Findings suggest that it may be permissible to withhold laboratory testing (and continue heparin administration) in patients with a low clinical score . More importantly, a high score appears to identify patients in whom there is an urgent need to stop heparin and start treatment with an alternate anticoagulant. The type of study described in this paper is notoriously difficult to perform because of all the human variables involved, and the authors deserve congratulations for having carried this important work through to a successful completion. Of course these findings do not imply that HIT antibody testing can be eliminated by using a clinical score, no matter what its predictive value for HIT.
