Automated IL-6 determinations are already available although in limited use so far. The other important issue is sequential specimen collection. Studies must be conducted if more is to be learned about the kinetics of key cytokines in the course of neonatal septicaemia, especially in the first 24 h after birth in cases of early-onset sepsis. For real-life situations, results of IL-6 and TNFa determinations from specimens collected from infants with suspected sepsis should be available to the clinician regardless of the hour. If this is not the case, even a more slowly rising marker, such as C-reactive protein, which can be determined on serial specimens over the first 24-h period with results made available to the clinician in real time, is better than a marker to be determined on weekdays during office hours. A third consideration is the specificity. Silveira and Procianoy did not find any difference in the IL-6 and TNF-a levels between confirmed and suspected septicaemia, and findings in other studies have been similar in this respect. This suggests that cases which on clinical grounds are suspected as neonatal septicaemia but remain blood culture negative are, in terms of host response, equally severe as those with a positive blood culture. On the other hand, one might sarcastically note that a good clinical eye for observing and diagnosing such cases is as good as the latest and most sophisticated laboratory assays. More seriously, the cytokine determinations, while sensitive enough, do not really seem to add much specificity to the diagnostic work-up. Therefore, while some help from the laboratory in the form of rapid cytokine determinations is likely to become available in the near future, the paediatrician on duty will still have to rely much on his/her clinical skills in the diagnosis and initiation of therapy for suspected arly onset neonatal septicaemia.
