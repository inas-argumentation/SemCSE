Brodifacoum poisoning: A clear and present danger to public health in the USA.
According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, “chemical threat agents can be poisonous vapors, aerosols, liquids or solids that have toxic effects on people. These chemicals can be naturally occurring in the environment or synthetically produced. Chemical releases can be unintentional, as in the case of an industrial accident, or intentional, as in the case of a terrorist attack.” To this end, superwarfarins, such as brodifacoum (BDF), are highly toxic anticoagulant rodenticides that are considered chemical threats to the US population by the Department of Human and Health Services (HHS) because of their potential misuse by terrorists. In this commentary we highlight recent examples of accidental and intentional exposure to this commonly used rodenticide. Introduction Superwarfarins are lipid-avid, very potent, long-lasting warfarin (Coumadin)-derived anticoagulant rodenticides originally developed to eradicate rodents that had developed resistance (Redfern et al. 1976, Rennison & Hadler 1975). Like warfarin, superwarfarins primary mode of action is to inhibit the enzyme vitamin K epoxide reductase, thus reducing the recycling of vitamin K, which is necessary for activation of several clotting factors, which normally leads to coagulation and prevention of excessive bleeding and hemorrhage. Superwarfarins are sold in the USA and around the world under a variety of trade names: Talon, Havoc, d-CON. Amongst the superwarfarins typically incorporated into rodent bait, brodifacoum (BDF) is the most widely used. Poisoning by ingestion, inhalation or absorption through the skin of BDF shows no signs or symptoms in humans other than delayed (after several days) development of severe, long-lasting (up to months) bleeding events. Consequently, victims may not suspect nor report exposure to BDF before overt bleeding is observed. Once manifested, bleeding is prolonged and can be fatal at high doses due to the long biological half-life of BDF which has been estimated to be over 90 days in serum, and almost 10 months in liver (Vandenbroucke et al. 2008), one of its main storage sites and where levels can reach 50-fold or greater than that in other tissues (Hauck et al. 2016). Importantly, BDF poisoning can be fatal if left untreated even at relatively small amounts (~15 mg). BDF poisoning can be accidental or intentional. With the increased use of BDF in rodent baits, the number of accidental poisonings (Greeff et al. 1987), primarily in young children, is over 10,000 per year in the US, having reached a peak incidence of over 17,000 in 2002 (King & Tran 2015). Fortunately, the vast majority of cases were concluded to have been unintentional. Intentional poisoning occurs in a small number of suicide attempts and there are occasional reports of attempted homicide using BDF. As examples, the FBI recently arrested a person suspected of spraying BDF on open food bars in Whole Foods, Meijer and Plum Market in Ann Arbor, Michigan (NY_Times 2016). In New York City, meatloaf served to inmates at the Rikers Island jail complex in 2015 has tested positive for BDF (Lupkin 2015). In 2011, a pipe bomb was found and defused in Spokane, Washington along the route of that year's Martin Luther King Jr. memorial march (FBI 2011). The device contained BDF in addition to the explosives, presumably meant to exacerbate blood loss by inducing anticoagulation in victims. The perpetrator belonging to a racist supremacy group was sentenced to 32 years in prison. The same year, two owners of a store in the Chinatown area of Manhattan, New York were convicted for selling unregistered and misbranded BDF manufactured and imported illegally from China that contained almost 61 times the amount of BDF allowed by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in commercially-available products (EPA 2011). This product was particularly dangerous since its packaging and appearance were deceptive and in one case it was mistaken for cough medicine. Medications and supplements obtained by individuals through unregulated Internet outlets could also be tainted with BDF as illustrated in a recent case of Zolpidem in Murray, Utah (McKinney et al. 2016). In 1981, 177 infants died in Vietnam of internal bleeding attributable to use of commercially-available talc powder (Johnson‟s baby powder) tainted with warfarin (Martin-Bouyer et al. 1984). This product is also sold in the US. Along the same line, a woman residing in San Diego, California experienced a life-threatening bleeding event after purchasing and consuming herbal (hibiscus) tea manufactured in Mexico City, Mexico tainted with warfarin, likely reflecting lower manufacturing or storage standards (LA_Times 1992). Traces of rodenticide, not determined whether to be warfarin or a superwarfarin, were found in lettuce sold in western Germany near Frankfurt (Frankfurt 2013). Sandwiches laced with rodenticide poisoned 25 workers in an auto-factory in Steinfeld, Germany (Medical_Daily 2013). Bags of flour were found at a retirement home in East Sussex, UK (Mirror 2016), and cakes laced with warfarin were found in Scotland (BBC 2015). To this end, cases of „„lacing‟‟ drugs of abuse, such as crack cocaine (Waien et al. 2001) and marijuana (La Rosa et al. 1997) with BDF presumably to achieve a prolonged „high‟ that led to severe, prolonged bleeding have been reported in the US. Alarmingly, threatening letters rigged with razor blades dipped in warfarin and taped inside the opening edge of the envelopes poised to cut the fingers of anyone opening the letters were sent in late 1999 by the London-based Justice Department, a radical group of animal activists, to several researchers at Harvard University, University of California-Davis, Emory University, University of Wisconsin and University of Oregon among others (Washington , LA_times_2 1999). The FBI considered these letters as acts of domestic terrorism in the category of "animal enterprise terrorism." Lastly, in 2004 17 kilograms of flocoumafen, another commercially-available, highly potent rodenticide superwarfarin, were found near water intake valves at a reservoir in Taiwan (Taipei 2014). More disturbing is that BDF is easy to synthesize, very stable and cheap to procure. For instance, chemical distributors in China provide free shipping of 200 L plastic drums containing 5% liquid BDF (DeanPestControl), a 1,000-fold higher concentration than that of the commercial product sold at various outlets in the US. Consequently, BDF can be purchased, concealed, delivered and disseminated in large quantities. Given its high potency and long-lasting debilitating and deadly effects, BDF could be used as a chemical warfare agent (“Agent of Opportunity“) by extremist/terrorist groups. Indeed, both the Rhodesian and South African apartheid governments used BDF to poison and kill freedom fighters in the 1970's by mixing it with food or soaking it into clothing (Brickhill 1992, White 2004), the latter cutaneous route having been reported in other cases of accidental poisoning following use of household baits (Abell et al. 1994). Currently, although there exist means to attenuate the consequences of superwarfrain induced anti-coagulation, there are no FDA-approved drugs to eliminate BDF from tissues. Hence, various blood products and vitamin K1, an FDA-approved drug that increases vitamin K levels allowing activation of coagulation factors and provides anticoagulant effects throughout the body, are provided to patients until BDF no longer interferes with coagulation, a period which can extend for months, after which symptoms may reappear (Card et al. 2014, Underwood et al. 2014). However, since these treatments do not inactivate or eliminate BDF from the body, the long half-life (months) of BDF necessitates extended (months) courses of high-dose (up to 600 mg/day, 120 tablets) vitamin K supplementation which is very expensive (hundreds of US dollars per month) resulting in poor adherence by the patients. This, in turn, leads to recurrence of life-threatening bleeding events that require immediate medical attention and hospitalization. Hence, malicious BDF poisoning in the context of a mass casualty incident represents an ongoing, life-threatening unmet public health need for which safe and efficacious drugs, including prophylactic countermeasures, must be developed. In view of its risk potential to public health, the US government has designated BDF as a Chemical Threat Agent (CDC) and has allocated substantial research funds through NIH's CounterACT program (NIH_CounterAct) to discover, develop and market safe and efficacious countermeasures to prevent and treat its lethal effects due to accidental exposure, industrial accidents, natural disasters, or terrorist attacks. These countermeasures are anticipated to be readily deployed to and administered by first responders and other healthcare providers. This trans-NIH collaborative drug development program is prosecuted in collaboration with other federal agencies, such as HHS' Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority (BARDA) and FDA's Medical Countermeasures Initiative (MCMi). In summary, a mass casualty incident due to superwarfarin poisoning represents a clear and present danger to the US population for which new, safe and efficacious drugs must be developed.
