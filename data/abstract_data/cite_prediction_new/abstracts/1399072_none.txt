Study of oxidative phagocyte metabolism using the fluorescent indicator hydroethidine
Phagocytic cells play the important role in protection of the organism against foreign entities due to their antimicrobial, antiparasitic, and antitumor properties [1 4]. The capability of phagocytes to perform a number of physiologically important functions is connected with their ability to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), namely, the superoxide radical anion, hydrogen peroxide, the hydroxyl radical, and singlet oxygen [5, 6]. Formation of the superoxide radical anion, the initial metabolite, results from one-electron reduction of oxygen with NADPH oxidase bound to the plasmalemma [7, 8]. It can dismutate either spontaneously or via intracellular superoxide dismutase (SOD) to hydrogen peroxide, which in turn transforms to hydroxyl radicals in the Fenton reaction and ito hypochlorous acid and hypochloramines in the presence ofneutrophil myeloperoxidase [9]. At the same time, excess production of these metabolites results in damage to nuclear structures [1, 10] and enzyme inactivation [1, 7, 11], and can be responsible for damage to their own membranes and cells [6, 12, 13]. At present most studies on the oxidative metabolism of phagocytes concerns extracellular ROS generation. However, it is known that some of the radicals are formed intracellularly probably not only due to NADPH oxidase but also due to the mitochondrial respiratory chain and the endoplasmic reticulum as well [5, 14, 15]. Unfortunately, the intracellularly generated products of incomplete reduction of oxygen are not determined by traditional methods (spectrophotometrically by reduction of cytochrome c or by chemiluminescence) as a rule. The use of fluorescent probes holds much promise in this regard. The probes are able to penetrate into phagocytes and change their luminescence properties after oxidation of their ROS [14, 16, 17].
