Genotyping for CYP2D6 in Patients with Infantile Hemangiomas Refractory to Topical Timolol
To the Editor: We read the study by Weibel et al (1) and found it interesting that an efficacious drug regimen of topical timolol gel 0.5% twice daily did not result in a benefit in 12.5% of patients, leading to hemangiomas that remained static or even continued to grow. Because the present study found that topically applied timolol is systemically absorbed, perhaps the patients that did not respond metabolize timolol at a faster rate. Studies of recombinant human CYP enzymes have confirmed that timolol is mainly metabolized by CYP2D6, an important member of the cytochrome P450 family (2). The specific genotype of this polymorphic enzyme determines the pharmacokinetics of timolol (2). Differences in CYP2D6 enzyme activity lead to four well-characterized phenotypes: poor, intermediate, extensive, and ultrarapid metabolizers (3). Patients may have a subtherapeutic plasma level of timolol if they have the ultrarapid metabolizer phenotype for CYP2D6. In the present study, it is possible that patients with static hemangiomas or hemangiomas that continued to grow after topical timolol treatment were ultrarapid metabolizers of CYP2D6. Likewise, although timolol has a good safety profile, the few patients who experience adverse effects may have the poor metabolizer phenotype and metabolize timolol slowly. These patients would have a supratherapeutic plasma level of timolol with normal doses of timolol and be at greater risk of adverse effects, such as bradycardia, hypotension, and bronchospasm (3). Because different pharmacogenetic variants of CYP2D6 may lead to poor clinical response or increase the possibility of adverse effects, we suggest exploring the possibility of genotyping for CYP2D6 when using timolol to treat hemangiomas and adjusting dosages accordingly.
