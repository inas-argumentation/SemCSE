Immunology and the Evaluation of Risk Factors for Development of Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis
I would like to congratulate Dr. Campbell for his work that found the lack of an effect of the use of proton pump inhibiters on the incidence of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) [1]. Their work certainly increases our understanding of this phenomenon and eases some concerns; however, it is not clear that there may be some underlying differences in their study from the previous work of Dr. Bauer that had found a slightly increased incidence [2]. Nearly 30 years ago my group had an interest in the immunologic mechanisms of infection responsible for spontaneous bacterial peritonitis [3] and, several years later, also in the mechanisms of peritonitis associated with peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) [4, 5]. While we found the protein content of fluid in the peritoneal cavity to be an important factor in both instances, a low ascitic fluid protein was found to be the most important risk factor for the development of SBP (P = 0.001) (Table 1, Fig. 1). Although we found significant correlations (Table 1) between ascitic fluid protein and complement (P \ 0.0001) and immunoglobulins (P = 0.017, P \ 0.0001), we were never able to precisely elucidate the mechanisms since albumin is also a polyanionic colloid with a counterion cloud [6] that may restrict movement essential cations like iron that are necessary for bacterial surface adhesion [7]. Nevertheless, since E. coli was the predominant organism of SBP in alcoholic liver disease and gram-negative bacterial cell wall endotoxin can directly activate the complement cascade, we felt that low complement activity was the more likely defect, while the deficient opsonic activity due to loss of immunoglobulins in nephrotic ascites might be more important since SBP classically resulted from the encapsulated pneumococcus in that disease. That reasoning should be subjected to review since recently investigators have shown that bacterial DNA, independent of endotoxin, can activate the complement cascade in ascites [8]. Whatever the mechanism, however, low ascitic fluid protein did appear to be closely linked to the ‘‘impaired immune response’’ to which Dr. Campbell alludes as responsible for the bacterial growth in ascites after transmigration from the colon. Several years after our original findings on the protective value of protein in ascitic fluid, other groupsnoted similar findings [9–11]. Even today some consider patients with low ascitic fluid protein levels to be of such a risk for SBP that prophylactic antibiotics are recommended [12]. Yet despite the importance of the ascitic level protein as a risk factor for SBP, I find no mention of its inclusion in any of their analyses of confounding factors. Indeed failure to account for such a confounding variable may be the difference between the present study and the previous work of Dr. Bauer since the patients who developed SBP in his study had statistically significantly lower ascitic fluid protein (P = 0.005) [2]. MELD scores, while useful for transplantation, do not include any estimate of ascitic fluid protein and may not be as useful as a risk marker for SBP. Since Dr. Campbell has noted that all his patients underwent paracenteses, I would think that a post hoc re-analysis of his data with the inclusion of ascitic fluid protein levels would be both simple and informative.
