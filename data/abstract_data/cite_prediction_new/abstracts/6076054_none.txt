The relationship of nitric oxide (NO) to vascular disease has a long and storied history. Even before the identification of the endothelium-derived relaxing factor as NO, abnormalities in endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation were recognized in human atherosclerotic coronary arteries.2 Soon thereafter, Bossaller and colleagues3 showed that this impairment in endothelium-dependent relaxation in response to muscarinic agonists is associated with attenuated cyclic guanosine 5′-monophosphate (cGMP) accumulation, consistent with a key signaling mechanism for NO. Vita and colleagues4 subsequently demonstrated that impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation of angiographically smooth coronary arteries correlates with risk factor burden, ushering in the notion that endothelial dysfunction and associated NO insufficiency is a precursor to overt coronary atherosclerosis.5 From the thrombosis perspective, platelet-derived NO was shown to be critical in modulating hemostatic potential,6 largely by impairing recruitment of platelets to the growing platelet thrombus.7 In keeping with this key biological role, a deficiency of platelet-derived NO was shown to predict the presence of an acute coronary syndrome8 and to contribute to the overall propensity to arterial thrombosis.9 Taken together, these observations show that altered NO bioavailability and signaling functionally link NO to the pathogenesis of atherothrombosis. Furthermore, early evidence suggested that adequate bioavailable NO can promote the regression of established atherosclerosis, in part, by improving endothelial function,10 and by inducing monocyte/macrophage apoptosis, as well.11

Functional polymorphisms in the endothelial NO synthase gene ( NOS3 ) that attenuate NO production are experiments of nature that confirm the adverse consequences of inadequate generation of NO by the endothelium.
